## 1. Background

### Dates of the oracles
- **Elam (49:34)** is the only dated oracle in the chapter: "in the beginning of the reign of Zedekiah king of Judah". Zedekiah was installed after Nebuchadnezzar took Jerusalem on 2 Adar (Feb/March) 597 BC (Babylonian Chronicle ABC 5, Rev. 12'-13', file `bg_livius_abc5.txt`, https://www.livius.org/sources/content/mesopotamian-chronicles-content/abc-5-jerusalem-chronicle/). That makes the Elam oracle c. 597 BC.
- **The other four oracles are undated.** Ellicott puts the Ammon oracle "in or about the fourth year of Jehoiakim (Jeremiah 25:21)", i.e. c. 605 BC (`bh_49-1.txt`). Matthew Henry thinks "it is probable that the other prophecies against the Gentiles, going before, were at the same time" as Elam (`henry_jer49.txt`). Keil & Delitzsch say Elam's title means "it was published later than the prophecies in Jeremiah 48 and in 49:1-33" (`bh_49-34.txt`). Calvin thinks the Damascus oracle was given "while the kingdom was yet standing, or at least before its final overthrow" (`calvin_jer49.txt`).
- **The "cup" framework.** Jeremiah 25:15-29 is the master list. Jeremiah takes the cup of wrath to Jerusalem first, then the nations, including "Edom, and Moab, and the children of Ammon" (25:21), "Dedan, and Tema, and Buz" (25:23), "all the kings of Arabia" (25:24), and "all the kings of Elam" (25:25). Verse 29: "For, lo, I begin to bring evil on the city which is called by my name, and should ye be utterly unpunished? Ye shall not be unpunished". Jeremiah 49:12 deliberately echoes 25:29 (KJV text in `kjv_jer25.txt`, https://biblehub.com/kjv/jeremiah/25.htm). Keil & Delitzsch on 49:12: "The idea is founded on that pervading Jeremiah 25, and there is use made of the words in Jeremiah 25:29." Matthew Henry opens his chapter with "The cup of trembling still goes round, and the nations must all drink of it, according to the instructions given to Jeremiah, ch. 25:15."
- **Restoration pattern:** Moab (48:47), Ammon (49:6) and Elam (49:39) receive "I will bring again the captivity" promises. Edom, Damascus and Kedar/Hazor get none. Edom's sentence is the opposite: "perpetual wastes" (49:13) and "no man shall abide there" (49:18).

### Ammon (49:1-6)
- **Rabbah = modern Amman.** Trapp: "Megalopolis, the metropolis of the Ammonites; it was afterwards called Philadelphia, from Ptolemy Philadelph, who re-edified it." (`trapp_jer49.txt`). The Wikipedia "Rabbah" URL redirects to the Amman article, which has sections "Rabbath Amman" and "Philadelphia" (`bg_amman_wikipedia.txt`, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rabbah). The Armstrong Institute notes the capital "Amman (which itself derives its name from Ammon)" (`bg_baalis_armstrong.txt`).
- **Milcom / "their king" (malkam), 49:1, 3.** This is a deliberate double meaning. Keil & Delitzsch: Jeremiah "evidently uses מלכּם in a double sense ... meaning the king and god of the Ammonites" (`bh_49-1.txt`). Calvin, in his discussion of 49:1, takes it as the king and criticizes Jerome for reading the idol's name (`calvin_jer49.txt`). Barnes: "The Ammonite god stands for the Ammonites just as Chemosh Jeremiah 48:7 is the equivalent of the Moabites."
- **Ammon seizing Gad.** After Tiglath-pileser III deported the Transjordan tribes (2 Kings 15:29; 1 Chronicles 5:26, which names "the Gadites"), Ammon moved into Gilead. Matthew Henry ties it to "the carrying away of the Gileadites, by the king of Assyria (2 Ki. 15:29, 1 Chr. 5:26)". Ellicott: "On the deportation of the Trans-jordanic tribes by Tiglath-pileser ... they made a more successful effort". The usual date for that campaign is 733-732 BC. That date is standard but does not appear in my saved files, so check it before stating it on air. Amos 1:13 gives Ammon's earlier atrocity: "they have ripped up the women with child of Gilead, that they might enlarge their border".
- **Baalis and the seal.** Jeremiah 40:14: "Baalis the king of the Ammonites hath sent Ishmael the son of Nethaniah to slay thee". He was behind the assassination of Gedaliah. Wikipedia (`bg_baalis_wikipedia.txt`, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baalis): "In 1984 an Ammonite seal, dated to c. 600 BCE, was excavated in Tell El-`Umeiri, Jordan that reads "belonging to Milkomor, the servant of Baalisha". Identification of 'Baalisha' with the biblical Baalis is likely,[1] but it is currently unknown if there was only one Ammonite king of that name." The Armstrong Institute (`bg_baalis_armstrong.txt`, https://armstronginstitute.org/385-king-baalis-first-evidence-of-a-biblical-ammonite-ruler) says it was found by the Andrews University excavation and reads "Belonging to Milcom-Or, Servant of Baalisa". It also mentions a second seal, "Baalis[a], King of B[ne Ammo]n", published by Robert Deutsch in 1999 "from a private collection". Caution: that second seal is unprovenanced, so present it more tentatively than the excavated Umeiri impression.
- **Later Ammon.** 1 Maccabees 5:6 still finds "a mighty power, and much people" in Ammon. Gill cites it, and Justin Martyr says many Ammonites still existed in his day. Origen already folds them into "Arabians" (Gill and K&D in `bh_49-6.txt`).

### Edom (49:7-22)
- **Places.** Teman (49:7, 20) was a district or city of Edom whose name means "south", and the region was famous for wisdom (Eliphaz the Temanite, Job 2:11; Ellicott, Poole and Gill in `bh_49-7.txt`). Hebrew *ḥokmâ* (Strong's 2451) appears twice in 49:7 ("wisdom"; "their wisdom"), and "Has vanished" translates *nisrəḥâ*, Strong's 5628, Niphal (`il_49-7.txt`, https://biblehub.com/interlinear/jeremiah/49-7.htm). Calvin spends a paragraph on that verb ("Rotten has become their wisdom ... superfluous"). **Bozrah** (49:13, 22) was the capital. K&D: "Bozrah was at that time the capital of the Edomites ... on the site of the village Buseireh". **Sela/"clefts of the rock"** (49:16): Ellicott suggests "Better, perhaps, in the fortresses of Sela" and warns that the visible remains of Petra "are, it must be remembered, of Roman origin" (`bh_49-16.txt`). Biblical Archaeology Society, "The Edomite Stronghold of Sela" (`bg_bas_sela.txt`, https://www.biblicalarchaeology.org/daily/biblical-sites-places/biblical-archaeology-sites/the-edomite-stronghold-of-sela/): es-Sela lies "just 3 miles north of the Edomite capital of Bozrah (modern Buseirah)", and "other candidates for Biblical Sela have been proposed, namely the steep-sided mountain of Umm el-Biyara in nearby Petra." **Dedan** (49:8) was an Arabian tribe living with Edom (Spurgeon: "some Arabian tribe or tribes ... probably dwelt in the rock city of Petra, and were mingled with the Edomites").
- **Obadiah parallels.** Jeremiah 49:9 matches Obadiah 5, 49:10 matches Obad 6, 49:14-16 matches Obad 1-4, and 49:7 echoes Obad 8-9. Obadiah 4: "Though thou exalt thyself as the eagle, and though thou set thy nest among the stars, thence will I bring thee down" (`kjv_obadiah1.txt`, https://biblehub.com/kjv/obadiah/1.htm). Who borrowed from whom is disputed. Calvin: "as Obadiah had preceded Jeremiah". Barnes leans the other way, noting that "Obadiah apparently after it" wrote. Trapp: "whence Jeremiah took this, and more besides, or else Obadiah from him." K&D treat Jeremiah as reworking Obadiah, changing "we have heard" to "I have heard" (`bh_49-16.txt`).
- **Edom in 586.** Psalm 137:7: "Remember, O LORD, the children of Edom in the day of Jerusalem; who said, Rase it, rase it, even to the foundation thereof." Obadiah 11: "even thou wast as one of them." See also Ezek 25:12-14; 35; Lam 4:21. The Ligonier devotional "God Charges Edom" (`ligonier_god_charges_edom.txt`, https://learn.ligonier.org/devotionals/god-charges-edom) traces the progression in Obad 10-14 and notes "some later Jewish traditions indicate that Edom helped the Babylonians burn the temple". Pulpit Commentary observes that Jeremiah himself makes "no allusion ... to any special bitter feeling of the Edomites towards the Israelites" (`bh_49-7.txt`). The oracle is probably earlier than 586, and Edom's notorious conduct in 586 confirmed it after the fact.
- **Nabonidus.** The Nabonidus Chronicle (ABC 7), third year "553/552 BCE", reads: "they/he encamped [against E]dom" (`bg_livius_abc7.txt`, https://www.livius.org/sources/content/mesopotamian-chronicles-content/abc-7-nabonidus-chronicle/). The word "Edom" is partly restored. Livius summarizes: "In year three he conquered the kingdom Edom". The BAS article describes a cliff relief at es-Sela of "a royal figure with a conical cap ... facing symbols of the sun, moon and stars", which "strongly suggests it was carved to commemorate the conquest of es-Sela and Edom during the southern campaign of the Babylonian king Nabonidus (555–539 B.C.E.)". **Date caution:** the chronicle gives 553/552 (the campaign ran in Kislimu, Dec 553). Some scholars, e.g. B. Crowell, argue for 551 from the relief inscription. I saw that only in a search summary and could not fetch it, so say "about 552 BC".
- **Afterwards.** The Pulpit Commentary names the Nabataeans as Edom's successors and says John Hyrcanus "compelled them to accept circumcision about B.C. 130" (`bh_49-7.txt`). Herod was an Idumean (Gill on 49:7). Rogland/Ligonier: "people from Idumea were among those drawn to follow Jesus the Messiah (Mark 3:8–9)".

### Damascus (49:23-27)
- Damascus stands for the Aramean states. **Hamath** (Hama on the Orontes) and **Arpad** (Tell Erfad, north of Aleppo) are often paired (Isa 10:9; 2 Kings 18:34; Ellicott, Barnes, Cambridge, Pulpit in `bh_49-23.txt`). "palaces of Benhadad" (49:27) echoes Amos 1:4. Henry: "Some think Ben-hadad ... was a common name of the kings of Syria, as Pharaoh of the kings of Egypt."
- Damascus had already fallen to Assyria (2 Kings 16:9). Several commentators say there is no clear record of a Babylonian sack. Pulpit: "we have as yet no monumental evidence as to the facts". Guzik quotes Feinberg: "Expositors have difficulty fitting this prophecy into any recorded event related to Damascus." JFB, citing Josephus, places it "about five years after the destruction of Jerusalem".
- **"there is sorrow on the sea; it cannot be quiet" (49:23).** The Hebrew verb is *haš·qêṭ* (interlinear gloss "to be calmed"), where *šāqaṭ* (Strong's 8252) is a Hiphil infinitive absolute, "to be calmed" (`il_49-23.txt`). The same verb is the keyword of the two previous oracles. In 47:6 the sword is told to rest, *ṯiš·qō·ṭî* ("will you be quiet"), and 47:7 asks "How can it be quiet" with the same word, *tiš·qō·ṭî* (`il_47-6.txt`, `il_47-7.txt`). In 48:11 Moab *wə·šō·qêṭ*, "and has been resting", on his lees (`il_48-11.txt`). So Philistia's sword cannot be quiet, Moab was too quiet, and Damascus cannot be quieted. The "sea" is a crux. Many manuscripts read *kayyām*, "like the sea", instead of *bayyām*, "in the sea" (Calvin's editor, Ellicott, Pulpit, JFB, Cambridge). Isaiah 57:20 ("the wicked are like the troubled sea, when it cannot rest", `kjv_isaiah_57-20.txt`) is usually cited.

### Kedar and Hazor (49:28-33)
- Kedar descends from Ishmael (Gen 25:13). These are Bedouin tent-dwellers (Ps 120:5; Isa 21:16-17). "Hazor" here is not the Galilean city. It probably comes from *ḥāṣēr*, an unwalled settlement, so it means semi-settled Arabs, and its "kingdoms" are sheikhdoms (Barnes, Pulpit, K&D in `bh_49-28.txt`). "the men of the east" (*bənê-qedem*) recalls Judges 6:3.
- **Babylonian Chronicle (ABC 5, Rev. 9'-10'):** "In the sixth year [599/598] in the month of Kislîmu" ... "the king of Akkad mustered his army and marched to the Hatti-land. From the Hatti-land he sent out his companies," / "and scouring the desert they took much plunder from the Arabs," ... "their possessions, animals and gods." (`bg_livius_abc5.txt`; Livius's inline editorial notes are omitted at the ellipses, and the note on "Arabs" says "Probably nomads in what is now Jordan.") This is a strong match for 49:28-32 ("Nebuchadrezzar king of Babylon shall smite"; "their camels shall be a booty").

### Elam (49:34-39)
- Elam lay east of Babylonia in what is now SW Iran, with its capital at Susa (Shushan, Dan 8:2; Esther 1:2). Its people descend from Elam son of Shem (Gen 10:22). Isaiah 22:6: "And Elam bare the quiver with chariots of men and horsemen". That is the "bow of Elam, the chief of their might" (49:35). Calvin and Trapp both stress the Elamites' fame as archers.
- **Babylonian Chronicle (ABC 5, Rev. 16'-20'):** "In the ninth year [596/595] ... the king of Elam [...] ... the king of Elam was afraid and, panic falling on him, he returned to his own land." (`bg_livius_abc5.txt`). So within a year or two of the oracle (597), Elam and Babylon were in open hostility. Guzik quotes J. A. Thompson on the same text. K&D, citing Hävernick, note that the oracle never names Nebuchadnezzar.
- **Acts 2:9:** "Parthians, and Medes, and Elamites". Calvin, Henry, Gill, Poole, Trapp, Barnes, Ellicott, JFB and Guzik all cite this as at least a firstfruits fulfillment of 49:39. The Pulpit Commentary pushes back (quoted below).

### Hebrew notes
- **šûb šəbût ("bring again the captivity" / "restore the fortunes")**: 49:6 *ʾāšîḇ ʾeṯ-šəḇûṯ* (Strong's 7725 + 7622, `il_49-6.txt`). In 49:39 the Bible Hub interlinear prints the Hebrew forms אָשׁוּב and שְׁבִית, while its transliteration reads ’ā·šîḇ ... šə·ḇūṯ (`il_49-39.txt`). That looks like a Kethib/Qere spelling variant, though I have not checked it in BHS. The same formula is used for Moab in 48:47 ("Yet will I bring again the captivity of Moab in the latter days") and for Judah in 29:14, 30:3 and 33:7. The phrase means a reversal of fortunes, broader than a literal return from exile, which is why Calvin, Henry, Gill, Poole and Trapp can apply it to the Gentiles' calling in Christ.
- **mišpāṭ (49:12)**: KJV "they whose judgment was not to drink of the cup", where "judgment" is *mišpāṭām*, Strong's 4941 (`il_49-12.txt`). Calvin takes *judgment* here as "God's decree, by which he apportions to each his own lot". The infinitive absolute (*šāṯōw* ... *yištū*; Calvin's rendering is "Drinking they shall drink") is emphatic. K&D: "The inf. abs. שׁתו for שׁתה intensifies".
- **šāqaṭ (49:23)**: see above.
- **tipleṣet (49:16)**, "thy terribleness", occurs only here (Pulpit calls it a "ἅπαξ λεγόμενον"). One view takes it as a slur on an idol, following *mipleṣet* in 1 Kings 15:13 (Ellicott, Gill "thine idol", Trapp, Thompson via Guzik). Calvin, Poole and Pulpit take it as the terror Edom inspired in others.

## 3. The hardest interpretive questions

### Q1. Why is there no restoration promise for Edom, when Moab (48:47), Ammon (49:6) and Elam (49:39) get one?
- **Calvin:** The difference is deliberate and moral. Edom was kin ("it had a nearer connection with the Israelites -- hence its cruelty was less to be borne"). It had long abused God's patience, from its refusal of passage in Numbers 20 onward. "When he prophesied before against the Moabites and the Ammonites, he mingled some consolation, but as to Edom, every hope is cut off." He allows that "God might have chosen some from that nation", but says this "favor remained hid". He also notes that some Edomites survived, "but this was not inconsistent with this prophecy, because they who remained alive became so scattered, that they never formed one people, nor had any name" (on 49:18).
- **Trapp:** God's oath in 49:13 shows "how exceedingly God was incensed against the Edomites, to whom therefore also no comfort is spoken, as is to Ammon and Moab".
- **Morgan (via Guzik):** The silence shows "the awful possibility of resisting not only the mercy of God, but His judgments also, so completely that there is no possibility of restoration." Guzik also quotes Feinberg: "There is no prophecy of future restoration for Edom."
- **Augustine and Rogland (the evangelical counterweight):** No national restoration, yet grace still reaches Edomites as individuals. Augustine reads "Mount Esau" in Obadiah 21 as "the Church of the Gentiles". Rogland: Idumeans were "among those drawn to follow Jesus the Messiah (Mark 3:8–9)".
- **Gill (on 49:11)** reports others who see "a remnant of the Edomites that should be preserved, and be converted to Christ in Gospel times."
- **Aphrahat** simply misremembers Edom as included. That is worth noting only as a caution.
- **Suggested synthesis for the episode:** The nation as a nation gets no future. Edom becomes the type of the unrepentant brother, compare Malachi 1:2-4 and Romans 9:13. Even so, 49:11 leaves the orphans and widows a door into the God of Israel, and the gospel later found Idumeans. Nobody is shut out because of their bloodline. Settled pride is what has no promise.

### Q2. Leave your fatherless children... let your widows trust in me (49:11): mercy or taunt?
- **Taunt or irony.** Calvin: God "taunts the Idumeans", and the irony fits because they "spared neither sex nor age". Gill (on 49:12) says verse 12 shows the preceding verse "is not said by way of promise and comfort, but threatening". Poole leans this way: "rather a threatening (whether by way of irony or no) than a promise."
- **"No one will be left to say it."** This is the first view Calvin reports, and Henry adopts it for Edom: "nor is there any to say, Leave me thy orphans". The Geneva Bible note: "there will be none left to take care of the widows and the fatherless" (`bh_49-11.txt`). Henry then applies the words to believers: "it is an unspeakable comfort to the people of God, when they are dying, that they may leave their surviving relations with God".
- **Genuine mercy to Edom's helpless.** Calvin's own editor in footnote 38 (citing Venema) takes this view, as do Keil & Delitzsch ("Yet the Lord will care for them, so that they shall not perish"), Ellicott ("The latter view seems truer to the prophet’s character", citing 48:36), Barnes, Cambridge ("decidedly Jeremianic"), Pulpit ("A merciful mitigation of the prophet's stern threat"; "a promise of the revival of the Edomitish people"), JFB (threat with an implied promise) and Benson (quoting Houbigant).
- **Both readings, depending on the hearer.** Trapp: "to the profane Edomites in derision, but to all true Israelites in serious sadness". The Targum redirects the words to Israel (reported by Gill, Henry and Benson).
- **Devotional tradition.** Winslow and J. A. James use the verse as God's direct word to widows. Spurgeon does not seem to have preached on it.
- **Suggested handling:** The Hebrew is imperatives plus a jussive ("let them trust"). Read plainly, it is an invitation. The context is total ruin of the adult males, which explains why many read it as grim. The strongest pastoral line is this: even inside Edom's death sentence, God names himself as the One widows can trust, the same God as Psalm 68:5. Mention Calvin's irony reading honestly as the major dissent.

### Q3. Those who did not deserve to drink the cup must drink (49:12): who are they, and is God unjust to them?
- **Most say Israel or Judah, "comparatively" undeserving.** Calvin: the measure is God's adopting grace, not their merit, because the Israelites "had been worse than all others". The answer is "not to the persons of men, but ... to the grace of God". He links it to 1 Peter 4:17-18 and Luke 23:31 ("if the green wood is burnt, what will become of the dry?"). For the church the cup is medicine, and for the wicked it is poison.
- **Poole:** "It must be taken in the most favourable sense imaginable". Either Israel in respect of its relation to God, or Israel in comparison with others. "When an Israelite hath not escaped the justice of God, an Edomite must not expect it."
- **Gill** allows either other nations less guilty than Edom or the Jews comparatively, and insists "this is not to be understood strictly of proper justice, but in a comparative sense".
- **Henry** holds both together: nations that were less hostile, "or Israel itself", among whom "there were many, very many, who kept his ordinances".
- **K&D:** Jeremiah does not mean "that nay injustice befalls the Jews" [sic, in source]. Election simply gives no "right to exemption".
- **Pulpit:** "Jehovah condescends to speak from a human point of view", comparing Isa 28:21, the "strange work". Barnes translates: "they whose rule was not to drink".
- **Cross-reference:** Jeremiah 25:29, "I begin to bring evil on the city which is called by my name, and should ye be utterly unpunished?" Christian application: Christ drank the cup in full (Ligonier's "Edom's Fall" devotional: "God sent His Son to endure the day of the Lord in our behalf").

### Q4 (bonus). Is the 49:39 restoration of Elam fulfilled at Pentecost (Acts 2:9)?
- **Yes, as firstfruits:** Calvin, Henry, Gill, Poole, Trapp (who adds a Persian bishop at Nicaea, citing Eusebius), JFB, Barnes, Ellicott ("a partial fulfilment") and Kidner via Guzik ("the curtain lifts an inch or two").
- **Caution:** Pulpit calls Pentecost alone "a meagre fulfilment" and treats Elam as a representative distant Gentile people. Henry and Benson also point to Cyrus, with Susa becoming a Persian capital.

## 1. Background and structure

**Shape of the chapter.**
- 3:1-5 is a night search: on her bed, then in the city, past the watchmen, then she finds him, holds him, and brings him "into my mother's house". It closes with the adjuration refrain from 2:7. It parallels 5:2-8, where the search goes badly (in 5:7 the watchmen beat her).
- 3:6-11 changes scene to a royal procession "out of the wilderness". It names "Solomon" three times (vv. 7, 9, 11) and ends at the wedding day.
- 3:6's question, "Who is this that cometh out of the wilderness", comes back in 8:5.

Song 8:5 (KJV), File: `text-song-8-5.txt` (biblehub.com/songs/8-5.htm)
> Who is this that cometh up from the wilderness, leaning upon her beloved?

Song 5:6 (KJV), the parallel failed search, File: `text-song-5-6.txt` (biblehub.com/songs/5-6.htm)
> I sought him, but I could not find him; I called him, but he gave me no answer.

**Dream or real?** The critical commentators lean toward a dream.

Cambridge Bible (A. Harper), on 3:1-5. File: `cambridge.txt` / `bh3-1.txt` (https://biblehub.com/commentaries/cambridge/songs/3.htm)
> Almost all commentators agree that we have here a dream narrated to some persons, in which the Shulammite seems to herself to have sought her lover in the city and failed to find him.

Cambridge Bible, on "By night" (Hebrew plural *lêlôth*, "nights"). File: `cambridge.txt`
> It would therefore seem that she means to say, that one night after another she dreamt that she missed and sought her lover.

Pulpit Commentary, on 3:4. File: `bh3-4.txt` (https://biblehub.com/commentaries/songs/3-4.htm)
> But the hypothesis of a dream is the most natural explanation.

**The litter or palanquin ("appiryon", 3:9).** The word occurs only here and has no clear Semitic root. LXX renders it *phoreion* and the Vulgate *ferculum*. BDB lists the possible origins as Sanskrit *paryanka* or Greek *phoreion*. Strong's guesses Egyptian.

Strong's / BDB, File: `strongs-668-appiryon.txt` (https://biblehub.com/hebrew/668.htm)
> origin dubious; no plausible Shemitic etymology; perhaps (so RS in YuleGlossary of Anglo-Indian words, 502) Sanskrit paryanka, litter-bed, 'palankeen;' perhaps (if poem be late) Greek φορεῖον

Ellicott, on 3:9. File: `bh3-9.txt` (https://biblehub.com/commentaries/songs/3-9.htm)
> Its derivation has been sought in Hebrew, Persian, Greek, and Sanskrit.

Note that 3:7 uses a different word, *mittah* ("bed/couch"). Durham notes that the two words differ (see below).

**The mother's crown (3:11).**
- *Bathsheba.* Barnes, File: `bh3-11.txt` (https://biblehub.com/commentaries/songs/3-11.htm)
> His mother - Bathsheba 1 Kings 1:11. This is the last mention of her in sacred history.
- *Poole* gives both readings, a royal crown or a wedding garland. File: `bh3-11.txt`
> The crown royal, wherewith his mother, Bathsheba, is said to have crowned him, because Solomon was crowned by David’s order upon her suggestion, and by virtue of his promise confirmed by an oath to her, 1 Kings 1:16, &c.
- *Cambridge*, on Jewish wedding crowns. File: `bh3-11.txt`
> She may quite well have put a wedding crown on his head, for it is the custom at Jewish weddings now that the bridegroom should be crowned.

**Mishnah Sotah 9:14 (bridegroom crowns abolished).** File: `mishnah-sotah-9-14-sefaria.txt`, from https://www.sefaria.org/api/texts/Mishnah_Sotah.9.14 (William Davidson Edition, CC-BY-NC). The same mishnah also uses *appiryon* for a bride's palanquin, which is a useful link to 3:9.
> In the war [pulemus] of Vespasian the Sages decreed upon the crowns of bridegrooms, i.e., that bridegrooms may no longer wear crowns, and upon the drums, meaning they also banned the playing of drums.

> In the last war, meaning the bar Kokheva revolt, they decreed that a bride may not go out in a palanquin inside the city

Gill and Keil-Delitzsch both cite this mishnah on 3:11.
- Keil & Delitzsch, File: `bh3-11.txt`
> It is seen from the Sota (ix. 14) that the old custom for the bridegroom to wear a "crown" was abolished in consequence of the awful war with Vespasian.
- Gill, File: `bh3-11.txt` (his footnote "(g)" reads "Misnah Sotah, c. 9. s. 14.")
> alluding to a custom with the Jews (g) and other nations (h), to put nuptial crowns on the heads Of married persons, both men and women, on the marriage day

**The refrain (3:5 = 2:7; cf. 8:4).**
- Durham says the refrain repeats 2:7 but now concerns Christ's presence in the church, "her mother's house", rather than her private enjoyment of him (quote in §3).
- Hudson Taylor reads 3:5 as the Bridegroom's charge, "until *she* please" (§3).

**"Paved with love" (3:10).** The Hebrew reads literally "its interior inlaid, love, from the daughters of Jerusalem". The preposition is *min* ("from"), not "for".

Cambridge, File: `bh3-10.txt` (https://biblehub.com/commentaries/songs/3-10.htm)
> The A.V. can hardly be right in rendering ‘love for the daughters of Jerusalem,’ for the preposition is min which cannot mean for. The R.V. has, more correctly, from the daughters of Jerusalem.

## 2. The hardest interpretive questions, and how the voices answer

### Q1. Dream or real? Why does she fail at first?

- **Modern and critical readers mostly say a dream.** Cambridge, Pulpit, and Carson ("I am inclined to think it is") all take it so.
- **The Puritans do not ask the question.** For Durham, Henry, Gill, Poole, and Trapp, the "night" is spiritual desertion. The "bed" is carnal ease or laziness: Durham, Gill, and Henry ("late and lazy seeking"). She fails because she sought him in a sluggish way, or because Christ withholds himself to train her.
- **Bernard** says the delay is "a concealment of love".
- **Gregory of Nyssa** turns the failure into a doctrine. God cannot be comprehended, so every arrival is a new beginning, and she finds him only "by faith".
- **Taylor** blames her worldliness. In 2:17 she put him off, so the night of 3:1 is the result.

### Q2. Why does she seize him and bring him to her mother's house?

- **Literal readings.**
  - Cambridge: it makes him "her acknowledged lover".
  - Carson: it signals "official consummation".
  - Poole, on the literal level: to "gain my mother's consent, and so proceed to the consummation of the marriage."
- **The visible church and its ordinances.** Durham, Gill, Henry, and Spurgeon (Sermon 1035, "we desire to bring Christ into the church") read the mother's house as the church. Christ is sought in private and then brought into public worship for the good of the other members.
- **The Jerusalem above (Gal. 4:26).** Poole and Hudson Taylor.
- **The Synagogue.** Bernard (Sermon 79) reads the mother's house as the Synagogue. The Church generously wants to bring Christ back to Israel "until the fulness of the Gentiles become in".
- **The heart restored to its original state.** Gregory of Nyssa: the chamber is the heart, and the "mother" is the first cause of our being.
- **Holding him.** Durham, Henry, and Spurgeon all tie "I held him" to Jacob at Jabbok (Gen. 32:26). Durham and Spurgeon both stress that Christ allows himself to be held. Spurgeon: "If Omnipotence helps you to hold Omnipotence, why, then, the deed is done!"

### Q3. Who is the "Solomon" of 3:6-11? One-character, two-character, and three-character readings

- **Three-character ("shepherd") hypothesis.** The Shulammite loves a shepherd and resists King Solomon. On this view 3:6-11 is Solomon's procession, which may be for another bride. Cambridge reports this option:
> Hence many hold that the verse is spoken of a woman, either of a princess whom Solomon, even in the midst of his wooing of the Shulammite, is about to marry, or of the Shulammite, who is seen approaching Jerusalem with Solomon as her husband in a bridal procession.
  (File: `cambridge.txt`)
- **Two-character, Solomon = the beloved.** Keil-Delitzsch argues that the queen mother's crowning means Solomon is the bridegroom who marries Shulamith with his family's blessing. The "seduction" reading is ruled out. File: `bh3-11.txt`
> Here also the seduction fable is shattered. The marriage with Shulamith takes place with the joyful consent of the queen-mother.
- **Carson (TGC):** the feminine "this" in 3:6 is most likely the woman, carried in Solomon's carriage to court with Solomon present.
- **Allegorical and typological readers** (Durham, Henry, Gill, Poole, Spurgeon, Taylor): Solomon is Christ. Henry: "It is Christ that is here meant, who is greater than Solomon". Durham notes a rise in the titles: "Solomon" (v. 7), "King Solomon" (v. 9), "crowned King Solomon" (v. 11).
- **Who is the mother, on this reading?**
  - The church, or believers, crowning Christ by faith: Durham, Henry, Gill, Poole, JFB.
  - The Jewish church crowning him with thorns: Henry, as an option, and JFB.

### Q4. How did the allegorical tradition read 3:1-4 as the soul seeking Christ?

- **Durham's four steps** (Clavis Cantici) became the standard Reformed outline: bed (formal, lazy duty), then city (public means), then watchmen (ministers), then "a little further" (Christ himself, beyond the means).
- **Henry's Concise Commentary borrows Durham almost word for word.** Compare Henry's "a humble, ardent suing by prayer, with a lively exercise of faith on his promises" with Durham's "an humble, ardent filing to him by prayer, with a lively exercife of faith on his promifes". Henry's full commentary names "Mr. Durham" on 3:10.
- **Spurgeon** preaches the same structure. In Sermon 2485 he tells hearers to "go beyond the means of grace".
- **Bernard** links the search to the women at the tomb. On his "little bed" reading of 3:1: "He is Risen ; He is not here."

## 1. Background and setting

**Place and name of the sea (6:1).** Herod Antipas built Tiberias and named it for the emperor. John gives both names, "Sea of Galilee" and "Sea of Tiberias." Luke places the feeding near Bethsaida.

- Luke 9:10 (ESV), https://biblehub.com/esv/luke/9.htm, file `luke9_esv.txt`
  > And he took them and withdrew apart to a town called Bethsaida.
- Albert Barnes, *Notes on the Bible*, John 6:1, https://biblehub.com/commentaries/john/6-1.htm, file `biblehub_6_1.txt`
  > The place to which he went was Bethsaida, Luke 9:10.
- Matthew Henry (complete) also explains the name Tiberias (Herod "called so in honour of Tiberius the emperor", file `henry_john6.txt`). R.C. Sproul gives the same detail in his sermon (see section 2).

**Which Passover (6:4)?** John names three Passovers (2:13; 6:4; 11:55 and following). If the unnamed feast of 5:1 was also a Passover, 6:4 would be the third. Gill counts it as the third. MacArthur notes that the timing depends on what the feast in 5:1 was. The "much grass" of 6:10 fits early spring.

- John Gill, *Exposition of the Entire Bible*, John 6:4, https://biblehub.com/commentaries/john/6-4.htm, file `biblehub_6_4.txt`
  > This was the third passover, since our Lord's baptism, and entrance on his public ministry; see John 2:13.
- *Pulpit Commentary*, John 6:4, same URL and file `biblehub_6_4.txt`
  > A month later than the Passover it could not be said that "much grass" was in the place.
- Gill also ties the barley loaves to the season. The barley sheaf of firstfruits was waved at Passover (file `gill_6_bh.txt`, at 6:9).

**The only miracle besides the resurrection in all four Gospels.** Ryle:

- J.C. Ryle, *Expository Thoughts on John*, John 6:1-14, https://www.gracegems.org/Ryle/j06.htm, file `ryle_john6.txt`
  > Of all the miracles related in the Gospels, this is the only one which all the four Gospel-writers alike record.
- Henry makes the same point ("the only passage of the actions of Christ's life that is recorded by all the four evangelists", file `henry_john6.txt`), and so do Sproul and MacArthur.

**Barley loaves as poor man's bread; Elisha's feeding (2 Kings 4:42-44).** Only John says the loaves were barley and calls the boy a *paidarion*, "little lad." In 2 Kings 4 a servant objects, "How can I set this before a hundred men?" Andrew's "what are they among so many?" sounds the same note.

- 2 Kings 4:42-44 (ESV), https://biblehub.com/esv/2_kings/4.htm, file `elisha_2kings4.txt` (the verse numbers are fused to the text in the saved file, as below)
  > 42A man came from Baal-shalishah, bringing the man of God bread of the firstfruits, twenty loaves of barley and fresh ears of grain in his sack. And Elisha said, “Give to the men, that they may eat.” 43But his servant said, “How can I set this before a hundred men?” So he repeated, “Give them to the men, that they may eat, for thus says the Lord, ‘They shall eat and have some left.’” 44So he set it before them. And they ate and had some left, according to the word of the Lord.
- Gill, John 6:9, https://biblehub.com/commentaries/gill/john/6.htm, file `gill_6_bh.txt`
  > yet it was bread of the coarsest sort, and what the meaner sort of people ate
- Henry, John 6:9, https://www.biblestudytools.com/commentaries/matthew-henry-complete/john/6.html, file `henry_john6.txt`
  > we find (2 Kings 4 42, 43) that twenty barley-loaves, with some other provision to help out, would not dine a hundred men without a miracle.
- Chrysostom saw the Elisha link too (see `chr2` below: Andrew "having heard ... how Elisha wrought a sign with the loaves 2 Kings 4:43"). Sproul thinks the link is possible but calls it speculation (`spr5`).

**Moses, manna, and "the Prophet who is to come into the world" (6:14; Deut 18:15).** This ties back to John 5:45-47 ("Moses ... wrote of me"). The crowd sees a new Moses feeding Israel in the wilderness.

- Gill, John 6:14, https://biblehub.com/commentaries/gill/john/6.htm, file `gill_6_bh.txt`
  > meaning that prophet, that Moses spoke of, in Deuteronomy 18:15; for the ancient Jews understood this passage of the Messiah, though the modern ones apply it to others; See Gill on Acts 3:22. And these men concluded that Jesus was that prophet, or the true Messiah, from the miracle he wrought; in which he appeared, not only to be like to Moses, but greater than he.
- Augustine (Tractate 24.7) cites "I will raise up for them a prophet like you" and comments: "Like, according to the flesh, but not according to the majesty." (file `augustine_tr24.txt`)
- Piper (file `piper_allproviding_gt.txt`) quotes Deut 18:15 and suggests that Jesus goes "up on a mountain for this miracle, the way Moses went up on the mountain."

**Passover nationalism and the attempt to make him king by force (6:15).**

- Ligonier Ministries (unsigned daily devotional), "Withdrawing from a Confused Crowd," https://www.ligonier.org/learn/devotionals/withdrawing-from-a-confused-crowd, file `ligonier_dev_withdrawing.txt`
  > Such uprisings were particularly likely to take place during Passover, a religious holiday that had become suffused with nationalistic aspirations.
  > Here was a chance for the crowd to experience a great political salvation again under a new prophet
- Matthew Poole, *Annotations*, John 6:15, https://biblehub.com/commentaries/poole/john/6.htm, file `poole_6_bh.txt` (Poole goes on to name Theudas and Judas of Galilee, Acts 5:36-37)
  > The Jews were a people exceedingly jealous of and zealous for their liberties, the Galilaeans especially, amongst whom our Saviour was at that time; so as they never wanted any thing but a leader for a sedition or rebellion.
- Gill, John 6:15, file `gill_6_bh.txt`
  > proclaim him the King Messiah; place him at the head of them, to deliver the nation from the Roman yoke, and set up a temporal kingdom, in which they might hope for great secular advantages
- Calvin is struck that 5,000 men would risk war with "Pilate’s army and the vast power" of Rome (file `calvin_john_ccel2.txt`, on 6:15).

**Twelve baskets.** Augustine and Cyril read them as one basket per apostle, food kept for the teachers. Henry: "one for each disciple; they were thus repaid with interest" Sproul mentions the twelve-tribes reading without pressing it. Chrysostom: the twelve baskets match the number of disciples, as a lesson for them (`chr4` context).

**The sign and the discourse to come.** Calvin, MacLaren, Henry, and Piper all note that John (who usually skips what the Synoptics tell) includes this miracle because it sets up the Bread of Life discourse the next day. Calvin, on 6:1 (file `calvin_john_ccel.txt`): John "repeats the history of a miracle which they had related. But he does so for the express purpose of passing from them to Christ’s sermon, which was delivered next day at Capernaum"

## 3. The hardest questions and how the voices answer

### Q1. Why test Philip (6:5-6), if Jesus "himself knew what he would do"?
- **The test does not mean Jesus was ignorant.** Chrysostom compares it to God "tempting" Abraham (Gen 22). "Prove" is said "after the manner of men" and means Christ "knew exactly." Spurgeon says the Holy Spirit added "for he himself knew" to guard Christ's deity (`spurgeon_jesus_knew.txt`, opening). Henry: `hen1`.
- **It was meant to make the need plain so the miracle would be seen for what it was.** Chrysostom (`chr1`), Calvin (`cal1`), and MacArthur (`mac2`) say Jesus states the impossible situation out loud so nobody can explain it away.
- **Why Philip in particular?** Two lines of answer. (a) Philip was slow to learn: he later says "Show us the Father" (14:8). This is the reading of Chrysostom (`chr1`), Cyril (`cyr1`, `cyr2`), and Spurgeon. (b) Philip was from Bethsaida, near where they were (1:44; Luke 9:10). This is the reading of Gill ("because he was of Bethsaida," `gill_6_bh.txt`), Henry ("Philip was of Bethsaida"), and Spurgeon (`spu3`). Spurgeon also guesses Philip was "the butler" of the Twelve.
- **What the test exposed.** Philip calculated what would *not* be enough and left out the Lord (`spu2`). Henry: `hen2`. Spurgeon: `spu1`, "He wanted not food of Philip, but faith."

### Q2. What kind of king did they want, and why does he refuse (6:14-15)?
- **What they wanted.** A new Moses who would lead an exodus from Rome at Passover (`lig1`, `lig2`, `spr2`, `gil3`, `poo1`). They also wanted a king for their bellies: Chrysostom (`chr5`), Henry (`hen7`), Sproul (`spr3`), MacArthur (`mac5`), and Piper (`pip4`, with 6:26).
- **Why he refuses:**
  - Men cannot make the King. Calvin says crowning is God's prerogative (`cal6`). Augustine: he will "bestow a kingdom on men" (`aug5`).
  - The kingdom is not of this world (18:36). This is the view of Calvin (`cal7`), Chrysostom (`chr6`), Poole, and Piper (`pip3`).
  - The timing was wrong: cross before crown. Augustine (`aug6`) says they wished "to anticipate the time." Calvin (`cal8`) says that if he had allowed it, the "hope of salvation would have been utterly destroyed." The Ligonier devotional says redemption comes "by His humble death on the cross."
  - He is an example of humility and contempt for worldly glory. This is the view of Cyril (`cyr4`), Chrysostom, Henry (`hen8`), and Ryle (`ryl5`).
  - He was no rebel against Caesar. Henry ("no enemy to Cæsar") and Poole (`poo2`) say so.
- **Application.** Calvin (`cal9`), Piper (`pip2`), and the Ligonier devotional (`lig3`) say the same thing: Jesus will be received as he presents himself, on his terms, not ours.

### Q3. What does "Gather up the fragments, that nothing be lost" (6:12) mean?
- **Proof that the miracle was real.** The leftovers show it was no illusion or fancy. This is the view of Chrysostom (`chr4`), Cyril (`cyr3`), Gill, and Henry.
- **Frugality and stewardship.** Do not waste God's gifts. This is the view of Calvin (`cal5`), Henry (`hen5`), and Gill. Henry adds Baxter: "How much less should we lose God's word..."
- **Overflowing bounty.** Henry (`hen6`); MacLaren (`mcl1`, `mcl2`), who argues that the "broken pieces" are surplus, not crumbs; Spurgeon (`spu7`).
- **Spiritual and allegorical readings.** The fragments are deeper truths entrusted to the apostles (Augustine, `aug4`; Cyril similarly). The twelve baskets are one per apostle (Henry, Chrysostom).
- **Link to the discourse.** "That nothing be lost" may echo 6:39, "that I should lose nothing of all that he has given me." I did not find this link stated in the saved sources, so treat it as our own observation, not a quoted one.

### Q4. Sign and discourse: what is the miracle *for*?
- Augustine says miracles "have a tongue of their own" (`aug2`). Daily providence is the greater miracle (`aug1`; Calvin makes a similar point about "the miracles of nature").
- Piper (`pip1`, `pip5`): the miracle shows that Jesus came "not to give you bread, but to be your bread," and it sets up 6:35 and 6:51.
- The crowd misreads the sign. Chrysostom (`chr5`) and Piper (with 2:23-24) say the enthusiasm is for the benefits, not for the Lord.

### *Hupogrammos* in brief
The word is used only here in the NT. It is a schoolroom term: the model letters a writing-master wrote at the head of a page, which the pupil copied or traced line by line (Vincent B6; Barnes B7; Calvin's editor C6). Some also took it as an artist's outline sketch (Geneva B8; Barnes B7). Leighton (L4) works the image out best: "every step of His is a letter of this copy." MacLaren (B9) gives the needed correction, that a copybook cannot save someone holding "a broken pen", and this leads into 2:24.

## 11. The hardest questions and how the voices answer them

**Q1. Does Peter endorse slavery?**
- Peter speaks to slaves directly, as moral agents who answer to God ("mindful of God"), and gives no matching instruction to masters here. The reason usually given is that masters were mostly pagans (JFB and Bengel on bh2-18: "He prescribes duties to these, and not to masters, the greater part of whom were heathens").
- Calvin (C2) holds that a cruel master "does not for the present lose his right". He is frank, though, that slaves were "counted no better than cattle" (C3). He also limits obedience: "servants are to be subject to their masters, only as far as God permits" (calvin.txt).
- Leighton (L1) says the text does not "justify, nor at all excuse" cruel masters. Their conduct remains "perverseness and crookedness" (the meaning of *skolios*). Poole (P2) reads "him that judgeth righteously" as both comfort to slaves and "terror of masters."
- Barnes (B3): the text cannot prove "he approved of slavery." The Pulpit Commentary (B4): slavery "must in time disappear under the softening influences of the gospel." Ligonier (8a): Peter "is not commenting on the normalcy of slavery"; Scripture "sets the trajectory for the abolition."
- A suggested line for the episode: Peter regulates how believers conduct themselves inside an unjust structure. He does not bless the structure. He calls the bad master "crooked," and he holds up a Lord who was himself scourged and crucified like a slave (B10, B13, B15, H6).

**Q2. How can unjust suffering be "grace" (*charis*) or "commendable"?**
- Gill (G1) takes *charis* literally, "Or 'grace'", as "a fruit and effect of grace." Calvin and his editor (calvin.txt fn [32]) read it as "favor", meaning "acceptable" with God. Poole (P1) and Calvin both say the contrast with deserved punishment is "comparative."
- What gives it value is the reason the person endures. Leighton (L3) says obedience is "not be pinned to the goodness and equity of his master." Calvin says "that one performs his duty, not from a regard to men, but to God." Ligonier says suffering is "a vocation laid upon us by the Father." Henry (H4) says even "the meanest servant" can glorify God.
- Calvin (C7) and Leighton (L6) add a warning: entrusting oneself to the just Judge does not mean asking God to be "an executioner" for us.

**Q3. Is the healing in 2:24 physical?**
- Read in context, the healing is from sin. The next verse explains it ("for you were straying like sheep"), and the tense ("you were healed") looks back to a finished work. Gill (G4) says it is "the remission of sin, which is meant by healing." Poole (P4) calls it "spiritual diseases, the guilt of their consciences." Spurgeon (S5) says the stripes heal first "by taking away the guilt of sin." Leighton (L8) speaks of stripes on a back "which in itself was free."
- Tabletalk (8b) gives the balanced answer: physical healing will come in the resurrection, but "it is an error to teach that such healing is guaranteed before the return of Christ."
- There is also a pastoral link to the slaves Peter addresses. *Molops* means the welt left by a beating (B12). Slaves who bore welts are told they were healed by Christ's welt (Gill G5; Bengel B16; Cambridge B13).

**Q4. Is Christ merely our example?**
- All the voices answer no. Verse 21 already says Christ suffered "for you," before it speaks of example (Mathis 8c). Calvin (C8) says example alone would be "very frigid." Henry (H5) speaks of a "more glorious design." Leighton (L7) calls "nothing further" "a high point of falsehood." Spurgeon (S1) says "we have done nothing unless we point him out as the substitute and sin-bearer." The Pulpit Commentary (B14) says "A martyr may be an example... he cannot bear our sins."
- How the two fit together: we imitate Christ's patience, not his miracles (Calvin C5) and not his redemption (Ligonier "Unlike Jesus, our suffering will redeem no one"). His substitution is what makes imitation possible: the Law is "twice written," first in his example and then in our hearts (Leighton L5). Calvin (C11) says Christ's death is effective "for the expiation of sins, and also for the mortification of the flesh." Polycarp shows the early church already held both together: he cites "bore our sins" (2:24) and then says "be imitators of His patience" (F1-F2).
