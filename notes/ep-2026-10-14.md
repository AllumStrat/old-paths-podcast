## 1. Setting and background

### Where these chapters sit
- Jeremiah 46-51 are the "oracles against the nations" (OANs), fulfilling the call of 1:10 ("over nations and over kingdoms"). In the Hebrew (and English) order: Egypt (46), Philistia (47), Moab (48), Ammon, Edom, Damascus, Kedar/Hazor, Elam (49), Babylon (50-51).
- The Greek Septuagint puts the oracles after 25:13 and in a different order; Jer 48 is LXX chapter 31. The LXX lacks the closing promise of 48:47 (Pulpit Commentary on 48:47: "omitted in the Septuagint"; src: jer_bh48_47.txt). It also lacks 48:45-46, the Numbers 21 taunt-song (J. I. Woods, *Jeremiah 48 as Christian Scripture*, Durham PhD 2009; her table of contents lists both the 48:47 restoration promise and the 48:45-46 taunt-song as material absent from the LXX; https://etheses.durham.ac.uk/id/eprint/2050/). This is a fact only; the thesis's copyright notice forbids quotation, so do not quote it.
- Chapter 48 is the longest oracle in the series except Babylon, and much of it reworks Isaiah 15-16 (see below).

### Jeremiah 47: Philistia
- **Heading: "before Pharaoh struck down Gaza" (47:1).** Three views, all in the sources:
  1. **Neco II, 609 B.C.**, on the march north that killed Josiah at Megiddo (2 Kings 23:29), or on his way back. Herodotus 2.159 says that after the battle "at Magdolos" Neco "took Cadytis, which is a great city of Syria" (Macaulay translation; src: jer_bg_herodotus_vol1.txt). Cundall (quoted in Guzik): "The Greek historian Herodotus records a tradition that after the battle at Megiddo, Neco overthrew Kadytis, which is usually identified with the Philistine city of Gaza." Harrison (in Guzik): "The attack may have occurred when Necho was marching to Harran in 609 BC." (src: jer_guzik47.txt.) Caution for the script: Herodotus's "Kadytis" is usually taken as Gaza, but some have identified it with Jerusalem (Herodotus 3.5 calls Kadytis a city "not much less than Sardis"; same src). Trapp also takes the Neco view: Gaza "was first smitten by Pharaoh at his return from Carchemish likely, after he had slain Josiah" (src: jer_trapp47.txt).
  2. **Calvin: Pharaoh Hophra, 588 B.C.** Calvin ties it to Pharaoh's short-lived march to relieve Jerusalem under Zedekiah (Jer 37:5-11). On the way home Pharaoh "plundered Gaza in his way, because it was very hostile to the Jews" (Lecture on 47:1; src: jer_calvin_c47_1.txt). Matthew Henry dates the chapter "b. c. 588" in his heading, but in the comment says the date "is not certain" and gives both options (src: jer_henry_ch47.txt).
  3. A third option often discussed in modern commentaries: the clash between Nebuchadnezzar and Egypt in late 601 B.C. The Babylonian Chronicle (ABC 5) records that in Kislev 601 "In open battle they smote the breast of each other and inflicted great havoc on each other," and Nebuchadnezzar went home (src: jer_bg_abc5.txt). An Egyptian strike on Gaza around 601/600 would fit. Treat this as a possibility; no source here asserts it.
  - Whatever the date, the point of the heading (Henry): the word came "when they were in their full strength and lustre ... in no peril from any adversary" (src: jer_henry_ch47.txt). The danger is not from Egypt in the south but "waters rise up out of the north" (47:2), meaning Babylon.
- **Fulfilment, Ashkelon 604 B.C.** Babylonian Chronicle (ABC 5, Obv. 18-20), Nebuchadnezzar's first year: "He marched to the city of Aškelon and captured it in the month of Kislîmu" [late 604]. "He captured its king and plundered it and carried off spoil from it." "He turned the city into a mound and heaps of ruins" (livius.org; src: jer_bg_abc5.txt). Compare 47:5 "Ashkelon has perished" and 47:7 "Against Ashkelon and against the seashore he has appointed it."
- **The Adon letter (Saqqara papyrus).** An Aramaic letter found at Saqqara in 1942 from a Philistine king, Adon, to Pharaoh: "[the forces] of the king of Babylon have come and have reached Apeq" ... "to send a force and rescue us" (translation on bookandsword.com; src: jer_bg_adon_bookandsword.txt). A Demotic note on the back has been read as "Ekron" (Porten 1981, cited on Wikipedia; src: jer_bg_adon_king_wiki.txt, jer_bg_adon_wiki.txt). Usually dated c. 604-603. It is a vivid parallel to 47:4, God will "cut off from Tyre and Sidon every helper that remains": the help Philistia begged for never came.
- **Kidner (via Guzik) on the aftermath:** "A Babylonian prism, now in Istanbul, mentions the presence – presumably with little choice in the matter – of the kings of Tyre and Sidon, of Gaza, and of Ashdod, at the court of Nebuchadnezzar, while a prison list now in Berlin records the rations for the king of Ashkelon, among other noted prisoners (including Jehoiachin of Judah)." (src: jer_guzik47.txt)
- **Caphtor (47:4, "the remnant of the coastland of Caphtor").** Compare Amos 9:7 ("the Philistines from Caphtor") and Deut 2:23. Traditional identification: Crete, linked with Egyptian *Keftiu* and Akkadian *Kaptara*; some modern scholars argue for Cyprus or Cilicia (Wikipedia "Caphtor"; src: jer_bg_caphtor_wiki.txt). Harrison (via Guzik): "Caphtor is the Old Testament designation of Crete, the land from which the Philistines came originally." Calvin, following the old versions, took Caphtor to mean Cappadocia (src: jer_calvin_c47_4.txt). Nice detail: the Philistines were the "Sea Peoples" immigrants of c. 1200 B.C.
- **The end of the Philistines as a people.** After Nebuchadnezzar's campaign of 604/603, Ashkelon, Ekron and the others were destroyed; the people were killed or deported. Those exiled kept calling themselves "men of Gaza" or Ashkelon for about 150 years, then lost their distinct identity; they "disappeared as a distinct ethnic group by the late 5th century BC" (Wikipedia "Philistines"; src: jer_bg_philistines_wiki.txt). Ashkelon's destruction layer of 604 is famous from the Leon Levy Expedition. Unlike Moab (48:47), Philistia gets no promise of restored fortunes in Jeremiah; yet Zechariah 9:7 promises that a Philistine remnant "too shall be a remnant for our God ... like a clan in Judah." The Pulpit/Biblical Illustrator homily on 47:6-7 asks exactly that question: "Is it contradictory, then, for Zechariah to prophecy the conversion of the Philistines? The rightful end of judgment is mercy." (A. F. Muir; src: jer_bhsermons47_6.txt)

### Jeremiah 48: Moab
- **Who Moab was.** Descended from Lot by his elder daughter (Gen 19:30-38). Israel was told not to dispossess Moab (Deut 2:9). Then came Balak and Balaam (Num 22-24) and the sin at Peor (Num 25); the law excluded the Moabite from the assembly (Deut 23:3-4). Eglon oppressed Israel (Judg 3). Ruth the Moabitess became David's great-grandmother and stands in Christ's line (Matt 1:5). David subdued Moab (2 Sam 8:2). Solomon built a high place for Chemosh (1 Kings 11:7), which Josiah tore down (2 Kings 23:13). Mesha rebelled after Ahab (2 Kings 1:1; 3:4-27). Moabite raiders harassed Judah in Jehoiakim's day (2 Kings 24:2), and Moab sent envoys to Zedekiah's anti-Babylon summit (Jer 27:3). Other prophetic oracles against Moab: Isa 15-16; 25:10; Amos 2:1-3; Zeph 2:8-11; Ezek 25:8-11.
- **Chemosh** (48:7, 13, 46 "people of Chemosh"; Num 21:29). Moab's national god. Harrison (via Guzik): "Chemosh was the principal Moabite deity (Numbers 21:29) and the sacrificing of children was an important part of his cult (2 Kings 3:27)." (src: jer_guzik48.txt). In 48:7 "Chemosh shall go into exile with his priests and his officials": the god goes into captivity with his worshippers, as idols were carted off (cf. Isa 46:1-2).
- **The Mesha Stele (Moabite Stone), c. 840 B.C.** Found at Dibon (Dhiban) in August 1868 by F. A. Klein, an Anglican missionary (Wikipedia; src: jer_bg_mesha_wiki.txt). It is Mesha's own account of throwing off Israel, and it reads like a Moabite mirror image of biblical theology. Text (older translation on the Wikipedia page):
  - "Omri was king of Israel, and oppressed Moab during many days, and Chemosh was angry with his aggressions."
  - "And Chemosh said to me, Go take Nebo against Israel ... and I took from it the vessels of Jehovah, and offered them before Chemosh." (This is the earliest known mention of YHWH outside the Bible.)
  - "And I built Baal-meon and made therein the ditch, and I built Kiriathaim."
  - "I built Aroer, and I made the road across the Arnon."
  - "And I built Beth-Gamul, and Beth-Diblathaim...Beth Baal-Meon"
  - "And the king of Israel fortified Jahaz ... and Chemosh drove him out before me"
  - "And as to Horonaim ... Chemosh said to me, Go down, make war against Horonaim, and take it."
  - Medeba ("Madeba"), Dibon, Ataroth, Bezer also appear.
  - **Script point:** nearly every town in Jer 48:1-5, 18-24, 34 (Nebo, Kiriathaim, Heshbon, Horonaim, Dibon, Aroer, Jahaz, Beth-diblathaim, Beth-gamul, Beth-meon, Medeba [Isa 15:2]) is on Mesha's victory stone. Jeremiah walks through Mesha's trophy list town by town and announces its undoing. Mesha boasted that Chemosh gave him these cities. Jeremiah says Chemosh will go into exile from them (48:7) and Moab "shall be ashamed of Chemosh" (48:13).
- **Fulfilment.** Josephus, *Antiquities* 10.9.7: "for on the fifth year after the destruction of Jerusalem, which was the twenty-third of the reign of Nebuchadnezzar, he made an expedition against Celesyria; and when he had possessed himself of it, he made war against the Ammonites and Moabites; and when he had brought all these nations under subjection, he fell upon Egypt" (Whiston; Gutenberg text broken across lines; src: jer_bg_josephus_ant.txt). That is 582/581 B.C. Compare Jer 52:30 (a deportation in Nebuchadnezzar's 23rd year). Keil and Delitzsch accept the report: "This statement is not to be questioned" (src: jer_bh48_47.txt). Thompson (via Guzik): "The end of Moab as an independent nation seems to have come in 582 BC when Nebuchadnezzar, no doubt because of a rebellion, marched against Moab and Ammon." Harrison (via Guzik): "Moab’s predicted extinction began with a heavy Nabatean settlement in the first century BC, and culminated under the Arabs in the Byzantine period." (src: jer_guzik48.txt). Moabites still appear after the exile (Ezra 9:1; Neh 13:1, 23). Gill notes that Alexander Jannaeus subdued them (Josephus *Ant.* 13; src: jer_gill48.txt). As a people they then vanish from history.
- **Isaiah 15-16 parallels.** Jer 48 freely reuses Isaiah: 48:5 = Isa 15:5; 48:29-30 = Isa 16:6; 48:31 = Isa 16:7; 48:32-33 = Isa 16:8-10; 48:34 = Isa 15:4-6; 48:36 = Isa 16:11 and 15:7; 48:37-38 = Isa 15:2-3; 48:43-44 = Isa 24:17-18. Henry: "it becomes God's prophets to speak the language of those that went before them. It is no plagiarism sometimes to make use of old expressions, provided it be with new affections and applications." (src: jer_henry_ch48.txt). Keil notes the one big change in 48:31: Isaiah's raisin-cakes (*ashishe*) of Kir-hareseth become the men (*anshe*) of Kir-heres. Jeremiah made the change "because his sympathy was directed" (Keil's sentence is cut off on the page after this point, so it is paraphrased here: toward people, not food) (src: jer_bh48_31.txt).
- **Numbers 21:28-29 and 24:17 in 48:45-46.** "A fire has gone out from Heshbon, a flame from the house of Sihon; it has destroyed the forehead of Moab, the crown of the sons of tumult. Woe to you, O Moab! The people of Chemosh are undone." That combines the old Amorite taunt-song (Num 21:28-29) with Balaam's oracle of the star and scepter that "shall crush the forehead of Moab" (Num 24:17). Thompson (via Guzik): "We may see in these words a claim that Balaam’s oracle against Moab was about to be enacted." (src: jer_guzik48.txt). **Script point:** the man Moab hired to curse Israel (Balak hired Balaam) ends up supplying the words of Moab's doom. Balaam's star-and-scepter oracle is messianic. So the same chapter that ends with "in the latter days" (48:47; cf. Num 24:14 "in the latter days") points toward the Son of David who descends from Ruth the Moabitess.

### Key Hebrew words (simple meanings)
- **47:6 *hoy chereb laYHWH*** "Ah, sword of the LORD!" The sword is told to "be quiet" (*tishqoti*, from *shaqat*, to be at rest or undisturbed), "rest" (*heragi'i*) and "be still" (*dommi*). (src: jer_bg_int47_6.txt)
- **48:11 *sha'anan*** "at ease, complacent"; ***shoqet*** "undisturbed, quiet". It is the same root (*shaqat*) as 47:6's "be quiet"! ***shemarim*** "dregs, lees", the sediment wine rests on. Benson notes the word "signifies the preservers," as if from *shamar*, keep (src: jer_bh48_11.txt). *Shamar* is a different root from the earlier *shama'* thread, so avoid confusing them. ***hurak mikkeli el-keli*** "emptied/poured from vessel to vessel"; ***ta'mo*** "his taste/flavor"; ***reycho*** "his scent/aroma". (src: jer_bg_int48_11.txt)
  - **Script point (link of the two chapters):** the sword of the LORD cannot be *shaqat* (quiet) because Moab has been *shoqet* (quiet) on his dregs. God's undisturbed patience ends with the undisturbed sinner.
- **48:12 *tso'im*** "pourers, tilters": the men who tip the jars (ESV "pourers who will pour him"). Keil cites Luther's "Schröter," cellarmen who haul casks out of the cellar (src: jer_bh48_11.txt).
- **48:10 *remiyyah*** "slackness, negligence" (KJV "deceitfully"; Prov 10:4; 12:24 "slack hand"). Keil: the word is used adverbially, not as "deceitfully" but "carelessly, negligently" (paraphrase; Keil's exact words are on jer_bh48_11.txt). ***'arur*** "cursed" (twice). ***mone'a charbo middam*** "withholding his sword from blood". Vulgate: *maledictus qui facit opus Domini fraudulenter, et maledictus qui prohibet gladium suum a sanguine* (src: jer_eeps_A04657.txt).
- **48:29, six words for pride:** *ge'on* (pride), *ge'eh* (proud), *gobho* (his loftiness), *ge'ono* (his arrogance), *ga'avato* (his pridefulness), *rum libbo* ("height of his heart", haughtiness). Four of them come from the one root *ga'ah*, "to rise up, be high". (src: jer_bg_int48_29.txt). Calvin's editor: "The word for “arrogance” means swelling; it is to grow big, and to claim more than what belongs to us." (src: jer_calvin_c48_26.txt)
- **48:31, 36 *yalal*** "wail, howl"; ***hamah*** "moan, murmur, sound" ("my heart moans like a flute", *chalilim*, funeral pipes; cf. Matt 9:23).
- **48:47 *weshabti shebut-Mo'ab be'acharit hayyamim*** "I will restore the fortunes (turn the captivity) of Moab in the latter days". *shub shebut* = "turn the turning" or "return the captivity", Jeremiah's standard restoration phrase (29:14; 30:3, 18; 31:23; 33:7, 11, 26). It is used of the nations only for Moab (48:47), Ammon (49:6) and Elam (49:39); Egypt gets a similar promise in 46:26. ***'ad-hennah mishpat Mo'ab*** "Thus far is the judgment on Moab". (src: jer_bg_int48_47.txt; Keil and Delitzsch in jer_bh48_47.txt)

## 3. Interpretive questions

### Q1. "Ah, sword of the LORD, how long?" (47:6-7). Who is speaking, and is the pity real?
Grammar: the sword is addressed in the second person feminine ("be quiet," "your sheath"). Verse 7 answers: "How can it be quiet when the LORD has given it a charge?"
- **Calvin: it is a dramatic speech, not real pity.** The prophet "assumes the character of another" and speaks for the Philistines. "The Prophet, it is certain, had no such feeling". Verse 7 is the answer that shuts down their plea. He stresses that God "bound his sword, as it were, by solemn protest ... never to cease from its work" (src: jer_calvin_c47_6.txt).
- **Henry: the pity is genuine.** "This expresses the prophet's earnest desire to see an end of the war, looking with compassion, as became a man, even upon the Philistines themselves". Then v. 7 "stops the mouth of his own complaint". He adds the famous proverb: "Every bullet has its charge" (src: jer_henry_ch47.txt).
- **Gill and Poole give both options and lean toward Henry.** Gill: "or rather of the prophet, commiserating their state as a man, though they had been the avowed enemies of his people" (src: jer_gill47.txt). Poole: "for good men are affected with the miseries even of the worst of men" (src: jer_poole47.txt).
- **Trapp:** the prophet stills his own heart: "Here the prophet quieteth himself howsoever by a humble submission to his holy will, who had put the sword in commission." (src: jer_trapp47.txt)
- **MacLaren** (Baptist) takes it as the prophet's cry, a "tender gleam of compassion" breaking out at Israel's nearest enemy. He ends at the cross: "God's sword was sheathed in Christ's breast." (src: jer_maclaren_sword.txt)
- **F. B. Meyer (via Guzik)** turns it to the Word as sword in the believer: "And even now it cannot rest." (src: jer_guzik47.txt)
- **Takeaway for the host:** Calvin and Henry disagree, and that disagreement opens the episode's main question. Can the man who announces God's judgment also weep over it? Calvin protects the certainty of the decree. Henry and MacLaren protect the prophet's heart. Both say verse 7 is final: the sword has a commission.

### Q2. "Cursed is he who does the work of the LORD with slackness" (48:10). How have preachers used it?
In context, the curse falls on Babylon's soldiers if they go easy on Moab. Down the centuries, preachers have applied it to slackness in any work for God. **The voices split here more sharply than anywhere else in the chapter.**
- **Calvin flatly rejects the popular application.** "This passage has been very absurdly explained, and it is commonly quoted as though the Prophet had said, that special care ought to be taken by us, not to omit anything of what God commands. But they thus misrepresent the meaning." For Calvin the verse is addressed (rhetorically) to the Chaldeans, for Judah's sake. Its real point is that Moab's destruction "was the work of Jehovah" even though the Babylonians acted "from avarice and ambition" (src: jer_calvin_c48_09.txt).
- **Henry, Gill and Trapp all make the very application Calvin rejects.** First they read it literally (Babylon must not spare, as Saul spared Agag and Ahab spared Ben-hadad). Then they widen it. Henry: "To this work is applied that general rule given to all that are employed in any service for God". Trapp: "But the text taketh in all lawful employments ... Ministers must look to the ministry which they have received of the Lord" (src: jer_henry_ch48.txt; jer_gill48.txt; jer_trapp48.txt).
- **Poole marks the limits:** "There is a time to withhold our hands from shedding blood, and that is always when we have not a special authority and call from God to it; and there is a time when God will curse those that do so withhold their hands, that time is when God doth require the shedding of it." (src: jer_poole48.txt)
- **Medieval: Gregory VII (Hildebrand), 1073**, applied the sword to *preaching*. Registrum I.15 (1 July 1073), as translated in I. S. Robinson, *JEH* 34 (1983): "Again the prophet says, “Cursed be the man who holds back his sword from blood” (Jeremiah xlviii. 10); that is, the word of preaching from the rebuke of carnal men." (src: jer_gregory7_cowdrey.txt). **Correction to the lead in the task:** Gregory the *Great's* Pastoral Rule does not cite Jer 48:10 in the NPNF translation. I searched all four books on New Advent. Gregory the Great makes the same point from Exodus 32:27 ("put every man his sword upon his thigh"): the pastor "spares not even those whom he loves on the score of relationship from the sword of his rebuke" (Pastoral Rule III.25 [NPNF "Admonition 26"]; src: jer_gregory_pastoral_bk3.txt). Gregory VII stands in that tradition and makes the Jer 48:10 link explicit. Gregory the Great is fine to quote for the idea, but say plainly that his text is Exodus 32, not Jeremiah 48.
- **Puritan / 17th century:** the verse was a favorite in fast sermons and war sermons. Stephen Marshall's 1641/2 Commons sermon is said to cite it (search result only, not verified; don't use). Increase Mather's 1686 Boston execution sermon cites it (earlyenglishprintedsermons.org index; src: jer_eeps_A50229.txt; the index only, no quotable text). Ellicott's warning: "It has been used, with less justification, in the religious wars of our own and other countries." (src: jer_bh48_10.txt)
- **Jeremy Taylor (Anglican bishop, 17th c.),** "Of Lukewarmness and Zeal": "He that serves God with the body without the soul serves God deceitfully." (src: jer_bhsermons48_10.txt)
- **Kidner (via Guzik)** sums up both sides: "Note the fearful twist to what may well have been a proverb or preacher’s text in verse 10a ... turning it into a charge to Moab’s executioners." (src: jer_guzik48.txt). Kidner's guess that it was already a proverb would explain why preachers have always pulled it out of context.
- **Script suggestion:** let Calvin and Henry disagree openly. Then make a careful bridge. The *text* is about God's judgment carried out without half-measures. The *principle* behind it is that God's work deserves our whole heart. That principle stands on other texts (Mal 1:14; Rom 12:11; Col 3:23), which is how Henry and Trapp actually argue. For a PCA audience: the question of "the sword" belongs to the magistrate, who may "wage war, upon just and necessary occasion" (WCF 23.2; src: jer_wcf_opc.txt), never to the church. Gregory's "sword of rebuke" is the church's only sword (the Word, Eph 6:17; Heb 4:12).

### Q3. How can God both decree and lament Moab's destruction (48:31-36), and what does 48:47's restoration mean?
**(a) Who weeps?** The "I" of 48:31-36 sits between first-person divine speech ("declares the LORD" in vv. 30, 35, 38; "I have made the wine cease," v. 33). Many readers therefore hear God himself wailing. The older commentators mostly say it is the prophet.
- **Calvin: dramatic voice again.** "The Prophet does not relate here what he would do ... he mentions their howling, crying, and complaints" (48:31). And on 48:36: "the Prophet, as it has been before stated, does not mourn the calamity of the people of Moab, but assumes the character of others" (src: jer_calvin_c48_28.txt; jer_calvin_c48_35.txt).
- **Gill** leans the same way, following Kimchi: "he does not so much design to express the affections of his own heart, as to show what reason the Moabites would have to howl" (src: jer_gill48.txt). JFB: it is "Not that it deserves pity", but the prophet's crying "vividly represents the greatness of the calamity." (src: jer_bh48_31.txt)
- **Henry and Poole: real compassion, and it is God-like.** Henry: "The ruin of sinners is no pleasure to God, and therefore should be a pain to us; even those that give warning of it should lay it to heart." (src: jer_henry_ch48.txt). Poole: "Jeremiah declareth his compassion toward these Moabites, though they derided the Jews when they were carried into captivity." (src: jer_bh48_31.txt)
- **Keil and Delitzsch:** the "I" is the prophet. Against critics who called such sympathy a contradiction, Keil says that view "makes out the prophet to be a Jew thirsting for revenge, which he was not." Keil quotes Rashi: "the prophets of Israel differ from heathen prophets like Balaam in this, that they lay to heart the distress which they announce to the nations" (src: jer_bh48_31.txt). (Balaam again! Balaam cursed for pay; the true prophet weeps over those he must condemn.)
- **A modern reading (Luther Seminary's Enter the Bible, unsigned; mainline, use with care)** says the voice is God's own: "God wails, cries out, and mourns for Moab," and "Israel has no monopoly on divine empathy." (src: jer_enterthebible48.txt). Not a conservative source. It is here only to name the modern view the host may want to answer.
- **A Reformed way through:** the God who "has no pleasure in the death of the wicked" (Ezek 18:23, 32; 33:11) is the same God who decrees and carries out the judgment (48:38 "I have broken Moab"). Jeremiah's tears are not a crack in the decree; they show the heart of the God who sent him. Fullest of all, they foreshadow Christ, who decreed Jerusalem's fall and wept over the city (Luke 19:41-44). Calvin's caution is worth keeping too: the tears do not mean the judgment is unjust or uncertain. The WCF's language of God as "most loving, gracious, merciful" ... "and withal, most just, and terrible in his judgments" (WCF 2.1, OPC text; src: jer_wcf_opc.txt) holds both together.

**(b) What does "I will restore the fortunes of Moab in the latter days" (48:47) mean?**
- **Calvin:** the promise is real but given "as it were accidentally". It is mainly for Judah: if God shows mercy even to Moab, the Jews may "feel assured that God would at length be propitious to them". In the next lecture Calvin adds that God "did not wholly obliterate that nation ... and from that nation also Christ, the Redeemer of the world, derived his origin" (via Ruth). He calls these "preludes of his mercy towards alien nations" (src: jer_calvin_c48_45.txt; jer_calvin_c48_47.txt).
- **Henry:** "Thus tenderly does God deal with Moabites, much more with his own people!" and "it looks further, to gospel times ... the captivity of the Gentiles, under the yoke of sin and Satan, shall be brought back by divine grace" (src: jer_henry_ch48.txt).
- **Gill:** a layered fulfilment: partly literal under Cyrus; spiritual "in the first times of the Gospel"; and again "in the latter day," through the conversion of whoever lives in Moab's land "when the fulness of the Gentiles is brought in" (postmillennial colouring; src: jer_gill48.txt).
- **Poole:** not the Jews' return to Moab, but "a spiritual reduction of them, by calling some of them under the kingdom of the Messiah, as the Jewish doctors themselves interpret it" (src: jer_poole48.txt).
- **Trapp:** never fulfilled "in the letter," so "it was a presage of the calling of the Gentiles to an interest in Christ" (src: jer_trapp48.txt).
- **JFB:** restoration "for the sake of righteous Lot, their progenitor" (src: jer_bh48_47.txt). **Clarke (via Guzik):** "Their conversion to the Christian faith must be the main end designed by this prophecy." (src: jer_guzik48.txt)
- **Where they differ:** Calvin keeps the focus on Judah's comfort (Moab's mercy is a side-gift) and on Christ's descent from Moab. Gill expects a still-future mass conversion in the region. Henry, Poole and Trapp read it as fulfilled in the gospel call of the Gentiles. All agree it points to Christ and "the latter days" (cf. Num 24:14, 17).
- **Textual note for honesty:** the LXX lacks v. 47 (Pulpit Commentary; src: jer_bh48_47.txt). The Reformed commentators all treat the Hebrew text as Scripture, and the parallel promises to Ammon and Elam (49:6, 39) support it.

### Smaller but rich: 48:11-12, the wine image (likely the most preachable verse)
- Wine-making practice: new wine is left to settle on its lees and then racked (poured off) from jar to jar to clarify it and lose its harshness. Wine left too long on coarse lees stays thick and harsh (Zeph 1:12, where the complacent men are thickening on their dregs). Isaiah 25:6 uses "wines on the lees" (KJV) in a good sense. Keil sorts the two senses out: good wine improves on its lees, inferior wine "becomes thereby more harsh and thick. The figure is used here in the latter sense, after Zephaniah 1:12" (src: jer_bh48_11.txt).
- Calvin's pastoral angle: for persecuted Israel, watching untouched Moab was a trial; the Prophet "exhorts them not to envy the happiness of the Moabites" (Ps 37) (src: jer_calvin_c48_10.txt).
- **Fun fact:** Spurgeon (Metropolitan Tabernacle) and J. C. Philpot (Gower Street Chapel, Strict Baptist) both preached on Jer 48:11 on the same Sunday, **21 July 1867** (src: jer_spurgeon761_flow.txt; jer_philpot_moab_flow.txt). Spurgeon applied it to the unconverted and then to believers and his own church. Philpot applied it to "a professor in the church of God destitute of divine grace."
- Kidner (via Guzik): the Hudson Taylor chapter titled "Emptied from Vessel to Vessel" (src: jer_guzik48.txt). That is a good modern bridge.

## 1. Setting and background

**Date and author.** The superscription (1:1) reads "Solomon's." Conservative scholars place it in the Solomonic era, around the 10th century BC. Others read "which is Solomon's" as a dedication ("in Solomon's tradition"). Either way it sits in Israel's wisdom corpus alongside Proverbs and Ecclesiastes. O'Donnell (Themelios 37.1) and Ortlund (Themelios 45.3) both argue that the refrain in 2:7 makes the Song wisdom literature, addressed especially to the unmarried.

**Genre.** Lyric love poetry with shifting speakers: the woman (the Shulammite of 6:13), the man, and the "daughters of Jerusalem" chorus. It is not a stage drama. Ellicott, on 2:7: the daughters "are present only in the poet's imagination." Egyptian love songs are the nearest ancient parallels: Keel, cited by Ortlund, notes that "foxes" appear as womanizers in Egyptian love songs.

**Structure of ch. 2.** Chapter 2 straddles two sections of the Song:
- 2:1-7 closes the opening movement (1:2-2:7), which ends on the first refrain (2:7).
- 2:8-17 is a self-contained spring poem. A gazelle bounding on the mountains frames it at both ends (2:8-9 and 2:17). Inside, "Arise, my love, my beautiful one, and come away" brackets the spring catalogue (2:10b and 2:13b).
- Ortlund's seven-part chiasm of the whole book: (A) 1:2-2:7, (B) 2:8-17, (C) 3:1-5, (D) 3:6-5:1 (the wedding), (C') 5:2-6:3, (B') 6:4-8:4, (A') 8:5-14. Sections A, C and B' each end with the refrain (2:7; 3:5; 8:4). The call to "come away" in B (2:10, 13) is echoed and finally fulfilled when the bride herself says "Come, my beloved, let us go out into the fields" (7:11-12).

**Cross-echoes to flag on air:**
- Refrain "do not stir up or awaken love until it pleases": 2:7; 3:5; 8:4.
- Mutual-possession formula: 2:16 "My beloved is mine, and I am his"; 6:3 "I am my beloved's and my beloved is mine"; 7:10 "I am my beloved's, and his desire is for me." Hudson Taylor reads the order as spiritual growth: first her claim on him, then his claim on her first, then only his desire.
- Left hand / right hand embrace: 2:6 = 8:3.
- Gazelle on the mountains: 2:9, 17 → 8:14 (the book's last line).
- "Pasturing among the lilies": 2:16; 4:5; 6:2-3.
- The vineyard as an image of the woman herself: 1:6 ("my own vineyard I have not kept", which links to last episode's Spurgeon) → 2:15 → 8:12.

**Plants, animals and other realia:**
- "Rose of Sharon" (2:1): Hebrew *ḥăḇaṣṣeleṯ* (khav-ats-TSEH-leth). It occurs elsewhere only in Isa 35:1. The word is linked to *beṣel*, "bulb." ESV note: "Probably a bulb, such as a crocus, asphodel, or narcissus." Cambridge Bible renders it "crocus of Sharon" (meadow saffron); Ellicott and Tristram prefer the sweet-scented narcissus. Sharon is the coastal plain, and the LXX simply has "flower of the plain/field" (Vulgate *flos campi*, Bernard's text). The point is that it is a common wildflower and she is speaking modestly. Not a garden rose.
- "Lily" (2:1-2, 16): *šôšannâ* (sho-shan-NAH), the root of the name "Susan." It may be any bright flower: iris, anemone, lotus.
- "Brambles/thorns" (2:2): *ḥôaḥ*, thorn bushes. He answers her modesty: if she is only a wildflower, she is a lily among thistles.
- "Apple tree" (2:3): *tappûaḥ*. Apricot, citron or apple are all proposed; Hudson Taylor says citron. Her "sitting in his shadow" uses the vocabulary of Ps 91:1 (Ortlund).
- "Banqueting house" (2:4): literally *bêṯ hayyāyin*, "house of wine" (ESV note). It may be a wine hall or a nuptial feast.
- "Banner" (2:4): *degel*, a military standard. Some modern scholars render it "intent" or "glance" from an Akkadian cognate; mention lightly if at all.
- LXX 2:4: "set love in order upon me" (*taxate ep' eme agapēn*; Vulgate *ordinate in me caritatem*). Origen, Gregory of Nyssa, Augustine and Bernard all build on this line, so it is the patristic "hook" of the chapter.
- "Raisins" (2:5): *ʾăšîšôṯ*, pressed raisin cakes, festive food (2 Sam 6:19). "Sick with love" means faint with love.
- "I adjure you" (2:7): *hišbaʿtî*, "I put you under oath." By the gazelles (*ṣĕḇāʾôṯ*) and does of the field (*ʾayĕlôṯ haśśādeh*). The Pulpit Commentary cites Delitzsch: this is perhaps the only adjuration in Scripture without God's name. Some (Garrett) hear puns on "Lord of hosts" (*ṣĕḇāʾôṯ*) and "God Almighty" (*šadday*). Ortlund doubts this.
- "Stir up or awaken" (2:7): *ʿûr*, two forms of the same root. "Love": *hāʾahăḇâ*, love itself, not "my love." Barnes: "The affection or passion in itself, not its object, is here meant." "Until it pleases": *ʿad šetteḥpāṣ*, "until it desires."
- "Gazelle / young stag" (2:9, 17): *ṣĕḇî*, *ʿōp̄er hāʾayyālîm*, images of speed, grace and shyness (Prov 5:19).
- "Time of singing" (2:12): *zāmîr*, which may also mean "pruning" (ESV note). "Turtledove": *tôr*, a migratory bird whose return marks spring.
- "Foxes" (2:15): *šûʿālîm*, foxes or jackals (ESV note). Cambridge Bible: "the burrower." They dig holes, gnaw shoots and eat grapes. "Our vineyards are in blossom" (*sĕmāḏar*).
- "Grazes among the lilies" (2:16): *hārōʿeh baššôšannîm*. ESV note: "Or he pastures his flock."
- "Until the day breathes" (2:17): *ʿad šeyyāp̄ûaḥ hayyôm*, from *pûaḥ*, "to blow, breathe." This is the breeze at day's turning, either dawn or the evening "cool of the day" as in Gen 3:8; commentators split. Barnes and Ellicott say evening; JFB says dawn. "Cleft mountains": *hārê ḇāṯer*, either "mountains of Bether" (KJV/ESV note) or "mountains of separation." Spurgeon, Gill and Henry all use "mountains of division."

## 2. Outline with key verses

**A. The wildflower and the lily: mutual praise (2:1-3)**
- 2:1 She: "I am a rose of Sharon, a lily of the valleys." This is modest self-description: "I'm just a wildflower." (Older Christ-reading interpreters such as Bernard, Henry and Benson put this verse in the Bridegroom's mouth. Taylor, Cambridge, Ellicott and the ESV give it to her.)
- 2:2 He: "As a lily among brambles, so is my love among the young women."
- 2:3 She: "As an apple tree among the trees of the forest..." She delights to sit in his shade and taste his fruit.

**B. The house of wine and the first refrain (2:4-7)**
- 2:4 "He brought me to the banqueting house, and his banner over me was love." This is public, unashamed love.
- 2:5 "Sustain me with raisins... for I am sick with love."
- 2:6 "His left hand is under my head, and his right hand embraces me!"
- 2:7 REFRAIN: "I adjure you, O daughters of Jerusalem... that you not stir up or awaken love until it pleases."

**C. The spring poem: "Arise, my love... come away" (2:8-17)**
- 2:8-9 He comes like a gazelle, leaping the mountains, and stands behind the wall at the lattice.
- 2:10-13 His invitation, framed by "Arise, my love, my beautiful one, and come away" (vv. 10, 13). Winter is past, the rains are over, flowers appear, the turtledove sings, figs ripen, vines blossom.
- 2:14 "O my dove, in the clefts of the rock... let me see your face, let me hear your voice."
- 2:15 "Catch the foxes for us, the little foxes that spoil the vineyards, for our vineyards are in blossom."
- 2:16 "My beloved is mine, and I am his; he grazes among the lilies." This is the covenant formula of mutual belonging.
- 2:17 "Until the day breathes and the shadows flee, turn, my beloved, be like a gazelle or a young stag on cleft mountains." Is this "come back to me" or "go for now"? See 3c below.

## 3. Interpretive questions

### (a) What does the refrain (2:7) forbid, and how does it fit the allegorical reading?
- **Plain sense, the modern Reformed consensus:** it is a wisdom warning about timing. Do not artificially arouse romantic and sexual love before its proper time, which is marriage. Ligonier: "let love arise on its own and not rush into it or force it to happen," yet "men and women can pursue each other." Ortlund (Themelios): the refrain identifies the audience, because "married couples don't need to be told to let love wake up on its own. Unmarried people do." Leeman (TGC): the very strength of love requires care.
- **Barnes / Pulpit:** genuine love is shy, like gazelles, and dreads intrusion; do not force love.
- **The older Christ-and-church reading:** a charge not to disturb the soul's rest in Christ, or not to grieve Christ away. Henry: the believer cannot but desire the continuance of communion, "yet Christ will, when he pleases, withdraw." Poole has a remarkable gloss on "until he please": "i.e. never," because sin can never please him. Bernard (Serm. 52) reads it as Christ guarding the contemplative soul's sleep. He frankly admits that the oath "by the roes and hinds" has "no [line break] reasonable consequence in the literal sense" (Q18). Hudson Taylor: "It is never by His will that our rest in Him is disturbed."
- **Grammar point:** the KJV "my love... till he please" led older readers to make "love" a person (Christ). Modern translations (ESV "love... until it pleases") follow Barnes and Ellicott: "love" is the passion itself. This shift explains most of the gap between the old and new readings. It is a good teaching moment on why translation matters.
- **Surprise:** Gill weighs both speakers at length and admits "there is some difficulty in these words."

### (b) Who are the "little foxes" (2:15)? Heretics to be hunted, or small sins to be mortified?
- **Bernard (Serm. 63-66):** foxes are first the subtle temptations of monks: flatterers, detractors, and vices disguised as virtues (Serm. 63-64). Then, allegorically, they are heretics (Serm. 64-66; 65-66 were prompted by Everwin of Steinfeld's letter about the Cologne Cathars). They are "little" "not because they have little malice, but because of their subtlety." They are to be "taken... not by force of arms, but by force of arguments." Bernard also lingers lovingly on "Take FOR US": Christ makes the church his partner.
- **Durham (Scottish Covenanter, 1660s):** foxes are false teachers. Even the little ones must be hunted: "How fmali a friend is our Lord to toleration!" This is a period piece and needs framing for listeners.
- **Matthew Henry:** both readings. Personal: "the first risings of sin." Ecclesiastical: heretics and schismatics. But they "must not be knocked on the head (Christ came not to destroy men's lives), but taken, that they may be tamed." So Henry is gentler than Durham, and closer to Bernard's "by arguments."
- **Poole / Gill:** false teachers, to be dealt with early, before error grows "incurable." Gill: the "tender grapes" are young converts, who are most vulnerable.
- **Spurgeon (Morning & Evening, May 30), Hudson Taylor, Gregory of Nyssa:** the inner reading. Small sins break fellowship with Christ. Taylor's list: "Little compromises with the world; disobedience to the still small voice in little things..." Gregory: even the princes and powers of darkness are mere "little foxes" under the hunters' power.
- **Plain sense (Cambridge, Ortlund):** a vineyard-watcher's ditty. Since the vineyard is her person (1:6; 8:12), the "foxes" may be anything, or anyone, that would spoil a young love before its time. Ortlund suggests she is teasing him that he is a "little fox" with his early invitation, which leads into (c).
- **Application bridge:** the plain sense (protect a budding love from things that spoil it) and the spiritual sense (guard the tender graces of a soul or church) run parallel without forcing either one.

### (c) 2:16-17: confident assurance, a prayer for his return, or a gentle "not yet"?
- **Durham, Gill, Spurgeon (No. 3185, No. 2477):** 2:16 is assured faith even while Christ's felt presence is absent. 2:17 prays for his speedy return "until the day break," meaning the gospel day, the resurrection or the Second Coming. Durham: the believer's relation to Christ "is the great ground of his happinefs and confolation, and not any fenfible prefence." Spurgeon calls 2:16 the happiest verse in the Bible, then sees "the lily... still among the thorns" in 2:17's shadows.
- **Hudson Taylor (surprise, dissenting):** reads 2:16-17 as backsliding complacency. She comforts herself with her secure position and "lightly dismisses Him... and the grieved Bridegroom departs!", which leads into the night search of 3:1-5. He then reads 2:16 → 6:3 → 7:10 as a progression of grace.
- **Ortlund (plain sense):** she pledges herself (2:16) and then gently sends him away (2:17). It is "not for lack of feeling," and it fits the refrain's "not yet."
- **Time of day:** "until the day breathes" may be dawn (JFB, Gill) or evening (Barnes, Ellicott, Keil-Delitzsch). The ambiguity suits a love that is real but not yet consummated.

**Also worth a note: speaker of 2:1.** Bernard, Henry and Benson say it is Christ: "I am the Rose of Sharon." Taylor, Cambridge, Ellicott, the ESV and nearly all modern commentators say it is the bride's modesty. Many hymns ("Jesus, Rose of Sharon") follow the older reading. Restraint here: we can say Christ is lovely without making 2:1 a direct self-description by Christ.

**Ordered love (2:4 LXX):** the patristic chain runs from Origen's Commentary (per Norris's footnote, Gregory's section is "a brisk paraphrase of Origen's much longer... discussion") to Gregory of Nyssa (Hom. 4), Augustine (City of God 15.22) and Bernard (Serm. 49-50). The ESV has "his banner over me was love," and the LXX reading is a different vocalization or reading of the Hebrew. Present it as how the church fathers heard the verse, not as the Hebrew text's meaning. It is still a rich and true doctrine: love God supremely and all else in due measure.

## Practical wisdom applications (for the host)
1. **Modesty and honor (2:1-2).** She calls herself a common wildflower; he calls her a lily among thorns. Love does not boast; it lifts up the other. (Bernard, Q7: whatever beauty we have, we received.)
2. **Unashamed, public love (2:4).** The banner is a public sign. Marriage is meant to be celebrated, not hidden. Spiritually, Christ is not ashamed to own his bride (Heb 2:11; Henry Q27, Poole Q30).
3. **Timing (2:7).** Do not rush or force romance or sexual intimacy. The refrain speaks to singles, dating couples and parents raising teens. It is not passivity (Ligonier Q56), and singleness carries no lesser worth.
4. **Ordered loves (2:4 LXX).** Love God supremely and everyone else in their proper measure. Augustine calls virtue "the order of love" (Q1). It is diagnostic: what do I love with my "entire soul and strength"? (Gregory Q2)
5. **Seasons (2:10-13).** Winters end (Henry Q28). There is hope for a marriage in a cold season and for a soul in spiritual winter (Gregory Q4).
6. **Little foxes (2:15).** Small neglects spoil young loves and young churches: unkind words, screens, secret compromises, small doctrinal drift. Deal with them early (Spurgeon Q36-37; Taylor Q48; Poole Q32). Guard "tender grapes," meaning young converts and new marriages (Gill Q34, Durham Q23). Correct error by argument and patience, not force (Bernard Q14, Henry Q25).
7. **Mutual belonging (2:16).** Covenant love is a mutual gift, not "I am my own" (Ortlund/Duguid Q60). His love comes first (Bernard Q16-17). Assurance rests on the covenant relation, not on feelings (Durham Q19, Q21).
8. **Waiting for the day (2:17).** The Christian life is lived "until the day breathes." This is longing, not despair (Spurgeon Q43-45).

## 1. Setting and background

**Where we are.** This is the second half of Jesus's long defense speech in Jerusalem (5:19-47). It follows the healing of the man lame 38 years at the pool of Bethesda on a Sabbath (5:1-9). The Jewish leaders want to kill him for two reasons: he "was breaking the Sabbath" and "was even calling God his own Father, making himself equal with God" (5:18). In 5:19-30 Jesus states his claim (the Son gives life and judges, and is to be honored as the Father is). In 5:31-47 he calls his witnesses, as in a law court. Then the tables turn: the defendant becomes the prosecutor, and Moses is named as the accuser.

- **Date and feast.** "A feast of the Jews" (5:1) is not named. Suggestions include Passover, Pentecost, Tabernacles, or Purim. The ministry date is roughly A.D. 28-31. John was "a burning and shining lamp" and "was" (past tense, 5:35), which suggests he was already in prison or dead (Ryle thinks he had "probably passed away").
- **Archaeology.** The pool of Bethesda with "five roofed colonnades" (5:2) was excavated near St. Anne's Church in Jerusalem: two pools with a central dividing wall, so four sides plus the middle make five porticoes. Critics once called John's detail invented. Good illustration for v. 46-47 ("believe his writings").
- **Legal background: Deuteronomy 19:15** (also 17:6): "Only on the evidence of two witnesses or of three witnesses shall a charge be established." The rabbis held that a man's testimony about himself was not admissible. Gill cites the Mishnah (Ketubot 2:9): "no man bears witness of himself." So Jesus calls more than two witnesses (John, the works, the Father, the Scriptures, with Moses as the last witness, for the prosecution).
- **5:31 vs. 8:14.** 5:31: "If I alone bear witness about myself, my testimony is not true." 8:14: "Even if I do bear witness about myself, my testimony is true, for I know where I came from and where I am going." See Interpretive Question 1.

**The four (or five) witnesses:**
1. John the Baptist (vv. 33-35): "a burning and shining lamp."
2. The works the Father gave him to accomplish (v. 36).
3. The Father himself (vv. 37-38). Is this the voice at the baptism, or the Father's witness in Scripture? Commentators split.
4. The Scriptures (vv. 39-40).
5. Moses, as accuser (vv. 45-47). He "wrote of me": Deut 18:15-19 ("The LORD your God will raise up for you a prophet like me from among you... it is to him you shall listen"), and also Gen 3:15, the Passover, the serpent, the tabernacle.

**Key Greek words (simple transliterations):**
- *martyria* / *martyreō*: testimony / to bear witness. The word appears about a dozen times in vv. 31-39. It is the theme word.
- *allos* (v. 32, "another"): another of the same kind. Many take it to mean the Father, not John.
- *lychnos* (v. 35): a lamp, an oil lamp that is lit and burns out. Compare 1:8: John "was not the light" (*phōs*). *kaiomenos kai phainōn* means "burning and shining." Augustine and Cyril both make much of the point that a lamp's light is borrowed.
- *pros hōran* (v. 35): "for an hour," "for a while." It points to their shallow, short-lived enthusiasm.
- *eraunate* (v. 39): "search/examine closely." The form is the same in the present indicative ("you search") and the present imperative ("search!"). Only context decides. KJV: "Search the Scriptures" (imperative). ESV/NIV/NASB: "You search" (indicative). See Question 2.
- *dokeite* (v. 39): "you think." They rightly think the Scriptures hold eternal life, but they miss whom they point to.
- *ou thelete elthein pros me* (v. 40): literally "you are not willing to come to me." ESV: "you refuse to come." The will (*thelō*) is the problem, not lack of evidence.
- *doxa* (vv. 41, 44): glory, honor, reputation. "Receive glory from one another" versus "the glory that comes from the only God" (*para tou monou theou*).
- *katēgoreō* (v. 45): accuse, to bring a charge. Moses, "on whom you have set your hope" (*ēlpikate*, perfect tense, a settled hope), is the prosecutor.
- *grammata / rhēmata* (v. 47): his writings / my words.

## 2. Outline with key verses

1. **The rule of evidence (5:31-32).** Self-testimony alone would not stand in court. "There is another who bears witness about me."
2. **Witness 1: John the Baptist (5:33-35).** "You sent to John" (1:19-27). Jesus does not need human testimony, "but I say these things so that you may be saved" (v. 34). "He was a burning and shining lamp, and you were willing to rejoice for a while in his light" (v. 35).
3. **Witness 2: the works (5:36).** "The very works that I am doing, bear witness about me that the Father has sent me."
4. **Witness 3: the Father (5:37-38).** "His voice you have never heard, his form you have never seen, and you do not have his word abiding in you."
5. **Witness 4: the Scriptures (5:39-40).** "You search the Scriptures because you think that in them you have eternal life; and it is they that bear witness about me, yet you refuse to come to me that you may have life."
6. **The diagnosis: the root of unbelief (5:41-44).** No love of God (v. 42). They reject the one who comes in the Father's name but will receive one who comes in his own (v. 43). "How can you believe, when you receive glory from one another and do not seek the glory that comes from the only God?" (v. 44).
7. **The prosecutor: Moses (5:45-47).** "There is one who accuses you: Moses, on whom you have set your hope. For if you believed Moses, you would believe me; for he wrote of me. But if you do not believe his writings, how will you believe my words?"

**Big idea for the episode:** Unbelief is not a lack of evidence but a problem of the will (v. 40) and of the heart's appetite for human approval (v. 44). The Bible-reader who does not come to Christ has missed the point of the Bible, and the Bible itself will testify against him.

## 3. Interpretive questions

### Q1. How can 5:31 ("my testimony is not true") square with 8:14 ("my testimony is true")?
- **Chrysostom (Homily 40):** Jesus speaks "with reference to their suspicions." It means "to you it is not true," words "addressed to their secret thoughts."
- **Calvin:** He speaks "by way of concession." He gives up his right "that he may convince his enemies by the authority of God."
- **Gill:** The point is legal. "'true' here, is not opposed to that which is 'false', but to that which is not valid in law." He ties this to Deut 19:15 and to rabbinic rules against self-witness. This is the clearest answer for listeners.
- **Poole** makes the same point: "he did not only testify of himself, his reputation did not stand upon his own single word."
- **Ryle** (Notes) warns that the words "must be qualified and restrained, or else one place of Scripture would contradict the other."
- *Synthesis:* The voices agree. 5:31 means "my self-testimony alone would not be legally valid in your eyes." In 8:14 Jesus asserts its intrinsic truth, because he knows his origin. Both are true.

### Q2. 5:39: "Search the Scriptures" (command) or "You search the Scriptures" (statement)?
This is the best "voices disagree" moment in the passage.
- **Imperative ("Search!"):** Chrysostom, Augustine, Calvin, Ryle (and the KJV). Chrysostom: "He said not, Read the Scriptures, but, Search the Scriptures." Calvin: "Christ enjoins us to search diligently for this hidden treasure." Ryle "decidedly prefer[s]" the imperative, because it is "more forcible" and fits the context. Spurgeon (Morning and Evening, June 9 PM) preaches it as a command.
- **Indicative ("You search"):** Cyril of Alexandria argues explicitly against the "smooth... and beaten explanation." He would "read it not imperatively." His reason: the "and" (or "yet") that joins "you will not come to me" makes sense only if Jesus is describing what they already do. Gill allows the indicative. MacArthur preaches it as present-tense fact ("This is what you do. This is your life"). The ESV, NIV and NASB translate it this way.
- **Poole** sits on the fence: "the words may be read either imperatively... or indicatively."
- **Ryle's catalogue** (Notes on 5:39) lists the indicative party (Cyril, Erasmus, Beza, Poole, Lightfoot, Bengel...) and the imperative party (Chrysostom, Augustine, Luther, Calvin, Rollock, Whitby, Alford...). Even the Reformers split: Beza (indicative) versus Calvin (imperative).
- *Pastoral point:* Either way the lesson holds. If indicative, it is a warning: you can study the Bible intensely and miss Christ. If imperative, it is a summons: search, because they testify of me. Both readings agree that the Scriptures' purpose is to bring us to Christ.

### Q3. What really keeps people from coming to Christ (5:40, 5:44)? Will, inability, or appetite for glory?
- **Spurgeon, "Free-Will, a Slave" (NPSP No. 52, Dec. 2, 1855):** He calls the verse "one of the great guns of the Arminians" and turns it around. The verse is about will, not ability, and it shows that "by nature no man will come to Christ." Yet the unwillingness is sin and guilt, not an excuse: "We do not preach this doctrine to excuse you, but to humble you."
- **Ryle:** Lost souls are lost by "want of will to come to Christ." It is not their sins and not any decree. *Caution for a Westminster host:* Ryle adds, "It is not any limit in Christ's work of redemption. He has paid a price sufficient for all mankind." That is the sufficiency side of the sufficiency/efficiency distinction, but stated in a way some Reformed listeners will notice. Use the will part; handle the atonement line with care, or leave it out.
- **Calvin** on v. 44: "the gate of faith is shut against all whose hearts are preoccupied by a vain desire of earthly glory."
- **Ryle on v. 44:** the man who loves human praise is "like a child sitting on the lid of his box, and wishing to open it, but not considering that his own weight keeps it shut." This is the best illustration in the file.
- **Cyril and Chrysostom** both read v. 43's "another... in his own name" as Antichrist. Calvin applies it to false teachers and the Pope.
- *Surprise:* MacArthur takes "the glory that comes from the only God" (v. 44) as a reference to Christ himself: "I wish the translators had capitalized the Glory." It is an unusual reading, worth mentioning as such and not endorsing.

### (Bonus) Who is "the other" witness (v. 32), and how did the Father witness (v. 37)?
- Ryle's notes list two camps for v. 32. One holds that the "other witness" is John the Baptist (Chrysostom, Lightfoot, Gill...). The other holds that it is the Father (Cyril, Athanasius, Calvin, Beza, Poole, Henry, Bengel...).
- On v. 37, Chrysostom thinks of the voice at the Jordan. Calvin rejects limiting it to the baptism. He takes it as the Father's witness through the Old Testament, so that v. 37 leads into v. 39. Ryle "decidedly prefer[s]" Calvin's view. Poole takes the baptism and transfiguration view.

## 1. Setting and background

- **Author, place and date.** Simon Peter (1:1), writing "through Silvanus" (5:12) from "Babylon" (5:13), almost certainly a code name for Rome. The usual date is c. A.D. 62-64, under **Nero** (emperor 54-68). It was written before or just around the great fire of Rome (July 64) and Nero's persecution that followed. Tradition (1 Clement 5; Eusebius) places Peter's martyrdom in Rome under Nero, c. 64-67. So "Honor the emperor" (2:17) was written about a ruler who would soon kill the writer. MacArthur and Gill both make this point.
- **Recipients.** "Elect exiles of the dispersion" in five Roman provinces of Asia Minor: Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia, Bithynia (1:1). Were they Jewish Christians or mixed churches? Calvin and Poole read them mainly as believing Jews of the dispersion, which is why they say Peter gives the old titles of Israel back to them. Most modern commentators note that 1:14, 1:18 ("futile ways inherited from your forefathers") and 4:3-4 point to many Gentile converts. Either way, Israel's titles now belong to the whole church in Christ.
- **Structure of the letter.** 1:1-2:10 covers identity: new birth, a living hope, living stones, a chosen people. 2:11-4:11 covers conduct as exiles: honorable behavior, submission in the state, the household and marriage, suffering for doing good. 4:12-5:14 covers fiery trial, elders, and standing firm. Our passage is the hinge: 2:9-10 sums up the identity section, and 2:11-12 heads the conduct section. 2:13-17 is the first application (the state), followed by slaves (2:18-25) and wives and husbands (3:1-7).

**Old Testament background for 2:9-10:**
- **Exodus 19:5-6** (at Sinai, before the law): "you shall be my treasured possession among all peoples... a kingdom of priests and a holy nation." LXX: *laos periousios*, *basileion hierateuma*, *ethnos hagion*.
- **Isaiah 43:20-21** (the new exodus): "my chosen people (*to genos mou to eklekton*), the people whom I formed for myself that they might declare my praise." LXX Isa 43:21 uses *periepoiēsamēn* ("I acquired") and *tas aretas mou diēgeisthai* ("to recount my excellencies"). Peter's "a people for his own possession, that you may proclaim the excellencies" stitches Exodus 19 and Isaiah 43 together.
- **Hosea 1:6, 9; 2:23.** Hosea's children were named *Lo-ruhamah* ("No Mercy") and *Lo-ammi* ("Not My People"), with the promised reversal: "I will have mercy on No Mercy, and I will say to Not My People, 'You are my people.'" Paul applies the same text to Gentiles in Rom 9:25-26. Calvin and the CCEL editor note that Peter follows the Hebrew and Paul the LXX.

**Key Greek words (simple transliterations):**
- *genos eklekton* (chosen race), *basileion hierateuma* (royal priesthood; Calvin notes Peter inverts Moses' "priestly kingdom"), *ethnos hagion* (holy nation), *laos eis peripoiēsin* (a people for [God's] acquisition/possession; KJV "peculiar people," from the Latin *peculium*, private property, not "odd").
- *exangeilēte* (v. 9): "proclaim, tell out." It appears only here in the NT but is found in the LXX (e.g., Pss 9:14; 79:13 LXX). MacArthur overstates when he says it "appears nowhere else in the entire Bible."
- *aretas* (v. 9): "excellencies, virtues, mighty deeds." Calvin: "wisdom, goodness, power, righteousness." Poole likewise. MacArthur stresses "heroic deeds."
- *paroikous kai parepidēmous* (v. 11): "sojourners and exiles." *Paroikos* means a resident alien without citizen rights. *Parepidēmos* means a temporary visitor passing through. Genesis 23:4 LXX: Abraham says "I am a *paroikos kai parepidēmos* among you" (as he buys a grave). Also Ps 39:12; Heb 11:13.
- *sarkikōn epithymiōn... strateuontai kata tēs psychēs* (v. 11): "fleshly desires... which wage war (as soldiers on campaign) against the soul."
- *anastrophē kalē* (v. 12): "good, beautiful, honorable way of life." The CCEL editor's note on Calvin says "conversation" (KJV) means conduct.
- *hēmera episkopēs* (v. 12): "day of visitation." Compare Isa 10:3 (judgment) and Luke 19:44 (gracious visitation). Calvin and Poole read it as God's gracious visit in conversion, and Calvin notes others take it as the last day.
- *hypotagēte* (v. 13): "be subject" (aorist imperative). *Pasē anthrōpinē ktisei*: literally "to every human creation." Calvin explains *ktisis* as "ordination," a building God has framed. ESV note: "every institution ordained for people."
- *basilei hōs hyperechonti* (v. 13): "the king/emperor as supreme." Greeks called the Caesar *basileus*, as Calvin notes.
- *phimoun* (v. 15): "to muzzle, silence," as in muzzling an ox. The CCEL editor on Calvin: "The word properly means to muzzle." *Agnōsian*: ignorance.
- *eleutheroi... epikalymma tēs kakias... theou douloi* (v. 16): free people, not using freedom as a "cover-up/veil" for evil, but as God's bond-slaves.
- **v. 17, four commands.** *Pantas timēsate* (aorist), *tēn adelphotēta agapate* (present), *ton theon phobeisthe* (present), *ton basilea timate* (present). **Key point:** Prov 24:21 LXX reads "Fear God, my son, and the king." Peter changes it. God alone is feared, and the emperor gets the same "honor" as "everyone." The verse brackets the emperor with "all people," not with God. Calvin, Poole and Gill all note that "fear God" is the hinge or spring of the other duties.

## 2. Outline with key verses

**A. Who you are: the people of God (2:9-10)**
1. Four titles from Sinai and Isaiah: "a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, a people for his own possession."
2. The purpose: "that you may proclaim the excellencies of him who called you out of darkness into his marvelous light."
3. The reversal: "Once you were not a people, but now you are God's people; once you had not received mercy, but now you have received mercy" (Hosea).

**B. How you live: as sojourners and exiles (2:11-12)**
1. Inward: "abstain from the passions of the flesh, which wage war against your soul."
2. Outward: "Keep your conduct among the Gentiles honorable," so that slanderers "may see your good deeds and glorify God on the day of visitation."

**C. How you live under rulers: free servants of God (2:13-17)**
1. The command and motive: "Be subject for the Lord's sake to every human institution."
2. The scope: "the emperor as supreme... governors as sent by him."
3. The purpose of government: "to punish those who do evil and to praise those who do good."
4. The aim: "by doing good you should put to silence the ignorance of foolish people."
5. The kind of freedom: "Live as people who are free, not using your freedom as a cover-up for evil, but living as servants of God."
6. The summary: "Honor everyone. Love the brotherhood. Fear God. Honor the emperor."

**Big idea:** Your identity (v. 9-10) shapes your conduct (v. 11-12), and that conduct includes your citizenship (v. 13-17). Royal priests who belong to heaven make the best citizens on earth, because they fear God and so can honor everyone, the emperor included, without fearing any man.

## 3. Interpretive questions

### Q1. Do the titles of Israel now belong to the church? In what sense is every believer a priest?
- **Calvin:** the titles that Moses gave the fathers "do now far more justly belong to you." He also says "ye are, each of you, consecrated in Christ, that ye may be the associates of his kingdom, and partakers of his priesthood." (Calvin reads the recipients as mainly believing Jews, but he extends the titles to all nations: "after the wall of partition has been pulled down by Christ, we are now gathered from every nation.")
- **Augustine (City of God 20.10):** all believers are called priests "because they are members of the one Priest." Priesthood is derived from union with Christ, not held independently. This is a strong patristic grounding for the "priesthood of all believers."
- **Ligonier devotional ("God's Royal Priesthood," 2017; unsigned) and Luther:** the Reformers did not reject the distinction between clergy and laity. They rejected the idea that "clergy are closer to God than the laity," so every calling becomes priestly service. It quotes Luther's *Address to the Nobility*.
- **Poole:** royal and priest. Believers are "spiritual kings" over their enemies, with a kingdom still to inherit.
- *For a PCA host:* WCF 25 (the church) and LC 158 (used last time on 2:5, so avoid it) are background. Augustine's "members of the one Priest" guards against reading the priesthood of believers as individualism.

### Q2. Must Christians obey a Nero? What are the limits of "be subject... to every human institution"?
- **Calvin** pushes subjection very far: "government established by God ought to be so highly valued by us, as to honor even tyrants when in power." He adds that "some kind of government, however deformed and corrupt it may be, is still better and more beneficial than anarchy." Calvin does elsewhere set limits (Institutes 4.20.32, obey God rather than men; not fetched here, so not quoted).
- **WCF 23.4** (American revision, OPC text): duty "to obey their lawful commands, and to be subject to their authority, for conscience' sake," and "Infidelity, or difference in religion, doth not make void the magistrates' just and legal authority." The word "lawful" is the limit.
- **Tertullian, To Scapula 2** (c. A.D. 212): Christians render "such reverential homage as is lawful for us and good for him." Honor, but within limits.
- **Justin Martyr, First Apology 17:** "to God alone we render worship, but in other things we gladly serve you." This is the patristic version of "Fear God. Honor the emperor."
- **Poole on "Fear God":** it shows "the due bounds of all the offices we perform to men, that nothing is to be done for them which is inconsistent with the fear of God."
- **MacArthur** (1980s sermons, "Submission to Civil Authority" 1-2) argues strongly against civil disobedience by Christians in protest movements. His example is abortion-clinic "rescue" blockades. He says "We are never commanded to do that." Contrast this with the limiting language of Poole, Tertullian and the WCF ("lawful commands"). *Note:* MacArthur's own church famously resisted COVID-era closure orders in 2020. That is not documented in our sources, so mention it only carefully if at all.
- **Gill:** "honour the king; Caesar, the Roman emperor, though a wicked, persecuting Nero."
- *Synthesis:* All agree: honor is owed even to a bad ruler, because the office is God's ordinance (Calvin's marriage analogy: abuse doesn't overturn the institution). Where they differ is emphasis. The confessional and patristic voices carefully specify "lawful," "as is lawful," and "inconsistent with the fear of God." The structure of v. 17 itself (fear for God, honor for the king) supplies the limit.

### Q3. What is "the day of visitation" (2:12), and how does good conduct "silence" slander (2:15)?
- **Calvin:** "I know that some refer this to the last coming of Christ; but I take it otherwise." He takes it as God's gracious visit when he converts unbelievers. Their holy lives are "a preparation, to bring back the wandering to the right way."
- **Poole:** "a gracious visitation, when God calls them by the gospel." He cites Luke 1:68, 78 and Luke 19:44.
- **Henry's commentary** (the 1 Peter portion): "There is a day of visitation coming, wherein God may call them by his word and his grace to repentance."
- **Gill** gives both options, a human visitation (magistrates examining Christians) or a divine one.
- **Polycarp (To the Philippians 10)** quotes 2:12 almost verbatim: "having your conduct blameless among the Gentiles... that the Lord may not be blasphemed through you." This is early evidence (c. A.D. 110-135) that the church used this verse.
- **Epistle to Diognetus 5** (2nd c.) describes the fulfilment: "they are reviled, and bless... they do good, yet are punished as evil-doers," and it quotes 1 Pet 2:11 in ch. 6.
- **Ferguson (Tabletalk 2026):** Christians have a different "accent." Outsiders will want to start the conversation. Peter assumes our lives will provoke the question.
