# EZEKIEL 9-12 (Old Testament)

## Setting and background
- Same vision as ch. 8: dated 8:1, sixth year, sixth month, fifth day = late summer 592 B.C., ~14 months after the call (593). Zedekiah reigns in Jerusalem (his 9-year-later rebellion leads to siege 588-586). Ezekiel sits in his house at Tel-abib with the elders; the vision carries him to Jerusalem (8:3) and returns him to Chaldea at 11:24; 11:25 he tells the exiles everything. Ch. 12 is new: a sign-act (baggage, digging through wall) explained as Zedekiah's flight (fulfilled 586: 2 Kings 25:4-7; Jeremiah 39:4-7; 52:7-11 — sons killed before his eyes at Riblah, eyes put out, taken to Babylon in fetters, died there).
- Book structure / glory arc: glory arrives (1-3), departs (8-11), Jerusalem falls (33:21), glory returns (43:1-5) by the east gate, 48:35 "The Lord is there". The departure is in stages: 9:3 from the cherub to the threshold; 10:4 again threshold, house filled with cloud; 10:18-19 from threshold to over the cherubim, stand at east gate; 11:22-23 from midst of the city to "the mountain on the east side" (Mount of Olives per Targum, Jarchi, Kimchi via Gill; Keil).
- Gill reports a rabbinic tradition (Talmud, Rosh Hashanah 31a) that the Shekinah made ten removes, ending in the wilderness and ascending to its place (Hosea 5:15). URL https://biblehub.com/commentaries/ezekiel/11-23.htm
- 9:4 "mark" = Hebrew tav (H8420), the last letter of the alphabet; in the old Hebrew script written like X or + (Ellicott, Cambridge, Keil, Pulpit). Used as a signature mark (Job 31:35 "here is my signature" = tav). Tertullian, Origen, Cyprian, Jerome saw the cross (Ellicott, Pulpit). Poole and Gill resist making it the cross; Keil: not a direct prophecy, but "something remarkable in this coincidence" (Schmieder) and Revelation 7:3 counterpart. Exodus 12 blood on doorposts by families; here foreheads by individuals (JFB, Poole).
- JFB on 9:4 ties "sigh and cry" to Lot, 2 Peter 2:7-8 (cross-reading connection!). Henry also: "and Lot, who vexed his righteous soul with the wicked conversation of his neighbours."
- 9:6 "begin at my sanctuary" quoted in 1 Peter 4:17 thread (episode 11 already linked). Do not reuse at length.
- 9:9 repeats 8:12 "The Lord has forsaken the land, the Lord does not see" — yesterday's line now quoted back by God as the charge.
- 9:8 and 11:13 two "Ah, Lord God!" intercessions (cf. 4:14 yesterday). Pelatiah = "Yahweh has delivered/rescued" (Strong's H6410, https://biblehub.com/hebrew/6410.htm); son of Benaiah ("the Lord builds"); dies while Ezekiel prophesies; Ezekiel: "Will you make a full end of the remnant (she'erit)?" Irony of the name. Jaazaniah son of Azzur, distinct from 8:11 son of Shaphan (Henry, Keil).
- 11:3 "The time is not near to build houses" (ESV) / KJV "It is not near; let us build houses" — Calvin: mocks Jeremiah 29:5 (build houses in Babylon) and Jeremiah 1:13 boiling pot. "This city is the cauldron and we are the meat": the choice cuts safe inside. God reverses: the slain are the meat; you will be brought out and judged "at the border of Israel" (11:10-11) — fulfilled at Riblah (2 Kings 25:20-21; Jeremiah 52:9-10, 24-27), in the land of Hamath at Israel's northern border.
- 11:15 "your brothers, even your brothers, your kinsmen" — Hebrew anshe geullateka, "men of your redemption" (Henry: "the men of thy redemption, so the word is", the kinsman-redeemer). Jerusalemites: "Go far from the Lord; to us this land is given" (cf. Jeremiah 24 good and bad figs).
- 11:16 "I have been a sanctuary to them for a while" (ESV; footnote "or in a small measure"); KJV "as a little sanctuary"; Hebrew miqdash me'at. Targum: "I have given them synagogues, second to my sanctuary" (Gill: Targum, Jarchi, Kimchi interpret it of synagogues). Rabbinic "mikdash me'at" = synagogue tradition (Talmud Megillah 29a; not fetched, mention only via Gill's Targum note).
- 11:19-20 new heart: parallels Jeremiah 31:33, 32:39 ("one heart"), Ezekiel 36:26-27. Calvin: "one heart" opposed to a divided heart, not mutual consent. Westminster Confession 10.1 (effectual calling: "taking away their heart of stone, and giving unto them an heart of flesh") — WCF 10.1 language is from Ezekiel 36:26 / 11:19. [Confession text: well known; quote only the phrase "taking away their heart of stone, and giving unto them an heart of flesh" — verify? Use paraphrase if not fetched.]
- Ch. 12: sign-act number 2 of the book's baggage (exile's baggage, dig through the wall — echoes 8:8 "dig in the wall"!). 12:2 "eyes to see but see not" (Isaiah 6:9-10; Jeremiah 5:21). 12:3 "Perhaps they will understand" (ulay). 12:12-13 the prince covers his face, "he shall not see it, and he shall die there" — Zedekiah blinded at Riblah (2 Kings 25:7). Josephus says Zedekiah disbelieved because Ezekiel and Jeremiah seemed to contradict (Jeremiah 32:4; 34:3 "your eyes shall see the eyes of the king of Babylon" vs Ezekiel "he shall not see it"). Both true.
- 12:17-20 eat bread with quaking. 12:21-28 two proverbs: "The days grow long, and every vision comes to nothing" (unbelief) and "he prophesies of times far off" (procrastination). God: "None of my words will be delayed any longer." Calvin connects to Noah (Hebrews 11:7). Connects to 2 Peter 2:3 "their condemnation from long ago is not idle, and their destruction is not asleep" and 2 Peter 3:4, 9 (scoffers, delay) — real connection for close.

## Interpretive questions
1. Who is the man clothed in linen? Henry, Poole: Christ the Mediator (Poole on 9:4 "i.e. to Christ"). Calvin: an angel ("he calls the angel a man, but on account of the form which he put on"), with a double office: protector, then burns the city (10:2). Keil/Pulpit: angel. Linen = priestly garment (Leviticus 16:4), Daniel 10:5, 12:6-7. Host: hold it with Calvin as the angel of the Lord's mercy; Henry's application stands either way — mercy marks before judgment strikes.
2. Did the mark really spare all the godly? Calvin raises exactly this: Jeremiah dragged to Egypt, many wicked spared (Jeremiah 39:10). Answer: "he does not mean them to be free from all injury... but he promises that they should be so separated from the ungodly, that they should acknowledge by sure experience that God was never forgetful of his faith and promise." Henry is more confident (Jeremiah, Baruch, Ebed-melech; Pella). Host: side with Calvin's honesty — the mark is not immunity from suffering but ownership.
3. Is the tav the cross? Tertullian yes; Poole "too curious, and as useless"; Gill no; Keil remarkable coincidence. Host: Ezekiel's hearers could not know it; it's a mark of ownership, ultimately fulfilled when God seals his servants (Revelation 7:3, which alludes to this passage = warranted NT move); enjoy the coincidence, don't build on it.
4. How can God leave his temple when he promised to dwell there forever (Psalm 132:14)? Calvin on 10:18-19 and 11:22-23: interruption, not abolition; the temple stood before God on the promise; Daniel prayed toward it; Haggai 2:9.
5. Why does God answer "brothers, brothers" to Ezekiel's 11:13 cry? Calvin: God rebukes the prophet for measuring the church by Jerusalem's elite; the remnant is among the despised exiles.

## 1. Setting

- **Text:** ESV Job 3:1-26, heading "Job Laments His Birth", read in full at https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Job+3&version=ESV.
- **Where it falls in the book:** Chapters 1-2 are prose. Job has twice blessed God or submitted to Him: 1:21 and 2:10 ("Shall we receive good from God, and shall we not receive evil?" ... "In all this Job did not sin with his lips"). His three friends then sit with him "seven days and seven nights" in silence (2:13). Chapter 3 is the first poetry in the book. It is a soliloquy: Job does not address the friends or God. The dialogue proper begins with Eliphaz in ch. 4.
- **Narrative hinge:** Satan predicted that Job would "curse" God to His face (1:11; 2:5), and Job's wife urged "Curse God and die" (2:9). Chapter 3 opens with Job opening his mouth and cursing (3:1), but he curses *his day*, not God, and the Hebrew verb is a different one (see Hebrew words below).
- **ESV key verses (verbatim):**
  - 3:1 "After this Job opened his mouth and cursed the day of his birth."
  - 3:3 "Let the day perish on which I was born, and the night that said, 'A man is conceived.'"
  - 3:8 "Let those curse it who curse the day, who are ready to rouse up Leviathan."
  - 3:11 "Why did I not die at birth, come out from the womb and expire?"
  - 3:17 "There the wicked cease from troubling, and there the weary are at rest."
  - 3:19 "The small and the great are there, and the slave is free from his master."
  - 3:20 "Why is light given to him who is in misery, and life to the bitter in soul,"
  - 3:23 "Why is light given to a man whose way is hidden, whom God has hedged in?"
  - 3:25 "For the thing that I fear comes upon me, and what I dread befalls me."
  - 3:26 "I am not at ease, nor am I quiet; I have no rest, but trouble comes."
  - (ESV footnote at 3:24: "instead of my bread" can also be read "like my bread".)

## 2. Outline

1. **3:1-2: Introduction (prose).** Job breaks the seven-day silence: "opened his mouth and cursed the day of his birth."
2. **3:3-10: Curse on the day and the night.**
   - 3:3 introduces both: the day of birth and the night of conception ("A man is conceived").
   - 3:4-5 curse the day: "Let that day be darkness!", which reverses "Let there be light" in Gen 1:3. Also "deep darkness" (*tsalmaveth*).
   - 3:6-9 curse the night: let it be barren, cut from the calendar, cursed by "those ... ready to rouse up Leviathan", with no dawn ("the eyelids of the morning").
   - 3:10 gives the reason: it "did not shut the doors of my mother's womb, nor hide trouble [*'amal*] from my eyes."
3. **3:11-19: Why did I not die at birth? Sheol as rest.**
   - 3:11-12 asks why he was not stillborn, and why "the knees" and "the breasts" received him.
   - 3:13-15: he would have "lain down and been quiet ... at rest" with kings, counselors and princes.
   - 3:16 wishes he had been a "hidden stillborn child."
   - 3:17-19 describe the leveling of the grave: the wicked cease from "troubling" (*rogez*), the weary rest, prisoners are at ease, there is no taskmaster, "the small and the great are there", and the slave is free.
4. **3:20-26: Why is light given to the miserable?**
   - 3:20-22 widen the question to all "the bitter in soul" (plural) who long for death and "dig for it more than for hidden treasures."
   - 3:23 brings it back to Job himself: "whose way is hidden, whom God has hedged in." This is the first time God (*Eloah*) is named as the actor in the speech.
   - 3:24 describes sighing in place of bread and groanings "poured out like water."
   - 3:25 "the thing that I fear comes upon me."
   - 3:26 ends on unrest: "no rest, but trouble [*rogez*] comes."
- **Structural notes:**
  - *rogez* appears in 3:17 and again as the final word of 3:26, framing the chapter's end. The grave is pictured as the place where *rogez* ceases, while Job's life is the place where *rogez* comes.
  - The verbs of rest in 3:13 (*shaqat* "be quiet", *nuach* "rest") come back negated in 3:26 (*lo shaqatti ... lo nachti*).
  - Christopher Ash (TGC) notes the dominant "Why?" (3:11, 16, 20, 23).

## 4. Interpretive questions

### (a) Did Job sin in chapter 3, given 2:10 ("In all this Job did not sin with his lips")?

The narrator's "did not sin" verdict is given at 2:10 and is **not repeated** after ch. 3. The older Reformed voices are close to unanimous on a "mixed" verdict: there is real infirmity and sin in the words, but no blasphemy and no apostasy. Satan loses his wager.

- **Calvin** (Sermon 11, Golding tr. 1574) holds the middle line explicitly. Some excuse Job altogether, and others say he is "altogither inraged". Calvin says: "it is certayne that Iob is not come too suche extremitie ... Neuerthelesse, there is a mixture in him". Words "scape him whiche are euill ... and cannot be iustified", "howbeit that his intent is not to fall out with God."
- **Poole:** "In vain do some men endeavour to excuse this and the following speeches of Job, who afterwards is reproved by God and severely accuseth himself for them, Job 38:2 40:4 42:3,6." The curse was sinful because it was vain, aimed at a creature, and cast blame on God. Yet "he doth not proceed so far as to curse or blaspheme God, but makes the devil a liar in his prognostics."
- **Trapp:** "Diem, non Deum; his day, and not his God". He defends Job against the Jewish charge that he was damned (compare Jeremiah), citing James 5:11 and 42:7-8, but adds: "It was too much howsoever of that". He also cites Bucholcer: "pain and grief speaketh rather than the man himself".
- **Gill:** Job cursed neither God, nor his friends, nor himself. He has "a mixture of weakness", "not to be excused of weakness and infirmity, since he is blamed not only by Elihu, but by the Lord himself", and he "owned his sin and folly, and repented of it, Job 40:4".
- **Caryl:** cursing a creature *as God's work* would be blasphemy, and cursing an insensible thing in itself is "unreasonable". Job's cursing is the second kind.
- **Gregory the Great** is the outlier. He reads Job's curse as a righteous, judicial pronouncement: "his cursing cometh not of the malice of one guilty of sin, but of the integrity of a judge ... of one sober in instruction". He also reads it allegorically, as a curse on the devil and the "day" of sin.
- **Derek Thomas** (2000) gives the pastoral caution: "Before we rush into accusation of sin on Job's part, we need to pause and reflect a little."
- **Ash (TGC)** frames the whole speech-cycle with two truths in tension: God says Job "has spoken rightly" (42:7), yet Job admits he spoke "without knowledge" (42:3).

### (b) Is cursing the day the same as cursing God?

No, not formally. The verb is qalal, not the *barak*-euphemism of 1:11/2:5/2:9. Caryl and Gill both make this lexical point (quote bank). Caryl's distinction is that to curse a creature "under the notion of a creature, or as it is the worke of God, is to blaspheme God". Job curses his day, not God's work as God's.

Poole sees "secret and indirect reflections upon God's providence". The question in 3:23 ("whom God has hedged in") is where the speech comes closest to accusing God.

### (c) Comparison to Jeremiah 20:14-18

ESV Jer 20:14-18 reads: "Cursed be the day on which I was born! ... Cursed be the man who brought the news to my father, 'A son is born to you,' ... because he did not kill me in the womb; so my mother would have been my grave ... Why did I come out from the womb to see toil and sorrow, and spend my days in shame?"

- **Verbal parallels:**
  - The cursed birthday: Job 3:3 / Jer 20:14.
  - The announcement "A man is conceived" / "A son is born to you": Job 3:3 / Jer 20:15.
  - The womb that did not close or kill: Job 3:10-11 / Jer 20:17.
  - The "why" of coming out of the womb to see trouble or toil: Job 3:10-11, 20 / Jer 20:18. Job's 'amal is Jeremiah's "toil" (also 'amal).
- **Differences:**
  - Jeremiah uses *arar* ("arur be the day"). Job uses qalal in 3:1 and *arar* only for the professional cursers in 3:8.
  - Jeremiah also curses a *person*, the messenger. Job does not.
  - Jeremiah's lament comes right after a burst of praise (20:13). Job's comes after seven days' silence.
- **Commentators who link them:** Poole on 3:1 and 3:3; Trapp on 3:1 (the rabbis call Job damned, "But why do they not then say the same of Jeremiah ... for cursing his birthday too?"); Caryl, who quotes Jer 20:14-18 in full and notes Jeremiah shows the curse was not "against the day for it selfe"; and Derek Thomas ("Jeremiah used almost identical language to depict his own Dark Night of the Soul").
- **Pastoral point:** two of Scripture's most faithful men said the same thing. That argues against reading Job 3 as apostasy, and it also shows it is not a pattern to imitate.

### (d) Does Job wish suicide?

No. He wishes he had never been born (3:3-10), or had died at birth (3:11-16), and he longs for death as for treasure (3:21-22). But he never proposes to take his life. The death-wish is put to God as a question ("Why is light given...?"), not as a plan.

- **Poole:** "Job durst not lay violent hands upon himself ... he was contented to wait all the days of his appointed time" (Job 14:14).
- **Trapp:** sets Job against Seneca's praise of suicide: "we have not so learned Christ, neither may we leave our station till called for by our Captain".
- **Gill:** such desires "should not be desired merely to be rid of present afflictions."
- **Henry (Concise):** to desire death only to escape the troubles of this life "savours of corruption".
- **Spurgeon (No. 2666 on 3:23):** answers the sufferer's question "Why do I continue to live?" with "God wills it" for the believer, and for the unconverted, "Because of God's mercy to you". In other words, life is prolonged so that you may repent.
- **Pastoral flag for the episode:** the text gives words to someone who wishes they had never been born. It neither endorses self-harm nor shames the lament. A brief, gentle line pointing listeners in crisis toward their pastor or emergency help may be appropriate.

### (e) How the voices answer, in brief

- **Infirmity, not apostasy:** Calvin, Poole, Trapp, Gill, Henry (Concise), Hall ("weakly cursed"). Several use the image of a soldier wounded but not defeated (Calvin: "he staggereth, he steppeth awrie").
- **Righteous and medicinal speech:** Gregory. Job curses as a judge, and his outburst is a "healing medicine" for the friends, who were themselves despairing of inward soundness.
- **Honest lament God permits:** Derek Thomas, Ash. Spurgeon is gentle too ("we will not condemn him") while warning that it is dangerous to begin "catechising God".
- **Christological turn:** Henry (Concise) calls Job "an especial type of Christ" in his inward sufferings. Thomas points to Gethsemane (Mark 14:33-34, citing Warfield). Ash points to the Christ who "suffered and died alone".
- **Patristic note:** Ishodad of Merv (via the SermonIndex ECF catena) says God "does not expect insensitivity on our part", but expects us not to "abandon ourselves to blasphemous words".

### (f) Ecclesiastes connections

ESV wording, fetched from Bible Gateway:
- **Eccl 4:2-3:** "I thought the dead who are already dead more fortunate than the living who are still alive. But better than both is he who has not yet been and has not seen the evil deeds that are done under the sun." This is the same "better not to have been" logic as Job 3:3-16.
- **Eccl 6:3-5:** "a stillborn child is better off than he. For it comes in vanity and goes in darkness, and in darkness its name is covered. Moreover, it has not seen the sun or known anything, yet it finds rest rather than he." This is very close to Job 3:16 ("as a hidden stillborn child, as infants who never see the light"). It shares the vocabulary of not seeing the light or sun, and of *rest* (Job 3:13, 17).
- **Eccl 7:1:** "the day of death than the day of birth." This is the sober wisdom form of what Job says in anguish.
- **Difference in tone:** Ecclesiastes says these things as a detached observer "under the sun". Job says them in personal agony.
- **The leveling of death:** Job 3:14-19 (kings and slaves alike) parallels Ecclesiastes' theme that one fate comes to all (e.g., Eccl 2:14-16, 9:2-3; not fetched this session, cited from the canon only).
- **Pastoral note:** Trapp (on 3:13) answers Job's idealized "rest" with Hezekiah, Isa 38:16 ("Life consists in action"). Matthew Henry's Concise line, "willing to die ... willing to live", sums up the balance.

## 1. Setting

**ESV text** (Bible Gateway, https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John+7:53-8:11&version=ESV)

**Time and place**
- **Feast of Tabernacles.** Chapter 7 is set at the Feast of Booths, and 7:37 is "the last day of the feast, the great day." John Lightfoot (Horae Hebraicae on John 8, below) reads 7:53 "They went each to his own house" as the end of the feast. After the first night, pilgrims could lodge in booths outside the walls within a sabbath day's journey. He says Jesus may have gone "to the town of Bethany,—or to some booth fixed in that mount,—is uncertain."
- **Mount of Olives.** A Bible Gateway ESV search for "Mount of Olives" finds **one** hit in John: John 8:1. (John 11:18 appears only in the Nave's Topical Bible side results, which describe Bethany as on the eastern slope of the Mount of Olives. The verse itself does not use the phrase.) This is the only place John's Gospel names the Mount of Olives. That is one of the "non-Johannine" features commentators point to. Search URL: https://www.biblegateway.com/quicksearch/?quicksearch=%22Mount+of+Olives%22&version=ESV
  - Compare **Luke 21:37-38 (ESV)**: "And every day he was teaching in the temple, but at night he went out and lodged on the mount called Olivet. And early in the morning all the people came to him in the temple to hear him." The wording is very close to John 8:1-2, which is why the f13 family of manuscripts places the story after Luke 21:38.
  - Bengel (Gnomon, below) ties the Mount of Olives to Gethsemane: Jesus went "to that mountain, in which they were afterwards about to take Him; ch. John 18:2."
- **Early morning, in the temple, seated to teach** (8:2). Sitting was the rabbinic teaching posture (ESV cross-refs Matt. 5:1; Luke 4:20). Bengel: "ἐδίδασκεν, He was teaching) On this account His interrupters were the more intrusive." In 8:20 the next discourse takes place "in the treasury" (Ambrose's Letter XXVI draws on this; see quote bank).
- **Who carried the stones / the witnesses' role.** Deut. 17:7 (ESV): "The hand of the witnesses shall be first against him to put him to death, and afterward the hand of all the people." The accusers were the witnesses, so under the law they would have to throw first. Calvin, Henry, Bengel and Sproul all make this point (see below). Bengel: "The witnesses were wont to be the first in the act of stoning. [Hence the expression is τὸν λίθον, with the article.—V. g.]" Henry links it to Stephen's stoning (Acts 7:58).
- **Roman restriction on capital punishment.** John 18:31 (ESV): "The Jews said to him, 'It is not lawful for us to put anyone to death.'" Sproul builds the "Rome horn" of the dilemma on this (see Interpretive questions).

## 4. Interpretive questions

### (a) What was the trap?
- **The law**: Lev. 20:10 (ESV) says "both the adulterer and the adulteress shall surely be put to death." Deut. 22:22 says "both of them shall die, the man who lay with the woman, and the woman." Deut. 22:23-24 prescribes stoning specifically for a **betrothed** virgin and the man.
- **Where is the man?** She was taken "in the act," so the man was there too, yet only she is brought. Ligonier: "It takes two people to commit an act of physical adultery... Where is he?" Sproul asks the same question. MacArthur suggests the man may have been part of the setup.
- **Moses vs. Rome (Sproul)**: If Jesus says "stone her," he can be reported to Rome for usurping capital authority (cf. John 18:31). If he says "don't," he can be denounced to the Sanhedrin as setting aside Moses. Ligonier's devotional says the same.
- **Law vs. mercy (Augustine, Ambrose, Calvin, Henry, MacArthur)**: If he condemns her, he loses his reputation for gentleness and grace toward "publicans and harlots." If he acquits her, he is against the Law. MacArthur calls it "the ultimate theological dilemma": how God's justice and mercy fit together. The answer is the cross.
- **Stoning or strangling?** Henry, Lightfoot and Sproul note that the general adultery law says "put to death" without naming stoning. In later rabbinic rule an unspecified death meant strangling, and stoning applied to the betrothed. Lightfoot therefore thinks she was probably betrothed. Bengel: "Either this woman was betrothed, or else the expression of the Scribes and Pharisees is abbreviated."

### (b) What did Jesus write?
Main views from fetched sources:
1. **Jer. 17:13**: "those who turn away from you shall be written in the earth" (ESV). Ambrose (Letter XXVI §14) and the NPNF synopsis of Jerome (*Adv. Pelag.* II §17) take this view. Henry reports "Jerome and Ambrose suppose he wrote, Let the names of these wicked men be written in the dust." Sproul mentions it.
2. **Finger of God / tables of the Law** (Exod. 31:18, "written with the finger of God"): Augustine, Ambrose and Bengel. Augustine: the Law was written on stone because of hard hearts; now on earth because he sought fruit.
3. **The sins of the accusers**: some late Greek MSS add "the sins of every one of them" (reported by Henry). Sproul's "favorite speculation" is that he wrote each man's sin.
4. **Matt. 7:3, the mote and the beam**: Ambrose §13.
5. **A gesture of refusal or disdain, not a message**: Calvin explicitly rejects Augustine's reading. Henry: "It is impossible to tell, and therefore needless to ask." MacArthur: "I don't think He wrote anything."
6. **Lightfoot's priestly analogy** (Num. 5, the bitter water): the priest stoops for dust and writes curses in a book. Christ stoops and writes in the dust against the accusers, and "does not write and blot out, but writes and writes again." Lightfoot adds a rabbinic tradition that the bitter waters do not test a wife whose accusing husband is himself unchaste.
- Fact: this is the only record in the Gospels of Jesus writing (Henry, Sproul, Bengel: "Only once He wrote, John 8:6").

### (c) "Let him who is without sin among you be the first to throw a stone at her"
- This rests on Deut. 17:7: the witnesses' hands are first (Calvin, Henry, Bengel, Sproul).
- It does **not** abolish courts or magistrates. Calvin says so explicitly ("not an absolute and unlimited prohibition"). So do Henry ("Not that magistrates, who are conscious of guilt themselves, should therefore connive...") and Sproul ("He is not completely destroying the whole judicial system").
- Sproul: Jesus "sides with Moses... Then He appoints the executioners and finds them lacking. But again, the only One qualified to do it, the only One without sin, was Jesus."

### (d) "Beginning with the older ones"
- Calvin: the higher in rank, the quicker moved by condemnation.
- Henry: "either because they were most guilty, or first aware of the danger."
- Bengel: "These had been most conscience-struck."
- MacArthur: "'cause they had a lot more to remember."
- Calvin and MacArthur both stress that conviction is not repentance: they fled from Christ instead of to him. Henry: "It is folly for those that are under convictions to get away from Jesus Christ."

### (e) "Neither do I condemn you; go, and from now on sin no more." Forgiveness, or only non-condemnation?
- **Non-condemnation (judicial) reading**: Calvin: "We are not told that Christ absolutely acquitted the woman, but that he allowed her to go at liberty." He did not take a judge's office (cf. Luke 12:14). Calvin still says he "comforts her by a promise of grace." Ryle: her guilt is "not proven" because no witnesses remain, "even though she was really guilty." Bengel: "He does not add, in peace; nor does He say, Thy sins are forgiven thee." Bengel also: "adultery is by no means sanctioned by Him, although He did not pronounce condemnation on the adulteress."
- **Forgiveness reading**: Henry's two-level answer is ideal for the episode. (a) Discharge from temporal punishment. (b) "As her discharge from the eternal punishment. For Christ to say, I do not condemn thee is, in effect, to say, I do forgive thee." Augustine: "I have blotted out what you have done." Ambrose: "He would have no man condemned, but all absolved" and "in the remission of sins no man can participate with Christ." MacArthur: "I believe this woman was redeemed that day," grounded in the cross. Sproul: "Go now in your forgiveness and sin no more."
- **Shared conclusion**: "Sin no more" shows that grace is not license (Augustine: "condemned sins, not man"). Compare John 5:14 (ESV): "Sin no more, that nothing worse may happen to you." Ryle on true repentance as forsaking sin.
- A suggested synthesis for a WCF audience: the Judge refuses to sit as a civil magistrate in a rigged case (Calvin and Ryle), yet as the sinless Lord he speaks no condemnation. That word is only just because he will bear her sin (MacArthur; cf. Rom. 8:1). Then he commands new obedience (WCF 15 on repentance).

# Research: 2 Peter 2:1-9 (Old Paths)

Prior episode: 2 Peter 1:12-21 ("men spoke from God as they were carried along by the Holy Spirit"). Chapter 2 opens with a deliberate contrast. Chapter 1 ends with the true prophets carried along by the Spirit, and 2:1 begins "But false prophets also arose among the people..." Peter moves from true prophecy to counterfeit prophecy.

---

## Setting

- **Text (ESV, fetched from Bible Gateway, https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=2+Peter+2%3A1-9&version=ESV):**
  > 1 But false prophets also arose among the people, just as there will be false teachers among you, who will secretly bring in destructive heresies, even denying the Master who bought them, bringing upon themselves swift destruction. 2 And many will follow their sensuality, and because of them the way of truth will be blasphemed. 3 And in their greed they will exploit you with false words. Their condemnation from long ago is not idle, and their destruction is not asleep. 4 For if God did not spare angels when they sinned, but cast them into hell and committed them to chains of gloomy darkness to be kept until the judgment; 5 if he did not spare the ancient world, but preserved Noah, a herald of righteousness, with seven others, when he brought a flood upon the world of the ungodly; 6 if by turning the cities of Sodom and Gomorrah to ashes he condemned them to extinction, making them an example of what is going to happen to the ungodly; 7 and if he rescued righteous Lot, greatly distressed by the sensual conduct of the wicked 8 (for as that righteous man lived among them day after day, he was tormenting his righteous soul over their lawless deeds that he saw and heard); 9 then the Lord knows how to rescue the godly from trials, and to keep the unrighteous under punishment until the day of judgment,
- **ESV footnotes:** v.4 "hell" = Greek *Tartarus*; v.4 "chains" = some manuscripts "pits"; v.6 some manuscripts "an example to those who were to be ungodly"; v.9 "trials" = or "temptations".
- **ESV cross-references worth using:** 2:1 Deut 13:1; Matt 7:15; Acts 20:30; Jude 4; Gal 2:4; for "bought," 1 Cor 6:20; 7:23; Rev 5:9; Ex 15:16; 1 Pet 1:18. 2:7 Gen 19:16. 2:8 Ps 119:136, 158; Ezek 9:4. 2:9 1 Cor 10:13; Rev 3:10.
- **Connection to the prior episode:** 1:19-21 says the prophetic word is "more fully confirmed" and came from men "carried along by the Holy Spirit." 2:1 "But false prophets also arose among the people" (in Israel). Peter's point: the church will have its own false teachers, just as Israel had false prophets alongside the true ones (Deut 13; Jer 23). Calvin: "that God has always tried and proved his Church by such a temptation as this, in order that novelty might not disturb their hearts."
- **Pastoral setting:** Peter writes near his death (1:14). The false teachers mock the Second Coming (3:3-4), live sensually (2:2, 10, 13-14), and are greedy (2:3, 14-15). The examples in 2:4-9 answer the scoffers' "where is the promise of his coming?" in advance: God has judged before and will judge again, and he has rescued his people before and will rescue them again.
- **Relation to Jude 4-7 (ESV, fetched from Bible Gateway):** Jude 4: "certain people have crept in unnoticed who long ago were designated for this condemnation, ungodly people, who pervert the grace of our God into sensuality and deny our only Master and Lord, Jesus Christ." Jude 5-7 uses the Exodus generation, the angels "kept in eternal chains under gloomy darkness until the judgment of the great day," and Sodom and Gomorrah "serve as an example."
  - Shared vocabulary: crept in / secretly bring in (Jude *pareisedusan* / Peter *pareisaxousin*); *aselgeia* "sensuality"; *despotēs* "Master"; "denying"; condemnation "long ago"; angels in chains of gloomy darkness kept for judgment; Sodom and Gomorrah as an "example."
  - Differences: Peter puts the events in biblical-chronological order (angels, flood, Sodom), where Jude has Exodus, angels, Sodom. Peter replaces Jude's Exodus with the Flood. Most importantly, Peter adds the **rescued righteous**, Noah and Lot, which Jude lacks. Jude's examples are judgment only. Peter's have a double edge: judgment and rescue. That is why 2:9 has two clauses.
  - Most scholars hold that one letter used the other, usually that 2 Peter used Jude. Calvin simply says Jude "treats exactly of the same subject."
  - Jude 4 calls the Master "Jesus Christ," which matters for the "bought them" debate (see below).

## Outline

**I. The false teachers described (2:1-3)**
- a. Their precedent: "false prophets also arose among the people" (Israel, Deut 13; Deut 32). Nothing new (v.1a).
- b. Their method: "secretly bring in" (*pareisagō*), smuggling (v.1b).
- c. Their content: "destructive heresies" (*haireseis apōleias*) (v.1b).
- d. Their apostasy: "even denying the Master who bought them" (v.1c).
- e. Their end: "bringing upon themselves swift destruction" (v.1d).
- f. Their influence: "many will follow their sensuality (*aselgeia*), and because of them the way of truth will be blasphemed" (v.2).
- g. Their motive and tool: "in their greed they will exploit (*emporeusontai*) you with false (*plastois*) words" (v.3a).
- h. Their verdict: "condemnation from long ago is not idle, and their destruction is not asleep" (v.3b). The judge is awake. This answers the "delay" scoffing of chapter 3.

**II. One long conditional sentence: God judges the wicked and rescues the righteous (2:4-9)**
- Protasis (four "if" clauses, Greek *ei gar* ... governing vv.4-8):
  1. **If** God did not spare angels when they sinned, but cast them into Tartarus, kept in chains of gloomy darkness for judgment (v.4). *Judgment only.*
  2. **If** he did not spare the ancient world, **but preserved Noah**, a herald of righteousness, with seven others, when he brought the flood (v.5). *Judgment + rescue.*
  3. **If** he turned Sodom and Gomorrah to ashes, condemned them to extinction, an example for the ungodly (v.6). *Judgment.*
  4. **And if he rescued righteous Lot**, greatly distressed (*kataponoumenon*) by the sensual conduct of the wicked (v.7), with parenthesis (v.8): that righteous man, living among them day after day, "was tormenting (*ebasanizen*) his righteous soul" over what he saw and heard. *Rescue.*
- Apodosis (v.9): **then** "the Lord knows how"
  - (a) "to rescue the godly from trials" (looking back to Noah and Lot), and
  - (b) "to keep the unrighteous under punishment until the day of judgment" (looking back to the angels, the flood generation, and Sodom).
- The sentence runs on into v.10a ("and especially those who indulge in the lust of defiling passion and despise authority"), linking the general principle back to the false teachers.
- Calvin's English editor notes that interpreters like Piscator and Macknight supplied a missing "then" after v.7, but "there is no need of this, the corresponding clause is in the ninth verse."
- Teaching point: the grammar itself preaches. The rescue of the godly is as certain as the judgment of the wicked, and it hangs on the same "if."

## Interpretive questions

### 1. "Denying the Master who bought them" (2:1) and definite atonement (WCF 8.8)

**The problem.** Arminians and Amyraldians cite this verse: Christ bought these false teachers, they are lost, so Christ's purchase extends to some who perish. That would cut against particular redemption.

**WCF 8.8** (OPC text, https://www.opc.org/wcf.html): "To all those for whom Christ hath purchased redemption, he doth certainly and effectually apply and communicate the same; making intercession for them, and revealing unto them, in and by the Word, the mysteries of salvation; effectually persuading them by his Spirit to believe and obey, and governing their hearts by his Word and Spirit; overcoming all their enemies by his almighty power and wisdom, in such manner, and ways, as are most consonant to his wonderful and unsearchable dispensation." Note the confession's own word "purchased." Its claim is that the purchase and its application have the same extent. (See also WCF 3.6 and 8.5.)

**The Reformed answers, grouped:**

- **(A) Despotēs = God the Father, and "bought" = Old Testament/Exodus-style ownership, not Calvary.**
  - *John Gill* (Baptist): "not the Lord Jesus Christ, but God the Father," because the word is *despotēs* not *kurios*. He also points to Jude 4, the absence of any price ("whenever redemption by Christ is spoken of, the price is usually mentioned"), and to Deut 32:6, "is not he thy Father that hath bought thee?", where "bought" "regards temporal mercies and deliverance." https://www.biblestudytools.com/commentaries/gills-exposition-of-the-bible/2-peter-2-1.html
  - *Matt Perman* (Desiring God staff article; Desiring God returned 403, read via the Servants of Grace republication) follows *Wayne Grudem*: Peter compares the false teachers to Israel's false prophets, and Israel as a nation was "bought" in the Exodus (Ex 15:16; Deut 32:6). https://servantsofgrace.org/?p=56687
  - Ligonier's *Tabletalk* devotional takes the same Deut 32:5-6 line.
  - **Weakness:** Jude 4 explicitly calls the *despotēs* "Jesus Christ," and *agorazō* is used of Christ's redemption in 1 Cor 6:20 and Rev 5:9. Many Reformed exegetes today (Schreiner, MacArthur) take the Master to be Christ.
- **(B) Owen's threefold "uncertainty" argument** (*Death of Death*, Book IV): (1) uncertain that "Lord" is Christ; (2) uncertain that "bought" means redemption by blood, since there is no mention of blood, price, or offering, and v.20 defines their deliverance as escaping "the pollutions of the world ... through the knowledge of the Lord"; (3) uncertain that Peter speaks of real redemption rather than their profession or the church's judgment of them. He also argues that if Christ died for all, being "bought" could not specially aggravate *these* men's sin. CCEL full text: https://ccel.org/ccel/o/owen/deathofdeath/cache/deathofdeath.txt (also https://www.ccel.org/ccel/owen/deathofdeath.html)
- **(C) Christ is the Master, but Peter speaks according to their profession / the visible church ("phenomenological" or judgment-of-charity language).** This is the main modern Reformed answer.
  - *Matthew Poole* (Christ "seems most probable"): spoken "in the style of the visible church, which should judge them to be so till they declared the contrary by their wicked actions"; "it being alike as to the greatness of the crime, whether he be really redeemed, or, professing himself to be so, denies his Redeemer." https://biblehub.com/commentaries/poole/2_peter/2.htm
  - *Thomas Schreiner* (TGC interview on *From Heaven He Came and Sought Her*): "the redemption posited there is phenomenological," read with 2:20-22. https://www.thegospelcoalition.org/blogs/justin-taylor/the-definitive-work-on-definite-atonement-a-new-website-new-interview-and-new-video/ His fuller treatment is in his NAC/CSC *1, 2 Peter, Jude* and his chapter "'Problematic Texts' for Definite Atonement in the Pastoral and General Epistles" in *From Heaven He Came*. Monergism's page for that chapter returned 403, so I have not quoted it.
  - *Ligonier/Tabletalk* ("False Prophets"): "Jesus 'buys' false teachers only in that He incorporates them into the visible church; He never atoned for their sin." https://learn.ligonier.org/devotionals/false-prophets
  - *Thomas Adams* (1633) gives several senses in which reprobates "may be said to be redeemed": (1) the all-sufficiency of the price; (2) "in respect of outward appearance," like Israel called "elect." He first lays down "that no soul which Christ hath truly bought can perish eternally" (John 6:39; 10:28; Rom 8:38).
  - *MacArthur* (not fetched from gty.org; a search summary of a Grace to You broadcast, "A Portrait of False Teachers, Part 2," aired 9 Sept 2022) reportedly stresses that the teachers claimed Christ as Redeemer while rejecting his lordship. **Not verified, so do not quote.**
- **(D) Calvin's own comment** does not engage the extent question. He reads the denial as moral: "when the grace of God is turned into lasciviousness," those who "throw off the bridle ... are not unjustly said to deny Christ by whom they have been redeemed." Calvin speaks of them as redeemed in some sense, and Amyraldians cite this. His focus is that redemption obligates us to live to Christ ("that he may be the Lord of our life and of our death"). The English editor's footnote adds that they "denied Christ as their sovereign, as they rendered no obedience to him, though they may have professed to believe in him as a Savior." On air, handle Calvin honestly: he is not deploying this verse either way on extent. https://ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom45.vii.iii.i.html

**Suggested on-air framing (PCA):**
1. Hear the verse's own force first. It is an aggravation: these men sat under the banner of the One who owns them by purchase, and they deny him. That is the horror. (Owen and Adams both call it the "aggravation" of their sin.)
2. Peter is describing apostates from the visible church, and Scripture often names covenant members by what they profess (Owen: "the perpetual course of the Scripture").
3. Verse 20 describes what they actually had: an escape from the world's pollutions "through the knowledge" of Christ, but no new birth. 2:22, "the dog returns to its own vomit," shows their nature never changed.
4. WCF 8.8: where Christ's purchase is redemptive, it is certainly applied. Rev 14:4, 1 Cor 6:20 and John 10:28 do not allow for a purchased sheep being lost.
5. Pastoral application: the warning is for professors. Do not trust a profession, a baptism, or a place in the church while denying the Master's lordship by your life.

### 2. Is Lot really "righteous," given Genesis 13 and 19?

- **The problem:** Lot chose Sodom for its well-watered plain (Gen 13:10-13), sat in its gate (19:1), offered his daughters to the mob (19:8), "lingered" and had to be dragged out (19:16), bargained for Zoar (19:18-20), and was made drunk and committed incest (19:30-38). How can Peter call him "righteous" three times in two verses?
- **Answers from the sources:**
  - **Justified by faith, not by perfection.** *Gill*: "a just man, being justified by the righteousness of Christ imputed to him ... though not without sin." *Ligonier/Tabletalk* ("Condemned to Extinction"): "Neither Noah nor Lot had any inherent righteousness in themselves that rendered them acceptable to God (see Gen. 9:20-21; 19:4-8)," yet their desire for holiness proved "they had by faith alone laid hold of Christ."
  - **Judged by the bent of his life.** *Matthew Henry*: "God does not account men just or unjust from one single act, but from their general course of life." https://biblehub.com/commentaries/mhcw/2_peter/2.htm
  - **The evidence Peter cites is inward grief.** Lot was "tormenting his righteous soul" (active voice) over what he saw and heard. *Calvin*: "just Lot underwent voluntary sorrows." *Poole*: "His grief was voluntary, and he active in it." Cf. Ps 119:136; Ezek 9:4.
  - **Delivered by mercy, not merit.** *Thomas Adams*: "Just Lot was delivered, neither for his justice, nor without it. Not for his own righteousness, but for God's mercy." Gen 19:16: "the LORD being merciful to him"; 19:29: "God remembered Abraham, and sent Lot out."
  - **Saints fall.** Adams: "Even a just Lot is suffered to fall ... Let their falling humble us, and their rising again comfort us."
  - **Jewish tradition.** Gill notes that rabbinic tradition was harsh on Lot ("a perfect wicked man"), while Wisdom 10:6 calls him "the righteous man." Peter follows the Wisdom-type estimate and grounds it in Genesis 18-19 (Abraham's plea for the "righteous" in Sodom).
  - **Hospitality.** *1 Clement* 11 says Lot was saved "on account of his hospitality and godliness," pairing him with Lot's wife as a warning against being "double-minded."
- **Application:** Lot is a sobering comfort. He is rescued, but he is the model of a compromised believer who pitched his tent toward Sodom and lost nearly everything. His righteousness shows in his vexation. Christians living in a "Sodom" culture should be grieved, not desensitized.

### 3. Other minor questions
- **Tartarus / angels in chains (2:4):** Peter uses a pagan term for the deepest prison (Bible Hub G5020). The sin of the angels is not specified. Many link it to Gen 6:1-4 via 1 Enoch (and Jude 6, 14). Calvin counsels sobriety: "it behoves us soberly to speak on the subject ... What is useful to us, God has made known." Recommend following Calvin and not speculating on air.
- **"Noah ... with seven others" / "the eighth person" (KJV):** eight saved (1 Pet 3:20). Calvin's editor notes Lightfoot's alternative ("eighth preacher of righteousness," counting from Enos), but the "one of eight" sense is standard.
- **Noah "a herald of righteousness":** Genesis records no sermon by Noah. The tradition is attested in 1 Clement 7 and 9, and Calvin says he preached "not only by his teaching and godly exhortations, but also by his anxious toil in building the ark for the term of a hundred and twenty years."
- **"Rescue the godly from trials" (2:9):** this means rescue *through and out of* trials, not exemption from them. Henry: "It is here presupposed that the righteous must have their temptations and trials." Calvin: God "suffers them sometimes as it were to pine away through daily weariness and languor," but they "are never to be deprived of his aid and help." Compare 1 Cor 10:13.
- **"Keep the unrighteous under punishment until the day of judgment":** the present participle *kolazomenous* raises the question of intermediate punishment before the final judgment. Calvin takes it as "reserved or kept to be punished." The ESV ("under punishment") implies punishment already in progress, which fits the WCF 32.1 doctrine of the intermediate state.
