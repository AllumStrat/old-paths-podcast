## 1. Setting and background

**Date.** Ezekiel 1:1-2 ties "the thirtieth year, in the fourth month, on the fifth day" to "the fifth year of the exile of King Jehoiachin." Jehoiachin surrendered in 597 B.C. (2 Kings 24:10-17), so the date is about July 593 B.C. (the month of Tammuz). Gill says Tammuz "answers to part of June, and part of July," and he notes that Ussher and others put the call "in the year 593, before the birth of Christ" (Gill on 1:1, pages/ez-gill1.txt, https://biblehub.com/commentaries/gill/ezekiel/1.htm). Keil uses an older chronology and gives 595 B.C. (pages/ez-kad1.txt).

**Ezekiel's deportation.** He went into exile with Jehoiachin in 597, when 10,000 leading people were deported (2 Kings 24:14). Ellicott: "The prophet was carried captive to Babylon with the king Jehoiachin (Ezekiel 1:2; comp. with Ezekiel 33:21) in the eighth year of the reign of Nebuchadnezzar (B.C. 596), ten thousand of the more important part of the people being transplanted to Babylonia at the same time (2Kings 24:14), eleven years before the destruction of Jerusalem and the Temple." (Ellicott intro by F. Gardiner, pages/ez-ell1.txt, https://biblehub.com/commentaries/ellicott/ezekiel/1.htm). Ezekiel was a priest, the son of Buzi (1:3). He had a house where the elders came to him (8:1), and his wife died in 588/587 (24:15-18).

**The Chebar canal.** Older writers such as Keil, Poole and Gill took the Chebar to be the Khabur river of northern Mesopotamia. The ISBE article on Bible Hub rejects that: "It is described as in 'the land of the Chaldeans,' and is not, therefore, to be sought in northern Mesopotamia. This rules out the Habor, the modern Chabour, with which it is often identified... Hilprecht found mention made of (naru) kabaru, one of these canals large enough to be navigable, to the East of Nippur, 'in the land of the Chaldeans.'" (pages/ez-topical-chebar.txt, https://biblehub.com/topical/c/chebar.htm). The naru Kabaru appears in the Murashu business archive from Nippur (5th century B.C.), which Hilprecht published. The Al-Yahudu ("Judah-town") tablets, dated 572-477 B.C., include a nearby town named Nār-Kabari. Sources: BAS Library summary (pages/ez-bas-yahudu.txt, https://library.biblicalarchaeology.org/?p=71553; full article paywalled), Pearce and Wunsch, *Documents of Judean Exiles and West Semites in Babylonia* (CUSAS 28, 2014). Those tablets show Judeans leasing land and doing business, which fits Jeremiah 29:5-7 ("build houses").

**Tel-abib (3:15).** In Hebrew the name means "hill of barley-ears." The ISBE gives the Akkadian theory cautiously: "That Tel-abib is written, as Fried. Delitzsch suggests, for Til Ababi, 'Mound of the Flood' (which may have been a not uncommon village-name in Babylonia) is uncertain." (pages/ez-topical-telabib.txt, https://biblehub.com/topical/t/tel-abib.htm). A *til abubi* was a mound left from ancient ruins, "flood-ruined" land that was resettled with deportees. Calvin, Gill and Barnes all go with the Hebrew "heap of grain" sense.

**Jehoiachin's ration tablets.** Bible Archaeology Report: "Between 1899 and 1917, Robert Koldewey oversaw the excavations at the ancient city of Babylon. In an underground room near the Ishtar Gate, he discovered a large cache of cuneiform tablets which documented the rations for prisoners and skilled laborers of Babylon." Also: "they establish that Jehoiachin was actually in captivity in Babylon, and that he was still recognized as the king of Judah." (pages/ez-bar-jehoiachin.txt, https://biblearchaeologyreport.com/2020/11/13/king-jehoiachin-an-archaeological-biography/). The tablets are dated about 592 B.C. (COJS, pages/ez-cojs-ration.txt), which is the year after Ezekiel's call. This is why Ezekiel dates his book by Jehoiachin's exile and not by Zedekiah's reign.

**Contemporary with Jeremiah.** Jeremiah 27-29 belongs to 594/593. In Jeremiah 29 the prophet writes to the exiles to settle down for seventy years, and he names the false prophets in Babylon, Ahab son of Kolaiah and Zedekiah son of Maaseiah (29:21-23), and Shemaiah (29:24-32). Jeremiah 51:59 records Seraiah's trip to Babylon with (or for) Zedekiah "in the fourth year of his reign" (594/593). Calvin makes the link explicitly (see quote C-1 below). The Ligonier devotional: "Apparently, God called Ezekiel in the same year King Zedekiah of Judah came to visit Babylon (Jer. 51:59)." (pages/ez-lig-word-lord-exiles.txt, https://learn.ligonier.org/devotionals/word-lord-exiles).

**The merkabah tradition.** Mishnah Hagigah 2:1 (COJS translation): "The forbidden sexual relations may not be expounded before three [or more] people, nor the account of creation before two, nor the Chariot (Ma'aseh ha-Merkavah) before one, unless he is a Sage who understands by his own knowledge." (pages/ez-cojs-hagigah.txt, https://cojs.org/mishnah_hagigah_2-1-_teaching_the_secrets_of_the_torah/). Jerome's Letter 53 records the related custom about age thirty (quote F-3). The Mishnah's word "chariot" does not appear in Ezekiel 1. It comes from 1 Chronicles 28:18 and Sirach 49:8.

**"Son of man" (ben adam).** It is used about 93 times in Ezekiel and always addresses the prophet. It stresses his creatureliness against the glory he has just seen. Derek Thomas, who is PCA, says the same (quote M-3).

**Chashmal, "gleaming metal" / "amber" (1:4, 27; 8:2).** Ellicott: "The word rendered 'amber' (chasmal) occurs only in this book (here, and Ezekiel 1:27 and Ezekiel 8:2), and is now generally recognised as meaning some form of bright metal, either glowing in its molten state, or as the 'fine brass' of Ezekiel 1:7 and Revelation 1:15, burnished and glowing in the light of the 'infolding flame.'" (pages/ez-ell1.txt). Modern Hebrew uses *chashmal* for "electricity."

**Book structure: the glory leaves and returns.**
- 1-3: the glory appears in Babylon.
- 8-11: the glory leaves the temple (10:18-19; 11:22-23, eastward to the Mount of Olives).
- 33:21-22: Jerusalem falls and Ezekiel's mouth is opened.
- 43:1-5: the glory returns from the east into the new temple.
- 48:35: the city's name is "The LORD Is There."

**New Testament echoes.**
- Revelation 4:6-8: four living creatures, lion, ox, man and eagle (one face each), with a rainbow round the throne (Rev 4:3 // Ezek 1:28).
- Revelation 10:9-10: the scroll is "sweet as honey" in the mouth but bitter in the stomach (Ezek 3:3, 14).
- Revelation 1:13-15: "one like a son of man" with feet like burnished bronze.
- John 12:41: Isaiah "saw his glory." Calvin applies the same logic to Ezekiel 1:26.

## 2. Outline with key verses

1. **Superscription and date (1:1-3).** "the heavens were opened, and I saw visions of God" (1:1). "the hand of the LORD was upon him there" (1:3). This is God in Babylon, outside the land.
2. **The storm-throne vision (1:4-28).**
   - A storm from the north, with fire and gleaming metal (1:4).
   - Four living creatures (1:5-14): four faces each (man, lion, ox, eagle), straight legs, wings touching. They move "straight forward" without turning, and "wherever the spirit would go, they went."
   - Wheels "within a wheel," with rims "full of eyes all around" (1:15-21).
   - The expanse "like awe-inspiring crystal" (1:22-25).
   - A throne like sapphire, and on it "a likeness with a human appearance" (1:26-27).
   - A rainbow brightness: "Such was the appearance of the likeness of the glory of the LORD. And when I saw it, I fell on my face" (1:28).
3. **The commission (2:1-3:11).**
   - "Son of man, stand on your feet" (2:1). The Spirit sets him on his feet (2:2).
   - He is sent to a "nation of rebels... impudent and stubborn" (2:3-4). "Whether they hear or refuse to hear... they will know that a prophet has been among them" (2:5).
   - "Be not afraid... though briers and thorns are with you and you sit on scorpions" (2:6-7).
   - The scroll of "lamentation and mourning and woe," written front and back (2:8-10). "Eat this scroll... it was in my mouth as sweet as honey" (3:1-3).
   - He is not sent to a foreign tongue. "the house of Israel will not be willing to listen to you, for they are not willing to listen to me" (3:4-7). "I have made your face as hard as their faces... like emery harder than flint" (3:8-9). "Hear with your ears" (3:10-11).
4. **Transport to Tel-abib (3:12-15).** "Blessed be the glory of the LORD from its place" (3:12). "I went in bitterness in the heat of my spirit, the hand of the LORD being strong upon me" (3:14). "I sat there overwhelmed among them seven days" (3:15; KJV "I sat where they sat... astonished").
5. **The watchman (3:16-21).** "I have made you a watchman for the house of Israel" (3:17). The four cases: wicked unwarned, wicked warned, righteous unwarned with "I lay a stumbling block before him," and righteous warned (3:18-21). "His blood I will require at your hand" (3:18, 20). "You will have delivered your soul" (3:19, 21).
6. **Binding and muteness (3:22-27).** The glory appears again in the valley (3:22-23). "Shut yourself within your house... cords... I will make your tongue cling to the roof of your mouth... But when I speak with you, I will open your mouth" (3:24-27). The muteness ends at 24:27 / 33:22, when news of Jerusalem's fall arrives, about seven years later.

## 3. Interpretive questions and how the voices answer

### (1) What is "the thirtieth year" (1:1)?
- **Prophet's age.** The view goes back to Origen and Jerome per Gill. Thirty was the age priests entered service (Num 4:3), so the priest who could not serve in Jerusalem is called as prophet at that age. Poole lists it first. The Ligonier devotional assumes it: "Ezekiel was thirty years old at the time of his call" (M-1). Keil rejects it because a full day-month date "points to an era generally recognised" (K-1).
- **Thirty years since Josiah's law-book (622 B.C.).** This is the Targum's view. Poole, Gill and Keil all mention it. Keil objects that "No trace... can elsewhere be pointed out of the existence of a chronology dating from these events."
- **Jubilee reckoning.** Calvin's own view: "I therefore do not doubt that this thirtieth year is reckoned from the Jubilee" (C-1). Gill lists it first, citing the rabbis.
- **Nabopolassar's era (from 625 B.C.).** Gill prefers it ("or rather it was the thirtieth year of Nabopolassar"). Keil: "Nothing, however, is know of any such era."
- **Takeaway for the episode:** God's call comes "in the thirtieth year" whatever the reckoning. Calvin notes it falls in the 35th year of Jeremiah's lonely ministry. God gave Jeremiah "a coadjutor" (C-1).

### (2) What do the living creatures and wheels signify? Who is on the throne?
- **Cherubim, angels serving providence.** Ezekiel 10:20 itself says "I knew that they were cherubim."
  - Calvin: four creatures because "God's providence rules everywhere throughout the world" (C-2). The eyed wheels mean "that providence which never wanders" (C-3).
  - Henry: "Providence, represented by the wheels... Look every way upon the wheel of providence, it has a face toward you" (H-1).
  - Spurgeon: "These 'wheels' signify Divine Providence," and the creatures are angels (S-4, S-5).
- **The four Gospels.**
  - Irenaeus: the four faces are "images of the dispensation of the Son of God," corresponding to the fourfold Gospel (F-1).
  - Jerome: Matthew, Mark, Luke and John are "the Lord's team of four, the true cherubim" (F-2).
  - Augustine weighs the competing assignments (lion = Matthew, man = Mark, calf = Luke, eagle = John) (F-4).
  - This is good church-history color, but it is typology built on Revelation 4 rather than Ezekiel's own meaning.
- **What the vision taught the exiles.** God is not confined to the temple.
  - Calvin: "their temple was as it were God's prison" (C-2).
  - Ligonier: "Unlike false gods, the true God is not bound by time or space" (M-1). Ligonier also says the throne-chariot shows God "getting ready to move from His traditional dwelling place in Canaan" (M-2).
- **The man on the throne.** He is the pre-incarnate Son, but in appearance and likeness only.
  - Calvin: "the Prophet saw God only in the person of Christ, because what is said of the likeness of a man cannot be transferred to either the Father or the Spirit" (C-4). Calvin rejects Jerome's view (the Father) and Servetus's view (a real body).
  - Henry: "The eternal Son, the second Person in the Trinity, who afterwards took the human nature, is here denoted" (H-2).
  - Gill: "this was no other than Christ; who, though he was not really man before his incarnation, yet often appeared in the form of a man" (G-1).
- **The rainbow.** Henry reads it as covenant mercy (H-2). Calvin refuses that reading here: "the whole of this vision was formidable" (C-5). This is a good disagreement to feature.

### (3) The watchman (3:16-21): eternal death or physical death? And the righteous who turns, plus "I lay a stumbling block" (3:20)
- **Physical or eternal death?**
  - Gill: "Not only a corporeal but an eternal death" (G-2).
  - Poole: "such courses will certainly end in death, and in damnation if not left" (P-2).
  - Henry admits the national-covenant setting but extends it: "Though such passages refer to the national covenant made with Israel, they are equally to be applied to the final state of all men under every dispensation" (H-4).
  - Calvin speaks of souls and "eternal death" in the watchman's charge (C-7).
  - A fair summary: in context the immediate threat is death in the coming judgment on Jerusalem (compare ch. 18 and 33). Reformed expositors still universally read it as pointing to the final judgment.
- **The "righteous man" who turns.**
  - Calvin: "righteousness is here called so:, which has only the outward appearance and not the root: for when once the spirit of regeneration begins to flourish... it remains perpetually." He also says the passage should "stir us up to seek from God continually a spirit of perseverance" (C-8).
  - Gill: "not of one that is truly righteous, or is justified by the righteousness of Christ" (G-3).
  - JFB: "not one 'righteous' as to the root and spirit of regeneration... but as to its outward appearance."
  - Poole leaves it open: "either one that hath for some continuance of time professed... or who is righteous only in appearance" (P-3).
  - Barnes: one "who had hitherto done the 'acts of righteousness' prescribed by the Law, but when trial came was shown to lack the 'principle of righteousness.'"
  - The PCA angle (WCF 17): the warnings are means God uses to preserve his saints, not proof that true saints fall away.
- **"I lay a stumbling block."**
  - Calvin takes it as punishment: "Punishment is here called a stumblingblock, when God demonstrates his vengeance against apostates" (C-8).
  - Gill gives both options: permitted occasion of sin, "or rather the punishment of sin."
  - Poole: "dispose affairs so by a just and punishing providence..." (P-3).
  - Pulpit: "In the case now before us the man has turned from his righteousness before the stumbling block is laid in his way" (B-1).
- **The watchman's duty applied to pastors.** Calvin: "What Ezekiel heard belongs to all teachers of the Church" (C-6). Spurgeon applies it to every believer: "when the camp is in imminent danger every man should turn watchman" (S-1).

### (4) The muteness of 3:26
- **Total or partial silence.** Ezekiel clearly speaks oracles in chapters 4-24, so the muteness is selective. He is silent except when God opens his mouth (3:27).
  - Keil: "This state of silence, according to which he is only then to speak when God opened his mouth..." It extends "over the whole period up to the fulfilment of his prophecies of threatening by the destruction of Jerusalem," citing 24:27 and 33:22 (K-2).
  - Gill: "not literally... but that such silence should be charged upon him" (G-4).
- **Judgment.** Calvin: "it is a sign of God's vengeance, when all prophecies cease" (C-10). Gill: "which was in judgment to them... a sore judgment upon them" (G-4).
- **Who binds him?** Calvin allows either a metaphor for the people's resistance or a literal threat (C-10). Keil: the cords come from invisible powers, as in 4:8. Ellicott: the silence is "attributed... to God Himself, whose providence was the ultimate cause."

### (5) "Bitterness in the heat of my spirit" (3:14) and "I sat overwhelmed seven days" (3:15)
- **Sweet scroll, bitter spirit: how do they fit together?**
  - Calvin: the sweetness is his "acquiescence in embracing the office" (C-9a). The bitterness is the remaining "human feelings... although not sinful" (C-9b).
  - Poole is harsher: "Ezekiel's weakness and distempered humour will appear in it; Jonas-like, he will be angry" (P-1).
  - Ligonier: "hearing the Lord's curse on sin is ultimately a sweet thing for those of faith" (M-2).
- **The seven days.**
  - Mourning and solidarity. Ellicott and Barnes: "seven days, the set time of mourning (Job 2:13)." Ellicott: "By this act the prophet shows his deep sympathy with his people." Poole: "I sat where they sat; sat sad and astonished, where I found and saw them sitting astonished" (P-4).
  - Preparation. Calvin: "this silence was a preparation for the discharge of his duty with greater fruit and efficacy" (C-9c). He also explains that Ezekiel had his commission but not yet his message, like an ambassador awaiting instructions.
  - Ellicott adds a priestly parallel. The week corresponds "to the week of the consecration of his fathers to their priestly of[fice]" (Lev 8:33). The quote is truncated in the file.
  - Henry: "He was overwhelmed with grief for the sins and miseries of his people, and overpowered by the glory of the vision he had seen" (H-4).

## 1. Setting, background, and book introduction

**Who and where**
- Job lived "in the land of Uz" (1:1). The ESV cross-references at 1:1 are Jer. 25:20 and Lam. 4:21 (pages/esv-job1.txt). Lam. 4:21 speaks of the "daughter of Edom, you who dwell in the land of Uz," which places Uz in or near Edom.
- Matthew Poole: "The land of Uz was either in Edom, called the land of Uz, Lam 4:21, or in some part of Arabia, not far from the Chaldeans and Sabeans, as this chapter witnesseth; so called probably from Uz, one of Esau's posterity, Gen 36:28 Jer 25:20." (https://biblehub.com/commentaries/poole/job/1.htm, pages/job-poole.txt)
- John Gill also mentions Uz the son of Nahor (Gen. 22:21) and Uz of Seir/Edom, then concludes "rather it is Arabia" (Petraea or Deserta). He calls it an instance "of the distinguishing grace of God, to call Job by his grace in the land of Uz, as it was to call Abraham in Ur of the Chaldeans". (https://biblehub.com/commentaries/gill/job/1.htm, pages/job-gill.txt)
- Job was probably not an Israelite. Anthony Selvaggio (Ligonier): "most scholars agree that Job was not an Israelite. This conclusion stems from the fact that he lived in the land of Uz rather than the land of Canaan (Job 1:1). It is likely that Job lived in the land of Edom, as Lamentations associates Edom with Uz (Lam. 4:21)." (https://learn.ligonier.org/articles/3-things-job, pages/job-lig-art-3-things-job.txt) [modern, copyrighted]

**Date (the patriarchal era)**
- Selvaggio gives four arguments: (1) the divine names resemble those of the patriarchal books; (2) the kind of wealth described (cattle and servants); (3) Job's 140 years (42:16) match patriarchal lifespans; (4) "most convincingly, Job acts in a priestly role for his family, suggesting that the Levitical priesthood had not yet been established (Job 1:5)." He dates the patriarchal period "approximately 2100–1800 BC". He also notes that the book's place after Esther in the canon "sometimes leads to wrong conclusions about who Job was and when he lived." (same page)
- Derek Thomas (Ligonier): "The mention of raiding Sabeans and Chaldean tribes suggests that Job lived during the second millennium, perhaps during the time of Abraham or Moses (Job 1:15, 17)." (https://learn.ligonier.org/articles/who-was-job, pages/job-lig-art-who-was-job.txt, published 2024-11-04) [modern, copyrighted]
- R.C. Sproul: "The setting for this narrative is in patriarchal times." (https://learn.ligonier.org/articles/book-job, pages/job-lig-art-book-job.txt) [modern, copyrighted]
- A dissenting voice: Gregory the Great (Preface §1) argued that Job lived in the time of the Judges. He identified Job with Jobab of Gen. 36:33. (pages/job-gregory-preface.txt) Most modern conservatives take the patriarchal view.

**Was Job a real man? (Ezekiel 14:14, 20 and James 5:11)**
- This is a useful bridge, because today's OT reading begins Ezekiel. Ezekiel 14:14, 20 names "Noah, Daniel, and Job" together as historical righteous men.
- Gill: "that there was such a man is as certain as that there were such men as Noah and Daniel, with whom he is mentioned by the Prophet Ezekiel, Ezekiel 14:14 and the testimony of the Apostle James is full to this purpose, who speaks of him as a person well known, and not to be doubted of; of whom, and of whose patience, the Jews he writes to had heard much, James 5:11" (Gill's Introduction to Job, pages/job-gill.txt)
- Derek Thomas: "The prophet Ezekiel mentions Job along with Daniel and Noah as examples of godly men, suggesting that Job was a historical person rather than a mere literary figure." (pages/job-lig-art-who-was-job.txt) [modern]
- Calvin's first sermon points to James twice. He says we "haue to behold the patientnesse of the man that is set heere before our eyes, according as S. Iames exhorteth vs" and must consider "not only the patience of Iob, but also the issue of it, as Sainct Iames sayth." (segments 13 and 18; see the Quote Bank)

**Authorship (uncertain)**
- Ellicott: "There is nothing whatever to guide us on this point except the evidence of the book itself... There is but one solitary fragment of tradition, which is that Job, like the Pentateuch, was the work of Moses. This may be worth nothing critically, but as a tradition it is simply the only one that exists." (Ellicott's Introduction, https://biblehub.com/commentaries/ellicott/job/1.htm, pages/job-ellicott.txt) Ellicott himself leans toward Moses.
- Gregory the Great's classic answer is that the human author does not matter (see the Quote Bank, the "pen" quote). This makes a good podcast line.

**Structure**
- Ellicott ("Plan of the Book"): "There is, first, an historical prologue... Secondly, a dialogue or argument carried on between Job and three of his friends... Thirdly... Elihu". He then lists God's speeches and the epilogue. (pages/job-ellicott.txt)
- The working outline:
  - prose prologue (chs. 1–2)
  - Job's lament (ch. 3)
  - three cycles of dialogue with Eliphaz, Bildad and Zophar (4–27)
  - wisdom poem (28)
  - Job's final defense (29–31)
  - Elihu (32–37)
  - the LORD's speeches and Job's repentance (38:1–42:6)
  - prose epilogue (42:7–17)
- Ligonier devotional: "the book of Job is unique in its inclusion of narrative bookends that introduce and conclude the wisdom poems found between them (Job 1–2; 42)." (https://learn.ligonier.org/devotionals/trials-job, pages/job-lig-trials-job.txt) [modern]
- Sproul on the book's genre and message: it is wisdom literature "not communicated in the form of proverb" but through a narrative, and its theme is "how God is involved in the problem of human suffering." (pages/job-lig-art-book-job.txt) [modern]

**Word notes**
- **ha-satan, "the Satan / the Adversary" (1:6).** ESV footnote: "Hebrew the Accuser or the Adversary; so throughout chapters 1–2" (pages/esv-job1.txt). Cambridge Bible (A.B. Davidson): "The Heb. is the Satan, where the presence of the article shews that the word has not yet become a proper name." Ellicott: "Here only and in Zechariah it is found with the definite article 'the adversary.'" (pages/job-cambridge.txt; pages/job-ellicott.txt)
  - **[note]** Davidson goes on to describe the Satan as "one of God's ministers" who "represents God's trying, sifting providence." That is a critical-leaning reading. A Reformed treatment should keep the NT identification (Rev. 12:10; 1 Pet. 5:8). Gregory explicitly links 1:9 to Rev. 12:10, "the accuser of our brethren."
- **bene ha-elohim, "the sons of God" (1:6).** Angels. Christopher Ash: "Some translations say 'angels', which is probably right, but sons of God is the literal translation. You meet them in 1 Kings 22 with Micaiah". (pages/job-ash-crossway.txt) Cambridge compares the heavenly-council scenes in 1 Kings 22:19ff., Isaiah 6 and Zechariah 3.
- **tam, "blameless" (1:1, 8).** Keil & Delitzsch: "The writer intentionally uses four synonyms together, in order to describe as strongly as possible Job's piety... תּם, with the whole heart disposed towards God and what is good, and also well-disposed toward mankind" (https://biblehub.com/commentaries/kad/job/1.htm, pages/job-kad.txt). Ligonier devotional: "This does not mean he was a sinlessly perfect man... The designation blameless in Scripture is often used for people who, though sinners, are in the main oriented toward loving and serving the Lord (see Gen. 6:9; Luke 1:5–7)." (pages/job-lig-trials-job.txt) [modern]
- **barak, "bless", used as a euphemism for "curse" (1:5, 11; also 2:5, 9).** ESV footnote: "The Hebrew word bless is used euphemistically for curse in 1:5, 11; 2:5, 9". Benson: "the word ברךְ, barack, here rendered to curse, usually signifies to bless; but it is evident it is here to be understood in a bad sense, as it is 1 Kings 21:10, where Naboth is accused of cursing or blaspheming God and the king". (https://biblehub.com/commentaries/benson/job/1.htm, pages/job-benson.txt)
  - **Irony for the podcast:** the scribe would not write "curse God" outright, and the chapter ends with Job literally doing barak in its true sense: "blessed be the name of the LORD" (1:21).
- **hinnam, "for nothing / for no reason / gratis" (1:9).** Keil & Delitzsch: "Genuine love loves God חנּם (adverb from חן, like gratis from gratia): it loves Him for His own sake; it is a relation of person to person, without any actual stipulations and claim." (pages/job-kad.txt) Gill footnotes "gratis" in Pagninus, Montanus, Junius & Tremellius and others (pages/job-gill.txt). The root is חן, "grace", so the question is literally whether Job fears God "gratis".
- **The four messengers.** Each ends "I alone have escaped to tell you" (1:15, 16, 17, 19), and each arrives "While he was yet speaking". The disasters alternate between human and natural agents:
  - Sabeans (human, from the south)
  - "fire of God" (natural; probably lightning)
  - Chaldeans in "three groups" (human, from the north-east)
  - "a great wind" (natural; it kills the ten children)
  Commentators on the messengers:
  - Poole on the Sabeans: "a people of Arabia, who led a wandering life, and lived by robbery and spoiling of others". On the "fire of God": "a terrible flame of fire sent from God in an extraordinary manner, to intimate that both God and men were his enemies". (pages/job-poole.txt)
  - Spurgeon's exposition of the fire: "this one would appear to be more severe than the former one because it seemed to come distinctly from God. 'The fire of God' — the lightning". (pages/job-spurg-si-comm.txt)
- **Job's worship (1:20).** He tore his robe, shaved his head, "fell on the ground and worshiped." Grief and worship happen together, and the text does not set them against each other. Gregory notes that Job is both mourning and enthroned (see the Quote Bank).
- **The 1 Peter 5:8 connection (used last episode).** Ellicott on 1:7: "St. Peter evidently had this passage in mind (1Peter 5:8, 'walketh about')." Cambridge also quotes 1 Pet. 5:8 at 1:7. Matthew Henry (Concise) on 1:6–12: "While we are on this earth we are within his reach. Hence it concerns us to be sober and vigilant, 1Pe 5:8." Spurgeon's exposition of 1:10: "The black dog of hell had been prowling around". (See the Quote Bank.)

## 2. Outline of Job 1 with key verses

1. **Job's character and household (1:1–5)**
   - 1:1 "blameless and upright, one who feared God and turned away from evil." The four-fold description is repeated by God in 1:8 (and 2:3).
   - 1:2–3: seven sons and three daughters, with great wealth. He is "the greatest of all the people of the east."
   - 1:4–5: the children's feasts, and Job's early-morning burnt offerings: "It may be that my children have sinned, and cursed God in their hearts." "Thus Job did continually."
2. **First heavenly council (1:6–12)**
   - 1:6–7: the sons of God present themselves, and the Satan comes "From going to and fro on the earth".
   - 1:8: God raises the subject: "Have you considered my servant Job".
   - 1:9–11: the accusation: "Does Job fear God for no reason?" The hedge, the blessing, and the "curse you to your face" wager.
   - 1:12: permission with a limit: "all that he has is in your hand. Only against him do not stretch out your hand."
3. **Four messengers, one day (1:13–19)**
   - Sabeans take the oxen and donkeys.
   - Fire of God burns the sheep.
   - Chaldeans take the camels.
   - A great wind kills the children.
   - "I alone have escaped to tell you."
4. **Job's worship (1:20–22)**
   - 1:20: torn robe, shaved head, falls on the ground and worships.
   - 1:21 "Naked I came from my mother's womb, and naked shall I return. The LORD gave, and the LORD has taken away; blessed be the name of the LORD."
   - 1:22 "In all this Job did not sin or charge God with wrong."

## 3. Interpretive questions with voices

### (1) Is God's permission to Satan troubling? Is the loss from Satan or from God?
The text says both. Satan's hand did the damage (1:12), and Job says "the LORD has taken away" (1:21). The narrator then approves Job's words (1:22).
- **God takes the initiative.** Ligonier devotional: "God is not exercising a simple permission... The Lord, in fact, initiates the whole matter". Derek Thomas: "it is God, not Satan, who suggests that Job become Satan's target". Thomas also admits: "We are not given an explanation as to how God is totally sovereign and not the author sin".
  - **[note]** "author sin" is the page's wording; the word "of" appears to be missing on the page.
- **Concurrence.** Ligonier "Divine Concurrence" devotional: Satan, the raiders and God act in the same event with different intents. "God's intent was good. The other players intended evil." It ties this to the cross.
- **Gregory.** Job does not say "the Lord hath given, the devil hath taken away". Only the Giver has taken, so "He hath only recalled what was His own".
- **Trapp.** "Job easily discerned God's arrows in Satan's hand".
- **Poole and Gill.** The raiders and the weather were "only God's instruments" (Poole); Satan "had no power but what was given from God" (Gill).
- **Spurgeon.** The dog who bites the stick (Sermon No. 3025). Also, "although he is mighty, he is not almighty".
- **Matthew Henry.** "God has the devil in a chain".
- **Calvin.** "God hath such a souerainty ouer his creatures, as he may dispose of thē at his pleasure".
- **Pastoral guardrail.** Satan is limited twice: "only against him" (1:12), and later "only spare his life" (2:6). Gregory: "His restraining him from an excessive assault of temptation, even in giving him permission."

### (2) The hinnam question: do we serve God for his gifts?
- **Satan's charge.** Poole: "It is policy, not piety, that makes him good; he doth not serve thee, but serveth himself of thee". Trapp: "he may swim well enough, when so held up by the chin."
- **What true love is.** Keil & Delitzsch: genuine love loves God hinnam, "for His own sake".
- **Spurgeon, Morning and Evening, Jan. 22 evening.** "Their love is the love of the table, not of the host; a love to the cupboard, not to the master of the house."
- **A balanced answer.** Gill: it is lawful to look to the reward "when they do not make these, but the will and glory of God, the sole and chief cause and end thereof". Henry: "It is not wrong to look at the eternal recompence in our obedience; but it is wrong to aim at worldly advantages in our religion."
- **Christopher Ash.** Ash calls Satan's charge a "prosperity gospel" accusation. He also says: "the glory of God depends on there being a man walking the earth who worships him purely and simply because he is God."
- **Selvaggio.** "God is vindicated as being worthy of worship simply for who He is, and Job is vindicated as a man of integrity."
- **Ellicott.** "Satan knows that the motive of an action is its only value".

### (3) Why does the reader see the heavenly court when Job never does?
- **Ellicott.** "The several speakers were shooting arrows in the dark; the reader only occupies a vantage-ground in the light afforded by a knowledge of the secret."
- **Derek Thomas.** "What Job does not know, and what we are privately told, is that behind these earthly trials lies a cosmic battle". Also: "Job was not privy to the voice of God in the opening two chapters".
- **Ash.** "we who have heard the heavenly scenes in chapters 1 and 2, we know more than Job knows". Ash also notes that Job is right "to say that God has done it."
- **Sproul.** God "never directly answers Job's questions... He answers Job with Himself."
- **Selvaggio.** "It does, however, explain to us why Job suffered (though the reason was never made known to Job himself)."
- **Calvin's first sermon.** We must glorify God "although we perceiue not for what cause hee doth it," and "wait till he shew vs wherefore he chastizeth vs."
- **Poole (a caution for the host).** Poole reads the council scene as "a parabolical representation" of providence, not a literal transcript, "it being usual with the great God to condescend to our shallow capacities". It is worth mentioning as one Reformed reading of the scene's genre (accommodation). It is not a denial of Satan's reality: Poole affirms that God governs "all the actions of men and devils".

### Bonus: Job 1:5 and a priest for his children
- **Gregory, literal reading.** Job knew "there can scarcely be feasting without offence".
- **Gregory, Christological reading.** Job's continual sacrifice pictures Christ, "Who without intermission exhibits to the Father His Incarnation in our behalf."
- **Spurgeon, Morning and Evening, Dec. 25 evening.** "Believer, come to this sacrifice continually".
- **Matthew Henry.** "as one who hoped for acceptance with God through the promised Saviour, he offered a burnt-offering for each."

# PART A. JOHN 7:37-44

## A1. Setting and background

**Occasion.** This is the Feast of Booths (Sukkot, 7:2), a fall pilgrimage feast running from Tishri 15. It looks back to the wilderness booths and celebrates the harvest (Lev 23:33-43; Deut 16:13-15). Jesus went up secretly "about the middle of the feast" (7:10, 14), and 7:37 is "the last day of the feast, the great day."

**Seventh day or eighth day?**
- *Seventh day (Hoshana Rabbah).* Mishnah Sukkah 4:5 says that on "the seventh day of Sukkot, they would circle the altar seven times" (pages/jn-sefaria-sukkah4.txt, https://www.sefaria.org/Mishnah_Sukkah.4.5). Mishnah Sukkah 4:1 says "the ritual of the water libation on the altar are in effect for seven days" (https://www.sefaria.org/Mishnah_Sukkah.4.1). If Jesus spoke on day 7, he spoke at the climax of the water rite.
- *Eighth day (Shemini Atzeret).* Lev 23:36, 39, Num 29:35 and Neh 8:18 support this. It was a Sabbath-like "holy convocation." Ellicott (H. W. Watkins) says the question is "of antiquarian rather than of practical interest" and that "The best modern authorities are for the most part agreed that it was the eighth day, i.e., the 22nd of Tishri" He adds that if the water ceremony was not repeated on day 8, "its very absence may have suggested the fuller declaration of the reality of which it was the representation" (pages/jn-bh737.txt, https://biblehub.com/commentaries/john/7-37.htm). Cambridge (Plummer) also argues for day 8: "To speak of the seventh day as 'the great day of the feast' would not be very appropriate" (same file). Barnes and Gill say the eighth day too.
- *Mishnah minority view.* Mishnah 4:9 records Rabbi Yehuda's view that the libation was poured "on all eight days of the Festival and not only seven." This minority view is useful because it shows that the eighth-day reading need not lose the water rite.
- *Chrysostom* explains "great" by the crowd: "on that day they were all collected together" (quote J-7).

**The water-drawing ceremony (Simchat Beit HaShoevah).**
- *The rite.* Mishnah Sukkah 4:9 describes it: a golden jug filled at Siloam, trumpet blasts at the Water Gate, and the libation poured into silver basins on the altar (quote J-29). It also records a Sadducee priest who "intentionally poured the water on his feet... and in their rage all the people pelted him with their etrogim" (same file). This is a vivid detail, but it may not be worth airtime.
- *The joy.* Mishnah Sukkah 5:1 gives the joy saying (quotes J-28 and J-27b).
- *Night lights.* Sukkah 5:2-4 describes the night celebration. Golden candelabra in the Women's Court were so bright that "there was not a courtyard in Jerusalem that was not illuminated", and the pious danced with torches (pages/jn-sefaria-sukkah5.txt). This is useful as a bridge to John 8:12 ("I am the light of the world"), which is spoken in the same setting.
- *Rabbinic link to the Spirit.* Gill (quote J-27) and Ellicott both cite the rabbinic tradition that from the place of water-drawing "they draw the Holy Ghost", based on Isaiah 12:3. Ellicott cites "Jer. Succa, v. 1". Barnes thinks the rite may have arisen from Isaiah 12:3, and he calls it a rite "uncommanded" by Moses (pages/jn-bh737.txt).
- *Rain prayers.* Gill notes that rain prayers began at this feast. The Talmud says "pour out water before me, that the rains of the year may be blessed unto you" (pages/jn-bh737.txt).
- *Translation caution.* The Sefaria translation (William Davidson/Koren-Steinsaltz, CC-BY-NC) expands the terse Mishnah with explanatory clauses. For broadcast, quote J-28 as "one translation renders it..." or use Gill's older rendering (J-27b).

**Wilderness background.** Booths recalled the wilderness, which included water from the rock (Ex 17:6; Num 20:11; Ps 78:15-16; 114:8). The Ligonier devotional notes this "twin giftings of water and the Spirit" theme (Num 11 and Ex 17) (pages/jn-lig-rivers-of-living-water.txt, https://learn.ligonier.org/devotionals/rivers-of-living-water). The Pulpit Commentary reports Godet's view that the Exodus 17 rock passages were "read during the feast" (pages/jn-bh738.txt).

**Zechariah 14.** Bengel claims that Zechariah 14 (which has "living waters shall go out from Jerusalem," 14:8, and Booths in 14:16-19) was the haftarah read "on the first day of the Feast of Tabernacles" (quote J-24). In current synagogue practice Zech 14 is in fact the haftarah for the first day of Sukkot. Whether that was so in the first century is unprovable, so present it as a possibility.

**Ezekiel 47 link (today's OT reading is Ezekiel 1-3).** Several commentators connect 7:38 with the river flowing from Ezekiel's temple:
- Matthew Henry: "here may be an allusion to the waters issuing out of Ezekiel's temple, Eze. 47:1" (quote J-22).
- Pulpit Commentary: Ezekiel 47 "is so akin to the saying of the Lord... that all real difficulty vanishes" (quote J-25).
- Bengel, Ellicott, Meyer and Vincent all list Ezek 47:1-12 among the candidate texts (pages/jn-bh738.txt).

This makes a natural bridge. The glory seen by the Chebar in Ezek 1 later fills a temple from which a river flows (Ezek 47), and in John 7 Jesus, who is the temple (John 2:19-21), offers that river.

**The Prophet and the Christ (7:40-41).** "The Prophet" points to Deut 18:15-18. Ligonier notes that "many first-century Jews saw them as two distinct figures" and that the Moses association fits because Moses also gave water (pages/jn-lig-jesus-brings-division.txt, https://learn.ligonier.org/devotionals/jesus-brings-division). Compare John 1:21, 25 and 6:14.

**Bethlehem and David (7:42).** The texts are Micah 5:2, 2 Sam 7:12-16, Isa 11:1 and Jer 23:5. The irony is that John's readers know Jesus was born in Bethlehem (Matt 2:1; Luke 2:4). Chrysostom says the crowd spoke "with malicious intent", not to learn (pages/jn-chrys51.txt). Calvin says they "do violence to this passage" out of "contempt" and could have inquired (pages/jn-calvin740.txt).

**7:44.** "no one laid hands on him" (ESV). This echoes 7:30, where no one arrested him because his hour had not yet come. Calvin ascribes it to "the providence of God" (pages/jn-calvin740.txt).

## A2. Outline with key verses

Scripture quotations are ESV (pages/esv-john7.txt). Glosses in square brackets are mine.

1. **The cry (7:37).** "Jesus stood up and cried out, 'If anyone thirsts, let him come to me and drink.'" He stands (a rabbi normally sat to teach) and cries aloud (compare 7:28; 12:44). This is a public summons on the feast's climactic day.
2. **The promise (7:38).** "Whoever believes in me, as the Scripture has said, 'Out of his heart [koilia, belly] will flow rivers of living water.'" Coming = believing (compare 6:35).
3. **The evangelist's gloss (7:39).** "Now this he said about the Spirit... for as yet the Spirit had not been given [Greek, literally 'the Spirit was not yet'], because Jesus was not yet glorified." This looks ahead to the cross, resurrection and Pentecost (12:16, 23; 20:22).
4. **The division (7:40-43).**
   - "This really is the Prophet."
   - "This is the Christ."
   - "Is the Christ to come from Galilee? Has not the Scripture said that the Christ comes from the offspring of David, and comes from Bethlehem...?"
   - "So there was a division [schisma] among the people over him."
5. **The restraint (7:44).** "Some of them wanted to arrest him, but no one laid hands on him." This leads into 7:45-52 and the officers' report (7:46) that no one ever spoke like this man.

## A3. Interpretive questions with voices

**Q1. Which "last day"? Does it matter?** See the background above. In practice, either way Jesus presents himself as the reality behind the water rite. Chrysostom gives the pastoral reason: Jesus waited until the last day "when they were returning home" (J-7). Calvin stresses his courage with the officers present (pages/jn-calvin737.txt on 7:37), and so does Spurgeon (J-30).

**Q2. Who may come? The universal offer and the qualifier "if anyone thirsts"**
- Calvin: the cry is loud so that none may be ignorant (J-13). But only the thirsty come, and "It belongs to the Spirit, therefore, to cause us to desire his grace" (pages/jn-calvin737.txt).
- Chrysostom: "I draw no man to Me by necessity and constraint" (J-6). This is a free invitation. A Reformed listener will want to pair it with John 6:44.
- Spurgeon on "thirst" as simple need: J-31, J-33.
- Spurgeon and Ryle on drinking as receiving with nothing brought: J-32, J-34, J-36.

**Q3. Which Scripture is meant in 7:38? No OT text matches exactly.**
- Chrysostom: "Nowhere" (J-8).
- Calvin: no single passage, but "the ordinary doctrine of the Prophets" (J-18). He names Isa 55:1 for 7:37 (J-14).
- Cyril: Jesus cited Scripture "not exactly as it had been before put out by the Divine Scripture" but "rather interpreting it according to its meaning", pointing to Isa 58:11 ("watered garden... spring") (pages/jn-cyril5.txt).
- Henry gives a catalogue that includes Ezek 47 and Zech 14:8 (J-22).
- Bengel favors Zech 14:8 (J-24).
- Pulpit favors Ezek 47 (J-25).
- Godet (reported in Pulpit) favors the smitten rock, Ex 17:6.

Candidates to name on air: Isa 44:3; 55:1; 58:11; Ezek 47:1-12; Joel 3:18; Zech 13:1; 14:8; Ex 17:6 / Ps 78:15-16.

**Q4. Whose "belly/heart"? The believer's or Christ's? (the punctuation debate)**
- *Believer's (the traditional "Eastern" punctuation, followed by ESV/KJV).* Augustine: "The belly of the inner man is the conscience of the heart", and the river is "Benevolence" overflowing to neighbors (J-2). Cyril: the believer overflows "into others' hearts" (J-12). Calvin: the believer has the Spirit as an inner, continually flowing fountain. He also mentions, and does not prefer, the view that the believer passes water on to others: "I consider it to be a simpler meaning, that whosoever shall believe in Christ shall have a fountain of life springing up, as it were, in himself" (pages/jn-calvin737.txt). Henry: "out of his heart or soul," and the streams spread to others. Ellicott, Cambridge and Ryle hold that the believer becomes a source for others (J-26, J-37).
- *Christ's (the "Western" punctuation).* Here one reads (my paraphrase, not a quotation): If anyone thirsts, let him come to me; and let him drink who believes in me. As the Scripture said, out of his belly... Bengel reads αὐτοῦ as "Messiah's. This is the fountain out of whose abundant flow believers receive" (pages/jn-bh738.txt, Bengel). Ellicott reports, and rejects, this re-punctuation (pages/jn-bh738.txt, line beginning "(38) There can be little doubt").
- *Chrysostom's own odd stop.* He puts a stop after "as the Scripture has said," attaching it to "He that believes in me" (J-8).
- *A Reformed synthesis.* The Ligonier devotional: "We ourselves are not sources of living water, but Christ empowers us by His Spirit and works through us to bless others" (J-39). The river begins in Christ, who is the smitten rock and the true temple (Ezek 47), and flows through believers.

**Q5. "The Spirit was not yet" (7:39). Does this mean the Spirit did not exist or did not work before Pentecost?**
- Augustine: the Spirit existed and worked in Simeon, Anna, Zechariah and the prophets, but "there was to be a certain manner of this giving, which had not at all appeared before" (J-4; full discussion in pages/jn-aug32.txt sec. 6). The vessel of faith determines the measure received (J-5). His deeper reason is that the Spirit is given after the resurrection so that our love would be set on the risen life (sec. 9).
- Chrysostom: "Calling the Cross, glory." Reconciliation by sacrifice had to come first, so that we might "become friends of God, and so receive the Gift" (J-11). He also says the prophets had the Spirit "but imparted It not to others, while the Apostles filled tens of thousands with It" (pages/jn-chrys51.txt).
- Calvin: "he speaks comparatively". The disciples "had undoubtedly received the first-fruits of the Spirit", but the full manifestation awaited Christ's enthronement at the Father's right hand (J-19).
- Henry: the Spirit "was in the Old-Testament prophets and saints". What is meant is "the eminent, plentiful, and general effusion" (pages/jn-mhcw7.txt).
- Cyril: the Spirit, lost to human nature in Adam, is restored permanently in the risen Christ (pages/jn-cyril5.txt, ch. II).

**Q6. Division over Jesus (7:40-43).**
- Calvin: the gospel does not create division but exposes it, like vapours that appear only when the sun rises (J-21). Men "make obstacles" out of the very Scriptures that lead to Christ (J-20).
- Ligonier: "no one can finally remain neutral" (J-40).

# PART B. 2 PETER 1:1-11 (first day of 2 Peter)

## B1. Setting and background (compact book introduction)

**Author and claim.**
- The letter names "Simeon Peter" (1:1). This is the rarer Hebraic form, used of Peter elsewhere only in Acts 15:14.
- The author claims to be an eyewitness of the Transfiguration (1:16-18).
- He speaks of his imminent death, which the Lord foretold (1:14; compare John 21:18-19).
- He calls this his second letter to them (3:1), and he calls Paul a beloved brother (3:15-16).

Cambridge (Plumptre) notes that a forger "would not have been likely to put a stumbling-block in the way of the reception of his work by adopting a different form of opening". His most probable explanation of the different style is "the employment of another amanuensis" (pages/pet-bh11.txt, https://biblehub.com/commentaries/2_peter/1-1.htm).

**Early reception.**
- Eusebius places 2 Peter among the disputed books (antilegomena) that were "nevertheless recognized by many" (P-1, P-2; Church History 3.3.1 and 3.25.3, https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250103.htm).
- Jerome reports doubts because of its "difference from the first in style" (P-3, https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/2708.htm). In another place (Letter 120 to Hedibia, not fetched) he attributes the difference to different interpreters (secretaries).
- The church received the letter at the councils of Laodicea (c. 363, disputed canon 60), Hippo (393) and Carthage (397). These council details are from general knowledge and not fetched, so verify before airing.

**Calvin's exact view (P-4 to P-6).** Calvin is a careful middle voice. Eusebius's doubts should not keep us from the letter, and Jerome's point about style "influences me somewhat more". He confesses "that manifest difference which distinguishes different writers". But if the letter is canonical, Peter must be its author, because a pseudonym "would have been a fiction unworthy of a minister of Christ". He concludes that it "must have proceeded from Peter; not that he himself wrote it, but that some one of his disciples set forth in writing, by his command" what was needed, near Peter's death. And he will not reject it: "to repudiate it is what I dread". For broadcast, the summary is that Calvin accepted it as Peter's apostolic letter, possibly written down by a disciple at his command, and he refused the idea of a forgery. He was not a doubter of its canonicity.

**Date and place.** If Peter died under Nero (Nero died A.D. 68), the letter falls about A.D. 64-68 and is probably from Rome.
- MacArthur: "The epistle may have been written just before his death (1:14; ca. A.D. 67–68)" (pages/pet-macarthur-intro.txt, https://blueletterbible.org/Comm/macarthur_john/bible-introductions/2peter-intro.cfm; MODERN, COPYRIGHTED).
- Ligonier gives "ca. 65 A.D." (P-49).
- Jerome says Peter died in "the fourteenth, year of Nero" (pages/pet-jerome-vir.txt). That is A.D. 67/68.

**Recipients.** They are the same churches as 1 Peter (3:1): Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia and Bithynia (1 Pet 1:1), largely Gentile congregations in Asia Minor (Ligonier, P-49 context).

**Purpose and the problem.** False teachers will arise "among you" (2:1). They deny the Master who bought them, they are licentious (2:2, 10, 18-19), and they scoff at the Lord's return (3:3-4). Peter answers with three things:
1. True knowledge (epignōsis) of God and Christ produces godliness (ch. 1).
2. Exposure and judgment of the false teachers (ch. 2).
3. The certainty of the Day of the Lord (ch. 3).

Calvin states the design as perseverance in one's calling (P-7). MacArthur describes the letter's purpose as exposing and defeating false teachers (pages/pet-macarthur-intro.txt).

**Structure.**
- 1:1-2 greeting.
- 1:3-11 God's provision and the call to growth (today).
- 1:12-21 Peter's testament: reminder, Transfiguration, prophetic word (tomorrow: 1:12, established in the truth, ἐστηριγμένους, which links to the previous episode's sterizō in Luke 22:32 and 1 Pet 5:10).
- 2:1-22 false teachers.
- 3:1-13 the scoffers and the Day of the Lord.
- 3:14-18 closing. "Grow in the grace and knowledge" (3:18) brackets the letter with 1:2-3.

**Greek notes.**
- *isotimos* (1:1): ESV "of equal standing", KJV "like precious". It is used only here in the NT. Meyer: it "means: “having equal honour or worth.”" (pages/pet-bh11.txt). Calvin's comment is P-8. Spurgeon calls it "a blessed equality" (P-38).
- *"our God and Savior Jesus Christ"* (1:1): one article (τοῦ θεοῦ ἡμῶν καὶ σωτῆρος Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ), the Granville Sharp construction. The parallel phrase "our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ" (1:11; 2:20; 3:18) clearly refers to one person.
  - For one person: Poole (P-25), Gill (pages/pet-bh11.txt: "of Christ Jesus, who is our God and Saviour: so that here is a testimony of the deity of Christ"), and Calvin's editor (pages/pet-calvin101.txt, footnote 145). Barnes leans that way.
  - For two persons: Cambridge (Plumptre) prefers referring "God" to the Father. Calvin himself, in the text of his comment, ascribes the righteousness "in common to God and to Christ".
  - Spurgeon preaches it as Christ's deity: "the glory of the Godhead" softened by "our Savior" (pages/pet-spurg551.txt).
  - The ESV/NIV/CSB all read "our God and Savior Jesus Christ."
- *epichorēgeō* (1:5, "supplement"; also 1:11, "richly provided"). It comes from the chorēgos, the wealthy citizen who paid for a civic chorus. Barnes and JFB use the chorus image (P-27; JFB: "said properly of the one who supplied all the equipments of a chorus", pages/pet-bh15.txt). Gill says faith "leads the van", or rather the chorus. The Pulpit cautions that in usage the word "came to mean simply to" supply or provide (P-28). Tie-in: God lavishly "supplies" an entrance (1:11) to those who "supply" virtue (1:5).
- *theias koinōnoi physeōs* (1:4), "partakers of the divine nature". See Q2 below.
- *bebaios* (1:10): sure, firm, confirm. EGT: "The word has a legal sense", a guarantee of title (P-29). Meyer: the making sure "can apply only to something which has been realiter accomplished in man, not to the decree of God in itself unchangeable and eternal" (pages/pet-bh110.txt).
- *ptaiō* (1:10): ESV "never fall"; the verb means to stumble.

## B2. Outline with key verses

Scripture quotations are ESV (pages/esv-2pet1.txt).

1. **Greeting (1:1-2).** Peter writes "To those who have obtained a faith of equal standing with ours by the righteousness of our God and Savior Jesus Christ". He prays, "May grace and peace be multiplied to you in the knowledge of God and of Jesus our Lord."
2. **God's provision (1:3-4).** "His divine power has granted to us all things that pertain to life and godliness, through the knowledge of him who called us to his own glory and excellence". Through "his precious and very great promises", "you may become partakers of the divine nature, having escaped from the corruption that is in the world because of sinful desire."
3. **Our diligence: the ladder of graces (1:5-7).** "For this very reason, make every effort to supplement your faith with virtue..." The ladder runs from faith through virtue, knowledge, self-control, steadfastness, godliness and brotherly affection, up to love.
4. **Fruitfulness versus blindness (1:8-9).** These qualities keep you "from being ineffective or unfruitful". Whoever lacks them "is so nearsighted that he is blind, having forgotten that he was cleansed from his former sins."
5. **Assurance and entrance (1:10-11).** "Therefore, brothers, be all the more diligent to confirm your calling and election, for if you practice these qualities you will never fall." Verse 11: "For in this way there will be richly provided for you an entrance into the eternal kingdom of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ."

The structure is grace first (1:3-4), "for this very reason" effort (1:5-9), and "therefore" assurance (1:10-11). This is the indicative-imperative order Piper stresses (P-52).

## B3. Interpretive questions with voices

**Q1. "faith of equal standing" (1:1). Is the faith equal in measure?**
- Calvin: equal not in measure, but because "all possess by faith the same Christ" (P-8).
- Henry: faith "unites the weak believer to Christ as really as it does the strong one" (P-22).
- Spurgeon: "the poorest Little-Faith" has a faith like Peter's (P-38, P-39).
- Hyde (Ligonier) on the Gentile/Jewish background (Gal 2): Peter, who once withdrew from Gentiles, now writes this (pages/pet-lig-art-predestination.txt).

**Q2. "Partakers of the divine nature" (1:4). Deification?**
- *Fathers.* Athanasius: "He was made man that we might be made God" (P-19). Irenaeus: "become what we are, that He might bring us to be even what He is Himself" (P-20; also 3.19.1, P-21, adoption).
- *Calvin* uses the strong language, "to deify us" (P-10). He immediately qualifies it: "nature is not here essence but quality". He rejects the Manichees and the "fanatics" (he probably means Osiander and the Libertines) who think God's nature swallows ours (P-11). He then makes it concrete as the restored image in holiness and righteousness, ending in glory (pages/pet-calvin101.txt).
- *Reformed guardrails.* Poole: "not by any communication of the Divine essence" but by divine qualities, the image of Eph 4:24 and Col 3:10 (P-24). Gill: "not essentially... so as to be deified, this is impossible" (P-26). Henry: renewal after the image of God (P-23).
- *Union with Christ.* Spurgeon denies that we become God ("a gulf fixed in respect of essence") and then roots it in union with Christ: members of his body, married to him (P-40, P-41).
- *Modern Reformed.* Ligonier: "we are not absorbed into God's being" (pages/pet-lig-his-divine-power.txt).
- *The means are the promises.* Piper: "the only knowledge of God that carries saving power is promising knowledge" (P-55). Spurgeon on meditating on the promises: P-42, P-43.
- *Confessional guardrail.* WCF 26.3: communion with Christ "doth not make them in any wise partakers of the substance of his Godhead" (P-56).

**Q3. Does the ladder of 1:5-7 mean synergism, or that we make ourselves holy?**
- Calvin: "it is not here... a gradation as to the sense", and faith is never naked (P-13). He also denies that the command proves free will: "all our progress and perseverance are from God" (pages/pet-calvin105.txt; also cited in Ligonier's 'Supplement Your Faith').
- Piper: "We labor for virtue because God has already labored for us" (P-52). The swimmer illustration follows (P-53).
- Ligonier: faith first and love last; love is "both our final goal and the virtue that must undergird all the others" (pages/pet-lig-supplement-your-faith.txt).

**Q4. "confirm your calling and election" (1:10). Sure to whom? Does assurance rest on works?**
- *Calvin.* The verse speaks of "the sign or the effect", not the cause. Holiness is "the evidence and proof of election", yet believers "fix their solid foundation on something else" (P-16). He attacks the licentious abuse of election (P-17). On why calling comes first, he says the visible effect is named before its cause.
- *Spurgeon.* The verse means sure "Not towards God, for they are sure to him: make them sure to yourself" (P-44). Calling comes first "to us" (P-47). He does not coddle doubt: "Scripture never says" that the doubter shall be saved (P-45), and "Idle men have no right to assurance" (P-46).
- *Westminster Confession 18.3* cites this text directly: "give all diligence to make his calling and election sure". It says assurance produces joy, love and "cheerfulness in the duties of obedience... so far is it from inclining men to looseness" (P-30). WCF 18.2 grounds assurance first in "the divine truth of the promises" (P-31).
- *Puritans.* Watson: "Sanctification is the seed, assurance is the flower" (P-32). The sealed letter (P-33). The practical syllogism (P-34). Brooks: assurance is worth labor, for "There are five things that God will never sell at a cheap rate" (P-35 to P-37).
- *Moderns.* Ligonier: a "reciprocal relationship between assurance and fruit" (P-50). Hyde: we only "confirm" what God has done (P-51; the source prints an odd quote mark). Piper: lack of diligence "may be a sign that we were never called". "The confirmation of your election is your progress in sanctification" (P-54).
- *Greek angle.* bebaios as a legal guarantee (P-29). Meyer on "realiter" (B1 above).

**Q5. "you will never fall" and the "rich entrance" (1:10-11). Rewards, or entrance itself?**
- Calvin: never fall "because sure and sufficient is the grace of God by which they are supported". The entrance is "a passage of hope" with "a long journey" remaining (P-18).
- Henry: those growing in grace "shall have an abundant entrance into the joy of their Lord" (pages/pet-mhcw1.txt; quoted in the Ligonier devotional 'Good Works and Assurance').
- Piper: the danger in 1:8-9 "is not the danger of slipping into the kingdom with no rewards. It is the danger of not being saved at all" (pages/pet-piper-confirm.txt).
- Connect the "richly provided" of 1:11 with the "supplement" of 1:5. It is the same verb, epichorēgeō.

**Q6. Forgetting one's cleansing (1:9).**
- Calvin: "the blood of Christ has not become a washing bath to us, that it may be fouled by our filth" (P-15).
- Piper: blindness in two directions, a haze over the future and a past forgiveness forgotten (pages/pet-piper-confirm.txt).
