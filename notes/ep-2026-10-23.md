# Research: Jeremiah 52 (the historical appendix that closes the book)

## 1. Setting and background

**Where we are in the book.** Jeremiah 51:64 ends with the colophon "Thus far are the words of Jeremiah." Chapter 52 is then set apart as an appendix. Except for 52:28-30, it runs almost word for word with 2 Kings 24:18-25:30. It leaves out the Gedaliah episode (2 Kings 25:22-26), which Jeremiah 40-43 has already told at length, and it puts the deportation register (52:28-30) in that slot. It overlaps with Jeremiah 39:1-10, but adds details: the princes slain at Riblah (52:10), Zedekiah kept in prison "till the day of his death" (52:11), the fuller vessel list and the pomegranate count (52:19-23), and the three deportation numbers.

**Who added it?** Nobody knows, and the commentators say so openly.
- Calvin did not lecture on chapter 52 at all. The CCEL editor's note says "as it is historical, and all its parts have been elsewhere handled, holy Calvin did not expound in his Lectures." On 51:64 Calvin argues that the scribe who collected Jeremiah's prophecies added "Thus far the words of Jeremiah," and he concludes that "the last chapter is not included in the prophetic book of Jeremiah," that it contains history "as far as was necessary to understand what is here taught."
- Keil & Delitzsch think Jeremiah 52 and 2 Kings 24-25 both draw on a common, fuller source. They note that this chapter spells the name "Nebuchadrezzar" where Kings has "Nebuchadnezzar," and that it has material Kings lacks. Their suggestion: "Baruch, as the editor of the collection of Jeremiah's prophecies, may have made the extract."
- JFB and Adam Clarke say "probably Ezra." Poole and Henry say an inspired person among the exiles wrote it. Poole works out the arithmetic: Jeremiah would have been "near a hundred years old" by Jehoiachin's death.
- Henry and Clarke both say the chapter serves as an introduction or "key" to Lamentations. Clarke reports that his old Wycliffite manuscript Bible runs straight on from 52:34 into Lamentations with a line about Jeremiah sitting and weeping.

**Dates (Babylonian chronology, with the usual caveat that some date the fall to 587).**
- 52:4: Siege begins in the 9th year of Zedekiah, 10th month (Tebet), 10th day. That is January 588 BC (also Ezekiel 24:1-2). This became the Fast of the Tenth Month (Zechariah 8:19; Gill).
- 52:5-7: The famine peaks and the wall is breached in the 11th year, 4th month (Tammuz), 9th day. That is July 586, roughly 18 months of siege. This became the Fast of the Fourth Month (Gill; Henry).
- 52:12: Nebuzaradan comes and burns the temple in the 5th month (Ab), on the **10th** day, in Nebuchadnezzar's 19th year. That is August 586. 2 Kings 25:8 says the **7th** day. The explanations:
  - (a) A copyist's error, since the Hebrew letters used as numerals for 7 (zayin) and 10 (yod) are easily confused. This is the view of the Pulpit Commentary, Ellicott on Kings, and Blayney in the Calvin translator's note. Keil & Delitzsch call it a copying slip and say "it cannot now be decided which is correct."
  - (b) Both dates are true. He arrived on the 7th and burned on the 10th (Poole), or he set out from Riblah on the 7th and arrived on the 10th (JFB).
  - (c) The Talmud (Taanit 29a) takes both as true: the gentiles entered on the 7th, desecrated the temple on the 7th and 8th, and set the fire toward evening on the 9th, and it burned through the 10th. Rabbi Yohanan said he would have fixed the fast on the 10th, but the sages chose the 9th "to mark the beginning of the tragedy." Hence **Tisha B'Av** (the 9th of Av). Ellicott adds that, according to Josephus, the Second Temple burned on the same day of the same month (Wars 6.4.8).
- **Book-closing frame.** Jeremiah 1:3 says Jeremiah's word came "until the captivity of Jerusalem in the fifth month." 52:12 is that fifth month. The Cambridge Bible on 1:3 cross-references "ch. 52:12 ff." The book opens with the almond rod (shaqed) and "I am watching (shoqed) over my word to perform it" (1:11-12). It closes with the dated record of how the word was performed, and then with a promise-shaped coda (Jehoiachin). Calvin on 1:12 insists that God's watching covers "not only... punishments, but also... promises," which suits an ending that moves from judgment (52:1-30) to a gleam of grace (52:31-34). Henry's summary is "no word of God shall fall to the ground." Episode 4 already drew out the shoqed echo in 44:27, so here it can be used briefly as the bookend.

**The temple vessels (52:17-23).**
- **The pillars of bronze** are Jachin ("He will establish") and Boaz ("In him is strength"), from 1 Kings 7:15-22. Henry reads their breaking as a sign that God would no longer establish His house. They were 18 cubits high (about 27 feet), 12 cubits around, four fingers thick, and hollow. Each capital was 5 cubits. 2 Kings 25:17 has 3 cubits; Poole harmonizes this by saying Kings measures only part of the capital. Each had 96 pomegranates "on the sides" and 100 in all, a detail found nowhere else (Keil & Delitzsch).
- **The bronze sea and the twelve bulls.** 2 Kings 16:17 records that Ahaz took the sea down off the bronze oxen and set it on a stone pavement. Gill and the Pulpit Commentary think Hezekiah probably restored it, which is why 52:20 can still list "the twelve bronze bulls." Ellicott notes that 2 Kings 25:16 omits the bulls and suggests Ahaz may have given the bronze to Tiglath-pileser. The text simply lists them, and "the bronze of all these things was beyond weight," exactly as when Solomon made them (1 Kings 7:47; Henry; Gill).
- **Jeremiah predicted this very list.** Jeremiah 27:19-22 names "the pillars, the sea, the stands, and the rest of the vessels." He said these would go to Babylon "until the day when I visit them," against the false prophets, who said the vessels already taken would soon come back (27:16; Hananiah in 28:3). Chapter 52 is the receipt. Henry says, "When the gold was gone, the brass soon went after it... according to Jeremiah's prediction, ch. xxvii. 19."
- **The return.** Ezra 1:7-11 records Cyrus bringing out the gold and silver vessels (5,400 in all) and counting them out to Sheshbazzar, "the prince of Judah." Older commentators identify him with Zerubbabel (Keil & Delitzsch, Gill, the Geneva note). Many moderns identify him with Shenazzar, Jehoiachin's son (1 Chronicles 3:18). Either way a Davidide carried them home. The great bronze pieces were broken up and never returned. This thread runs through earlier episodes: in 51:44 Bel is made to disgorge what he swallowed, and Ellicott, Benson, JFB and Poole on 51:44 all tie that to the temple vessels and to Ezra 1:7.

**Seraiah and Zephaniah (52:24).**
- **Seraiah the chief priest** is not the Seraiah of 51:59; Poole says so. He is the son of Azariah (1 Chronicles 6:14-15) and the ancestor of Ezra (Ezra 7:1, "Ezra the son of Seraiah"). Ellicott says "grandfather or great-grandfather"; Gill says "supposed to be the father." Nebuchadnezzar executed him at Riblah, yet his line produced the scribe who would teach the law to the returned exiles.
- **Zephaniah the "second priest"** (the high priest's deputy; the Targum calls him the sagan, per Gill) appears in Jeremiah 21:1 and 37:3 (sent by Zedekiah to ask Jeremiah to pray) and in 29:25-29. There, a false prophet in Babylon writes demanding that Zephaniah put Jeremiah in the stocks, and Zephaniah reads the letter to Jeremiah instead. He was a sympathetic hearer who never quite obeyed (Poole and Ellicott give the cross-references).
- The 72 men executed (2 Kings 25:18-21 has "five" of the king's council where Jeremiah has "seven") are, in Henry's phrase, "made monuments of divine justice." Lightfoot's odd reconciliation, quoted by Henry, is that the other two were Jeremiah and Ebed-melech, who were released.

**The deportation numbers (52:28-30), found only in Jeremiah and absent from the LXX:**
- 7th year of Nebuchadnezzar: 3,023 "Judeans."
- 18th year: 832 "from Jerusalem."
- 23rd year (582/581): 745, taken by Nebuzaradan.
- Total: 4,600.

2 Kings 24:14-16, for the 597 deportation, has 10,000, or 7,000 fighting men plus 1,000 craftsmen. The options:
- (1) **Different groups or subsets.** The 3,023 were men of Judah proper and the rest were Benjamin and other tribes (Gill, JFB, Poole citing the English Annotations). Or the figures count adult males only (the Calvin translator's note, which estimates "not... less than fifty thousand" in all with families). Or it is a precise register against Kings' round numbers (Ellicott).
- (2) **Emend "seventh" to "seventeenth."** Then 52:28 would be captives taken during the siege, before the city fell (Cambridge, Barnes, and the Pulpit Commentary following Ewald and Keil). But the **Babylonian Chronicle (ABC 5)** dates Jehoiachin's capture to "the second day of the month of Addaru" in Nebuchadnezzar's **seventh** year, that is, 16 March 597. So Jeremiah 52:28's "seventh year" matches Babylonian reckoning exactly (Livius notes it "contradicts the date in 2 Kings 24.12 (the eighth year)"). That undercuts the emendation. Kings counts by Judean reckoning, or by when the deportation was completed; Gill, Henry and JFB said long ago that it was "in part about the end of the seventh year, in part about the beginning of the eighth." Barnes notes "In each date there is a difference of one year," because the register uses a different reckoning from Kings.
- (3) **The 23rd-year deportation (582)** is not narrated anywhere else. Suggested causes are reprisal for the murder of Gedaliah (Henry, though "this is uncertain"; Gill) or Nebuchadnezzar's campaign against Moab, Ammon and Egypt that year (Josephus, Antiquities 10.9.7, cited by Cambridge; Ellicott). Poole: "we are not certain."
- **The small totals** show how emptied the land already was. Ewald, quoted in the Pulpit Commentary, says they found "only 4600 men more whom they thought worth the trouble of transporting." Yet Ezra 2:64 counts 42,360 returnees. Henry says "as at first in Egypt, so again in Babylon, the Lord made them fruitful in the land of their affliction."

**"The poorest of the people" (52:15-16).**
- 52:15 says "some of the poorest of the people" were carried away, while 52:16 says "some of the poorest of the land" were left. Keil & Delitzsch and Ellicott treat the first phrase as a copyist's slip; it is absent from Kings and from Jeremiah 39:9. The Calvin translator's note says it is in all the manuscripts but one and concludes that "some of the poor of the city were evidently left, as well as some of the poor of the land."
- Policy: Poole says "a thing very usual with conquerors... that their conquests may yield them some revenue." Gill says the king wanted "some advantage by the conquest."
- The irony from 39:10 is that Nebuzaradan gave the poor "vineyards and fields." Those who had nothing inherited the land that the proud lost (compare Jeremiah 24's good and bad figs).

**Jehoiachin's release (52:31-34).**
- Jehoiachin (also Jeconiah or Coniah) became king at 18, reigned three months, surrendered in 597, and was taken to Babylon (2 Kings 24:8-15).
- In the "37th year of the exile," on the 25th of Adar (2 Kings 25:27 says the 27th; Poole, Gill and JFB say the decree was given on one day and carried out on the other), Evil-merodach "lifted up the head" of Jehoiachin. Evil-merodach is Amel-Marduk, Nebuchadnezzar's son, who reigned 562-560 and was murdered by his brother-in-law Neriglissar (Pulpit). The date is 562/561, early 561 or spring 560 depending on reckoning (Livius).
- Jehoiachin was then about 55 (Gill; Henry). Josephus says Evil-Merodach "esteemed him among his most intimate friends" because "his father had not kept his faith with Jeconiah, when he voluntarily delivered up himself."
- Why the release? Speculation runs from a Jewish tradition (Seder Olam; Gill; JFB) that Evil-merodach had himself been imprisoned by his father and befriended Jehoiachin in prison, to Irving Finkel's modern suggestion along the same lines (cited by Livius), to a general accession amnesty (Pulpit). Keil & Delitzsch: "We cannot make any safe conjecture as to the motives."

**Archaeology: the Jehoiachin ration tablets ("Weidner tablets").**
- Robert Koldewey's excavations at Babylon (1899-1917) found a royal storehouse with nearly 300 ration texts from Nebuchadnezzar's 10th to 35th years. It is a vaulted building at the northeast corner of the Southern Palace, once wrongly thought to be the base of the Hanging Gardens (COJS).
- Four texts list monthly oil rations for "Ya'u-kinu, king of the land of Yahudu" and his **five sons**. E.F. Weidner published them in 1939 (ANET 308; the tablets are in Berlin).
- The one dated tablet comes from Nebuchadnezzar's 13th year (592/1). So the ration lists date from *before* the 561 release. They show that Babylon still called him "king of Judah" and fed his household. The COJS page even infers that he was not imprisoned for the whole 37 years.
- Livius gives the lines: "10 sila of oil to... [Ia]-'-kin, king of Ia-[a-hu-du]; 2½ sila of oil to the [five so]ns of the king..." Note that Livius's phrase "after his release" is loose, since the dated tablet is from 592.
- The striking point for the episode is that the Bible ends with Jehoiachin's daily ration, and archaeologists dug up his ration slips.
- One caution: 1 Chronicles 3:17-18 lists seven sons. They may have been born later, or the "five" may be the sons alive in 592 (Livius discusses this).

**Key Hebrew.**
- **nasa' 'et-ro'sh**, "lifted up the head" (52:31; ESV "graciously freed"). This is the idiom of Genesis 40:13 and 20, where Pharaoh "lifted up the head" of the cupbearer, restoring him from prison, and of Psalm 3:3. Poole: "which signifies in Scripture the altering of one's estate that is in misery, Genesis 40:20."
- **bigde kil'o**, "his prison garments" (52:33). Compare Joseph changing his clothes before Pharaoh (Genesis 41:14, 42) and the robe of Luke 15:22 (Cambridge).
- **'aruchah**, "allowance" (52:34). This is a ration of food, a meal or allowance (BDB). The same word is used of the "allowance" (or food ration) Nebuzaradan gave *Jeremiah* in 40:5. So both the prophet and the king end up living on Babylon's ration.
- **tamid**, "continual" or "regular" (KJV "a continual diet"). It is the word for the temple's "continual" burnt offering. The temple and its daily tamid are gone, yet the son of David is given a tamid at a gentile table. That last point is a literary observation, not drawn from a quoted commentator.
- **devar-yom beyomo**, "a day's matter in its day" (YLT: "the matter of a day in its day"). This is the same phrase as the manna in Exodus 16:4 ("gather a day's portion every day"). Spurgeon's Morning and Evening reading builds on exactly this "daily portion." Jeremiah's version adds "until the day of his death." The Cambridge Bible thinks "all the days of his life," the last words, may have been added "to avoid the inauspicious ending with the word death." This is like the synagogue practice of re-reading a hopeful verse after the gloomy last verse of Lamentations, Isaiah and Malachi.

**Davidic hope thread.**
- **The promise:** 2 Samuel 7:14-16; Jeremiah 33:17 ("David shall never lack a man to sit on the throne").
- **The curse:** Jeremiah 22:24-30, where God would pull off Coniah "though he were the signet ring on my right hand." "Write this man down as childless" means childless *as a king*. He had sons (1 Chronicles 3:17; the ration tablets). Calvin says the curse was "confined to a temporary punishment, and extend only to the coming of Christ."
- **The reversal:** Haggai 2:23 takes Zerubbabel, Jehoiachin's grandson, son of Shealtiel, and makes him "like a signet ring." Calvin explicitly links this to Jeremiah 22:24.
- **The genealogy:** Matthew 1:11-12 names "Jechoniah... Shealtiel... Zerubbabel." Irenaeus uses Jeremiah 22 to argue that Christ was not Joseph's natural son. Ra McLaughlin (Third Millennium) argues instead that the curse was limited to Jeconiah's lifetime, so Matthew can trace Christ's throne-right *through* Jechoniah.

## 2. Outline

1. **52:1-3, Zedekiah's evil reign** (from 2 Kings 24:18-20). Key verse: "because of the anger of the LORD... he cast them out from his presence. And Zedekiah rebelled."
2. **52:4-11, Siege, breach, flight, and Riblah.** Key verses: 52:6 ("no food for the people of the land"); 52:11 ("put him in prison till the day of his death"), which is only in Jeremiah and pairs deliberately with 52:34.
3. **52:12-16, Temple, palace and city burned; walls broken; deportation; the poor left.** Key verse: 52:13.
4. **52:17-23, The temple's bronze broken and carried off, with the pillars described.** Key verse: 52:20 ("which Solomon the king had made... beyond weight").
5. **52:24-27, The priests and officials executed at Riblah.** Key verse: 52:27 ("So Judah was taken into exile out of its land").
6. **52:28-30, The deportation register (4,600).**
7. **52:31-34, Jehoiachin lifted up.** Key verses: 52:31-34, ending "until the day of his death, as long as he lived."

The structure is two prisoners and two endings. Zedekiah is in prison "till the day of his death" (52:11). Jehoiachin is at the king's table "until the day of his death" (52:34). The chapter that began with a king blinded ends with a king seated.

## 3. Interpretive questions

**Q1. Why end a prophetic book with history copied from Kings, and why give the last word to Jehoiachin rather than Jeremiah?**
- *Proof of fulfilment.* Henry: "History is the best expositor of prophecy." Clarke: "very properly subjoined... to show how exactly they were fulfilled." Cambridge: "to shew the fulfilment of that overthrow which Jeremiah had so persistently foretold." This ties back to 1:12 ("I am watching over my word").
- *The prophet's words are finished (51:64), so the rest is "history only."* This is Calvin. Keil & Delitzsch call it "an appendix to, the prophecies."
- *A bridge to Lamentations.* Henry and Poole say the chapter serves as "a key to it."
- *A memorandum for exiles.* Henry calls it a "constant memorandum to those who in Babylon preferred Jerusalem above their chief joy."
- *The last word goes to the Davidic line.* Ligonier: "chapter 52 ends not in devastated Jerusalem but rather in Babylon," which is "a hopeful sign that David's throne had not been forgotten." Carson, on the identical ending of Kings: "The story of redemption is not yet done."
- *Tension:* Jeremiah's own last recorded word (ch. 44) is judgment on the Judeans in Egypt. The book was arranged so that the final scene is grace in Babylon, which is where Jeremiah 24 and 29 said the future lay ("the good figs").

**Q2. Is Jehoiachin's release hope or a melancholy note?** There are real opposing views.
- *Hope:*
  - Keil & Delitzsch: "the gracious decree of God, that the seed of David... should not be utterly rejected (2 Samuel 7:14-15)... a comforting sign to the whole of the captive people."
  - The Pulpit Commentary: "a happy omen." Cambridge on Kings: the careful dating shows the exiles "regarded the act of Evil-merodach as a gleam of hope for all the nation."
  - The Geneva Bible note: "thus by God's providence the seed of David was preserved even to Christ."
  - Henry: "though the night of affliction be very long, yet we must not despair."
  - Carson: "hope still beckons."
  - Ligonier: "a sign that was hard to see... flickering and barely visible."
- *Melancholy or qualified:*
  - Joseph Parker, in the Biblical Illustrator: "Jehoiachin at his best was only a decorated captive; he was still in Babylon. That is the sting." Parker also says, "not a single word is said about his change of heart."
  - The Pulpit Commentary concedes the narrative has been "gloomy and dispiriting."
  - Jeremiah 22:26-27 ("you shall not return") is exactly fulfilled. He dies in Babylon, eating at a pagan king's table. Gill: "it is certain he did not live to the return."
  - Chrysostom, writing of Daniel and his friends' honours at the Babylonian court, says they "would have chosen ten thousand times rather to be outcasts at home, than kings in Babylon." That is the Psalm 84:10 instinct, and it applies uncomfortably well to Jehoiachin's seat "above the seats of the kings."
  - Modern critical scholarship is divided. Martin Noth famously saw the notice as adding nothing hopeful; von Rad saw a deliberate Davidic hope. This is reported in secondary summaries only (see the paraphrase notes); do not quote.
- *Synthesis:* Calvin on 22:30 gives the frame. The Davidic line was "for a time buried," and "the grace of God, then, was hidden for a time, but never extinguished." The ending is small on purpose. It is a mustard-seed sign, not the restoration. The real "lifting of the head" waits for the Son of David (Psalm 3:3; Matthew 1:12). Henry's typology says the believer's death is like Jehoiachin's release: shedding "that prison-garment" for "the table, of the King of kings."
- *A side debate on whether Jehoiachin deserved it:* JFB says "God, in his elevation, rewarded his having surrendered to Nebuchadnezzar" (compare Jeremiah 38:17). Parker calls him "in his heart a bad man" (2 Kings 24:9). The text itself gives no motive and no repentance; it presents pure, unexplained favour.

**Q3. The numbers and the poor left behind.** See the background section above. The main options are subsets (Judah only, males only), emendation to "seventeenth," or different calendars. The Babylonian Chronicle now supports "seventh year" (Babylonian reckoning) for 597. The small totals show a land already drained. Henry calls Israel "often miracles both of judgment and mercy," multiplying in exile. The poor left as vinedressers inherit the vineyards (39:10). Pastoral note: the remnant the Babylonians thought not "worth the trouble of transporting" (Ewald) were the ones God left on the land.

# Research: Song of Songs 5 (all 16 verses)

## 1. Setting and background

- **Where we are in the book.** 5:1 closes the Song's central movement (roughly 3:6-5:1: the royal procession, the groom's head-to-foot praise of the bride in 4:1-7, the "garden locked" in 4:12, her invitation "Let my beloved come to his garden" in 4:16, and his answer "I came to my garden" in 5:1). 5:2 begins a new movement (5:2-6:3 or 5:2-6:10) of loss, search and recovery, a darker echo of the night search in 3:1-4.
- **The center.** Commentators commonly treat 4:16-5:1 as the climax and formal center of the Song. A study outline on sermons.com says of 4:1-5:1 that "This section also contains the formal center of the book" (raw: work/raw/sermonscom_center.txt). The SIL/UBS translation notes call 4:16 and 5:1 the climax and say the chapter break at 5:1 is artificial (5:1 answers 4:16). Barnes: "The first half of the Song of Songs is fitly closed. The second half of the poem commences Sol 5:2 with a change of tone" (work/raw/bh_song5_1.txt). (I could NOT verify the often-repeated claim that there are exactly 111 Hebrew lines before and after 4:16-5:1; don't use the number on air. A safe line: "at about the midpoint of the Song.")
- **Plain sense of 5:1.** Keil & Delitzsch read 5:1 as the consummation of the marriage: "It supposes a union of love, such as is the conclusion of marriage following the betrothal, the God-ordained aim of sexual love within the limits fixed by morality" (bh_song5_1.txt). Pulpit: "The bridal night intervenes. The joy of the king in his bride is complete." So the center of the Song is the wedding night, blessed within marriage.
- **Hebrew words.**
  - *dodim* (5:1, "be drunk with love", ESV): the word used for "love/caresses" throughout the Song (1:2 "your love is better than wine"). The Cambridge Bible notes the debate: is it "lovers/beloved friends" (vocative) or "love/caresses" (object: "drink deep of love")? ESV takes the second sense ("be drunk with love"). *shakar* = drink deeply, be drunk/sated (Cambridge cites Haggai 1:6 to show it can mean drink to satiety).
  - *'er* (5:2, "my heart was awake"): from *'ur*, "to be awake/stir" (same root as "do not stir up or awaken love" 2:7; 3:5; 8:4). Keil: "To sleep while the heart wakes signifies to dream."
  - *dod* (beloved), the bride's word for him 5:2, 5:4, 5:5, 5:6 (x2), 5:8, 5:9 (x4), 5:10, 5:16: the chapter hammers the word.
  - *dagul* (5:10, ESV "distinguished," KJV "chiefest"): literally "bannered," lifted up like a standard (cf. *degel*, banner, 2:4). "Among ten thousand" = a myriad.
  - *machamaddim* (5:16, ESV "altogether desirable," KJV "altogether lovely"): plural abstract of *chamad*, to desire. Literally "he is all desires / desirablenesses." Flavel and Spurgeon both comment on this (see quotes).
  - *re'a* (5:16, "friend"): "This is my beloved and this is my friend": lover and companion. Same root as *ra'yati* "my love/companion" (5:2).
  - Veil (5:7): *redid*, a light outer wrap/mantle (Keil: a thin upper robe, LXX *theristron* "summer overdress"), not her underclothing.
- **Culture.** Ancient cities had night watchmen making rounds and others posted on the walls (Gill and Poole distinguish the two). A respectable woman alone in the streets at night would be suspected as a prostitute (cf. Prov 7:10-12), which helps explain the beating on the plain-sense level. Myrrh on the hands (5:5) fits perfumed lovers (cf. Prov 7:17; Esther 2:12). Ivory, gold, sapphire (lapis lazuli), alabaster/marble columns, cedars of Lebanon (5:14-15): temple and palace materials (1 Kings 6-7; 10:18), so her description of him reads almost like a description of a statue or a building: precious, solid, royal.
- **The wasf.** 5:10-16 is the only head-to-toe praise song (Arabic *wasf*) spoken by the woman about the man (he describes her in 4:1-7; 6:4-9; 7:1-9). Head (gold) to legs (alabaster on gold bases) to overall bearing (Lebanon) to mouth (sweet), then the summary.
- **Calvin wrote no commentary on the Song. Do not quote Calvin here.**

## 2. Outline

1. **5:1 The feast at the center.** He: "I came to my garden, my sister, my bride... I ate my honeycomb with my honey, I drank my wine with my milk." Then: "Eat, friends, drink, and be drunk with love!"
2. **5:2-8 The night knock and the lost beloved.**
   - 5:2 "I slept, but my heart was awake. A sound! My beloved is knocking. 'Open to me, my sister, my love, my dove, my perfect one, for my head is wet with dew.'"
   - 5:3 Her excuses: "I had put off my garment; how could I put it on? I had bathed my feet; how could I soil them?"
   - 5:4-5 His hand at the latch; her heart thrilled; she rises, hands dripping myrrh on the bolt.
   - 5:6 "I opened to my beloved, but my beloved had turned and gone. My soul failed me when he spoke. I sought him, but found him not; I called him, but he gave no answer."
   - 5:7 "The watchmen found me... they beat me, they bruised me, they took away my veil."
   - 5:8 "I adjure you, O daughters of Jerusalem... tell him I am sick with love."
3. **5:9 The daughters' question.** "What is your beloved more than another beloved, O most beautiful among women?"
4. **5:10-16 Her answer, the portrait.** "My beloved is radiant and ruddy, distinguished among ten thousand"... head of gold, eyes like doves, lips like lilies, arms rods of gold, body polished ivory, legs alabaster columns, appearance like Lebanon. "His mouth is most sweet, and he is altogether desirable. This is my beloved and this is my friend, O daughters of Jerusalem."
   - Notice the turn: the question that was meant to dismiss him ("more than another?") becomes the occasion of her richest praise, and by 6:1 the daughters want to seek him with her.

## 3. Interpretive questions

### (1) Is 5:2-8 a dream, and what is the "fault" of her delay?
- **Dream reading.** Barnes: "I sleep, but my heart waketh - A poetical periphrasis for 'I dream.'" Keil & Delitzsch: sleeping while the heart wakes "signifies to dream." Cambridge treats 5:2-7 as a troubled dream narrated next morning. On the plain-sense level this is a lover's anxiety-dream after the wedding: the fear of losing the one you love by your own hesitation. That is realistic married love, not only honeymoon bliss.
- **The fault.** Her excuses in 5:3 are almost comic (I've undressed, I've washed my feet). Taylor calls it "slothful self-indulgence" and adds that when she finally rises she is more taken up with anointing herself with myrrh than with welcoming him. Sibbes reads it as the drowsiness of a true believer: "I sleep" is a real confession, "but my heart waketh" an honest acknowledgment of grace; he distinguishes a "fit" of sleepiness from the "state" of a Christian. Sibbes: "NATURALLY we are prone to delays in heavenly things, and then to cover all with excuses." He also insists the fault is ours, not Christ's: Christ "delights not in strangeness."
- **Disagreement.** Gregory of Nyssa does NOT read the delay as a fault at all. In his reading the bride's undressing and washing are stages of her ascent (putting off the "tunics of skin"), and every frustration (seeking and not finding, calling and not answered) shows that God is always beyond what the soul has grasped: *epektasis*, endless straining forward (Phil 3:13). So Sibbes and Taylor see backsliding and loving chastening; Gregory sees spiritual progress.
- **Restraint.** Even Sibbes, the most detailed allegorist here, says on 5:10: "We will take that which is safe, because we will have sure footing, as near as we can, in this mystical portion of Scripture." Good bridge to the podcast's plain-sense-first position.

### (2) Why do the watchmen beat her (they helped in 3:3), and who are they?
- **Plain sense.** In 3:3 the watchmen simply "found" her; she asked and moved on. Here they beat her, bruise her, and take her outer wrap. A woman out alone at night is treated as a streetwalker. The scene intensifies the cost of the lost moment at the door.
- **Sibbes (and Poole, Gill): the watchmen are ministers who wound.** Ministers are "watchmen for souls" (Heb 13:17), but here "Those that should have been defensive prove most offensive." He applies it to false or harsh church leaders: "Now, for shepherds to become wolves, for watchmen to become smiters, what a pitiful thing is it!" He adds a warning not to judge people by the disgrace thrown on them: "Truth hath always a scratched face."
- **Gregory of Nyssa: the watchmen are angels, and the wound is good.** "Who, then, are the watchmen? Who indeed, but the servants of the one who guards Israel," the "ministering spirits" of Heb 1:14. Being struck is "the happiest thing possible"; the blow is the Spirit's rod that heals, and losing the veil reveals her beauty. He compares her to the rock Moses struck, from which water flowed.
- **Hudson Taylor: a middle view.** The watchmen are rebukers who "appear to have appreciated the gravity of her declension more correctly than she had done." Not wolves, not angels, but people who saw her inconsistency.
- **Rutherford** applies 5:6-7 to the persecuted Church of Scotland: because the Beloved was not let in "when He stood at the door, with His wet and frozen head," He makes us seek Him a while, and meanwhile the watchmen strike "the poor woman."
- So: three readings of the same verse. Restraint: on the plain-sense level, note the contrast with 3:3; on the spiritual level, the safest point (shared by Sibbes, Taylor, and Gregory) is that the one who has lost the sense of Christ's presence often meets bruising, even from the religious, and must keep seeking.

### (3) Who speaks "Eat, friends, drink, and be drunk with love" (5:1)?
- **Options.** (a) The groom, continuing to speak, inviting the wedding guests (Barnes: "A salutation from the king to his assembled guests"; Cambridge, following Ewald, favors an invitation to the marriage feast). (b) A third voice, the daughters of Jerusalem (Ginsburg) or the poet to the couple (Hitzig), blessing the union. (c) If *dodim* is the object ("drink deeply of love"), the line may be addressed to the lovers themselves: "be drunk with love." The SIL notes list both possibilities.
- **A theological reading of (b).** Since only God (or the narrator speaking with the narrator's authority) could pronounce such a blessing over the wedding night, some modern readers hear the voice as the divine approval of married love at the very center of the book. This is a reasonable inference, not something any of my verified sources states; present it as a possibility.
- **Puritan reading.** Sibbes and Poole: Christ speaks to the church and her friends, inviting them to the feast of the gospel (word and sacraments). Sibbes: "Christ provides a banquet, and invites his friends, not his enemies." Henry (Concise): "He also invites his beloved people to eat and drink abundantly."

# Research: John 6:41-59 (the grumbling, the Father's drawing, the flesh and blood)

## 1. Setting and background

- **Place and time.** The discourse is set in the synagogue at Capernaum (6:59), the day after the feeding of the 5,000 and the walk on the water (6:22-25). The Passover was near (6:4), so the crowd's minds are already on Exodus: manna, wilderness, lamb. Capernaum was Jesus' Galilean base. The black basalt foundation under the white limestone synagogue ruin (a later, 4th-5th century building) is widely held to be from the 1st-century synagogue: a vivid detail for "he said these things in the synagogue."
- **Where we are in John.** This is the second half of the Bread of Life discourse (6:25-59). Yesterday's section (6:25-40) set out "I am the bread of life" and the Father's giving of people to the Son. Today the crowd ("the Jews", 6:41, 52) murmurs, as Israel murmured over manna in Exodus 16. Jesus answers by making the claim *harder*: first the Father's drawing (6:44), then "my flesh" (6:51), then eat flesh and drink blood (6:53). The fallout comes next (6:60-71: "this is a hard saying", many leave, Peter's "to whom shall we go?").
- **The OT background.**
  - Murmuring (Greek *gonguzo*, 6:41, 43) echoes the wilderness grumbling over manna (Exod 16:2, 7-8; Num 11).
  - 6:45 quotes Isaiah 54:13, "All your children shall be taught by the LORD" (in the context of the restoration of Zion after the Servant's suffering in Isaiah 53). Calvin notes this is about "the restoration of the Church."
  - The blood prohibition (Gen 9:4; Lev 17:10-14) is why 6:53 shocks. Ellicott counts seven Pentateuch passages forbidding eating blood. Leviticus 17:11 also says "the blood makes atonement." That is the clue to Jesus' meaning: his blood is atonement.
- **Key Greek words.**
  - *helkuo* (draw, 6:44; also 12:32 "draw all people", 18:10 Peter *drew* his sword, 21:6 and 21:11 *haul* the net, Acts 16:19 Paul and Silas *dragged* into the marketplace, James 2:6 *drag* into court). Thayer (via Blue Letter Bible) lists both "forcibly draw against his will" (Acts 16:19) and, figuratively, "draw by inward power, lead, impel" (John 6:44). LXX usage includes Jeremiah 31:3 ("with lovingkindness have I *drawn* you") and Song 1:4 ("*draw* me after you"), which Spurgeon and Augustine both use.
  - *phago* (aorist "eat", 6:50-53) shifts to *trogo* in 6:54, 56, 57, 58. Strong's/Thayer gloss *trogo* as "to gnaw, crunch, chew", originally of animals feeding and of people eating raw fruit or nuts. It appears six times in the NT: four in John 6 (6:54, 56, 57, 58), once in John 13:18 ("he who *ate* my bread has lifted his heel against me"), and once in Matthew 24:38. **Caution:** Meyer's NT Commentary (on biblehub 6:54) says the change carries "no special intention as if to use a stronger term (to chew, to crunch)", because by NT times *trogo* often just served as a present-tense form of eating. So the host can mention the vividness but should not overstate it.
  - *alethes* (6:55, "my flesh is *true* food", "*real* food" in some manuscripts/translations; KJV "meat indeed"). This is real, genuine food, as opposed to the manna, which could not stop death.
  - *meno* (abide, 6:56): "abides in me, and I in him." It points ahead to John 15.
- **Westminster Confession 29.7** (fetched from opc.org) gives the Reformed position on the Supper: believers "inwardly by faith, really and indeed, yet not carnally and corporally but spiritually, receive, and feed upon, Christ crucified". See the quote bank if the host wants the exact wording.

## 2. Outline

1. **6:41-42 The grumbling.** "Is not this Jesus, the son of Joseph?" They are offended by the Incarnation's lowliness. (Calvin and Ryle both make this point.)
2. **6:43-46 The Father's drawing.** "No one can come to me unless the Father who sent me draws him. And I will raise him up on the last day" (6:44). "They will all be taught by God" (6:45, Isa 54:13). Everyone who has heard and learned from the Father comes.
3. **6:47-51 Manna and death; the living bread.** "Your fathers ate the manna in the wilderness, and they died" (6:49). "The bread that I will give for the life of the world is my flesh" (6:51). This is the first explicit pointer to his sacrificial death.
4. **6:52 The dispute.** "How can this man give us his flesh to eat?"
5. **6:53-58 The intensification.** "Unless you eat the flesh of the Son of Man and drink his blood, you have no life in you" (6:53). Then the switch to *trogo* (whoever "feeds on" my flesh). Flesh is true food, blood true drink (6:55). Abiding (6:56). "As the living Father sent me, and I live because of the Father, so whoever feeds on me, he also will live because of me" (6:57).
6. **6:59 Setting note.** "In the synagogue, as he taught at Capernaum."

## 3. Interpretive questions

### Q1. What does "draw" (6:44) mean? Compel, woo, or something else?
- **Sproul (Ligonier sermon "Bread of Life," 2002):** This is the strongest view. He says Kittel's dictionary renders the word "compel". The same word is used of Paul and Silas being dragged (Acts 16). He tells the story of debating an Arminian NT professor who cited *helkuo* for drawing water from a well. Sproul answered that water is inert and cannot be wooed. Anti-"woo".
- **Ligonier devotional "Man's Radical Fallenness" (unsigned Ligonier devotional):** It cites Acts 16:19 and James 2:6: "He drags us, against our fallen wills, to Jesus."
- **Calvin (Commentary on 6:44):** He takes a middle register. The drawing is "not violent, so as to compel men by external force", "but still it is a powerful impulse of the Holy Spirit, which makes men willing who formerly were unwilling". He calls it "false and profane" that only the already-willing are drawn.
- **Spurgeon ("Human Inability," No. 182, 7 March 1858, Music Hall, Royal Surrey Gardens):** He explicitly denies "compulsion whatever" and "drag[ging] people ... by the hair of their heads". Instead God "draws them by the heart quite as powerfully". He makes them willing and cites Ralph Erskine: "with full consent against his will." **Note the tension of vocabulary with Sproul and Ligonier ("drag", "compel") vs Spurgeon ("no compulsion"). Both mean effectual grace; they differ on whether "drag" is the right picture.** That makes a good on-air moment.
- **Augustine (Tractate 26):** "Drawn by delight". The green twig held out to a sheep, nuts shown to a child. "He delights by teaching, not by imposing a necessity." Yet he also says "Noble excellence of grace! No man comes unless drawn," and "why He draws one and draws not another, do not desire to judge." The drawing is sovereign *and* sweet.
- **Chrysostom (Homily 46):** He guards free will against the Manichaeans: "the words do not take away our free will, but show that we greatly need assistance." On 6:45 he reads "all" as "all that have the will." This is closer to the "enabling, not effectual" reading.
- **Cyril of Alexandria (Commentary on John, Book 4):** Verse 45 "shews that it is not a compulsory nor forcible drawing."
- **Ryle (Expository Thoughts, 6:41-51):** "His inability is not physical, but moral... It is not true that he would come if he could. It is true that he could come if he would." The Father's drawing supplies "a new will."
- **John 12:32 ("I will draw all people to myself"):** This is the standard objection to the effectual reading (if *helkuo* = effectual, universalism?). Reformed answer (Calvin elsewhere, Sproul): "all people" = all kinds of people, Gentiles as well as Jews (Greeks have just come asking for Jesus in 12:20-21). I did not fetch a quote on 12:32 specifically, so state it as a paraphrase.
- **Synthesis for the host:** Every voice agrees the drawing is necessary (6:44 "no one can"). The Reformed voices (Calvin, Spurgeon, Sproul, Ryle) agree it is effectual: all who are taught come (6:45), and the one drawn is raised (6:44b). They disagree only on imagery. The Greek fathers Chrysostom and Cyril stress that it is non-coercive and leave room for the will. Augustine anticipates the Reformed synthesis: sovereign grace that works *through* delight, not against the will.

### Q2. Is 6:53-56 about the Lord's Supper?
- **Sacramental reading (fathers):**
  - *Chrysostom (Homily 46)* explicitly says Jesus "introduced the discourse on the Mysteries" (= the Eucharist). Christ "has kneaded up His body with ours". He does allow in 6:48 that "bread" can mean "either His saving doctrines and the faith which is in Him, or His own Body."
  - *Cyril of Alexandria* says the "Mystic Blessing" (the Eucharist) unites us to Christ "as if one should join wax with other wax". He warns against staying away from it.
  - *Augustine* is double-sided and very useful for the host. He speaks of the sacrament, *but* he defines eating as abiding: "This it is ... for a man to eat that meat and to drink that drink, to dwell in Christ, and to have Christ dwelling in him." He says the unworthy press "the sacrament ... carnally and visibly with his teeth" without eating Christ. (This is the Augustine whom Calvin loved to cite.) In Tractate 27 he urges "that we eat not the flesh and blood of Christ merely in the sacrament."
- **Reformed reading (primarily faith):**
  - *Calvin* says "this discourse does not relate to the Lord's Supper, but to the uninterrupted communication of the flesh of Christ, which we obtain apart from the use of the Lord's Supper". He gives two arguments. First, if this were the Supper, all communicants would have life, yet many partake to condemnation. Second, it would have been "foolish and unreasonable" to discourse on the Supper before he instituted it. **Yet** "the doctrine which is here taught is sealed in the Lord's Supper," and "faith alone is the mouth ... and the stomach of the soul." Also note his subtle point on 6:47-51: eating is not simply *identical* to believing. "To eat him, therefore, is an effect or work of faith." Faith receives Christ; eating is the union that follows.
  - *Ryle* is the strongest: "Above all, the words were not spoken with any reference to the Sacrament of the Lord's Supper. We may eat the Lord's Supper, and yet not eat and drink Christ's body and blood." He gives three reasons. First, literal eating of blood would be revolting to Jews and against the law. Second, it would interpose a bodily act between the soul and salvation. Third, it would shut out the penitent thief and admit godless communicants.
  - *WCF 29.7*: The Supper is the place where believers "feed upon, Christ crucified", "spiritually". So the Supper signifies and seals the same feeding that John 6 describes. That matches the host's PCA framing exactly.
- **Note the tension:** Calvin says "the ancients fell into a gross error" (infant communion based on 6:53). Ryle, an Anglican bishop, is even more anti-sacramental here than Calvin. Calvin keeps the link: John 6 is "sealed in the Lord's Supper." Ryle says "We may eat ... Christ's body and blood, and yet not eat the Lord's Supper."

### Q3. Why does Jesus intensify rather than soften (6:51-58)?
- When the crowd stumbles at "bread from heaven," Jesus does not explain it away. He says "my flesh," then "flesh and blood," then (6:54ff.) uses *trogo*. And the crowd stays offended (6:60, 66).
- *Chrysostom* asks this exact question ("how this was a fit season for these words, which neither edified nor profited"). He answers: "indistinctness in what is said always rouses the hearer," and "the offense was caused by their own folly, not by any difficulty in the words." In Homily 47 he notes that when they called it impossible, "He shows not only that it is not impossible, but that it is absolutely necessary."
- *Calvin on 6:51:* "He often repeats the same thing, because nothing is more necessary to be known." And on 6:54: "This is a repetition, but is not superfluous; for it confirms what was difficult to be believed."
- *Sproul:* People do not like the word "compel", "for the same reason that the people walked away when they heard Jesus give the original message."
- *The trogo point:* Many preachers (and Strong's/Thayer's lexical gloss "gnaw, munch, crunch") see a deliberate escalation from *phago* to *trogo*. Meyer disagrees: "no special intention." An honest way to put it: the word is earthy, used of animals feeding, and Jesus uses it four times. Even if it had softened in everyday use, he did not reach for a gentler word. He chose to make the claim *more* concrete, not less, because only a crucified Christ received wholly is true food.
- *Ellicott (6:53):* He links the blood language to Leviticus 17: "the blood maketh atonement." The shocking image is atonement imagery.

# Research: 1 Peter 3:13-22

## 1. Setting and background

- **Letter and audience.** Peter writes to "elect exiles of the dispersion" in five Roman provinces of Asia Minor (1:1), most likely from Rome ("Babylon," 5:13), in the early 60s AD, before his martyrdom under Nero (c. 64-68). The readers are largely Gentile converts who now face slander, social exclusion and family pressure rather than (yet) state-wide persecution. Stephen Nichols (Ligonier) on the readers: "These exiled Christians were ostracized for their faith and suffered persecution. They were insulted and slandered. Some of them suffered at the hands of their own family members."
- **Where we are in the letter.** 2:11-4:11 is the long section on living honorably among unbelievers: submission to authorities (2:13-17), slaves (2:18-25, with Christ as example), wives and husbands (3:1-7), all believers (3:8-12, quoting Psalm 34). 3:13-22 moves from "don't repay evil for evil" to suffering *for righteousness*, then grounds it in Christ's own unjust suffering and triumphant vindication (3:18-22). 4:1 picks up again: "Since therefore Christ suffered in the flesh, arm yourselves with the same way of thinking."
- **Isaiah 8:12-13 behind 3:14-15.** In Ahaz's day (c. 734 BC, the Syro-Ephraimite crisis), Judah panicked at the alliance of Syria and Israel and Ahaz sought Assyria's help. The LORD told Isaiah: "Do not call conspiracy all that this people calls conspiracy, and do not fear what they fear, nor be in dread. But the LORD of hosts, him you shall regard as holy; let him be your fear." Peter quotes it and does two striking things: he shifts "their fear" to mean the terror the persecutors try to inspire, and he puts "Christ" (ESV "Christ the Lord") where Isaiah has "the LORD of hosts", a high Christology. Calvin notes the shift explicitly (see quote bank). Isaiah 8:14 goes on to the "stone of stumbling," which Peter already used in 2:8.
- **Noah background.** Genesis 6-8; "eight persons" = Noah, his wife, three sons and their wives (Gen 7:13). 2 Peter 2:5 calls Noah "a herald (keryx) of righteousness", the noun related to "proclaimed" (ekeryxen) in 3:19. 2 Peter 2:4 and Jude 6 speak of angels who sinned being kept in chains/Tartarus for judgment; 1 Enoch (esp. ch. 6-16) develops the Genesis 6 "sons of God" as rebellious angels ("Watchers"), and Enoch is sent to announce their doom (1 Enoch 12-16). Jude 14 quotes 1 Enoch, so the readers likely knew the tradition.
- **Apostles' Creed connection.** "He descended into hell" (a late addition to the Creed) has often been linked to 3:19. Westminster Larger Catechism Q50 explains it as Christ's "continuing in the state of the dead, and under the power of death till the third day" (paraphrase only; not fetched for this file). Calvin denies that 3:19 refers to the descent (quote bank).

### Key Greek words (Bible Hub Strong's pages, saved in work/raw/p3_strongs_*.txt)
- **apologia** (627): "a speech in defense" (NASB: defense, vindication). Courtroom word (Acts 22:1; 25:16; Phil 1:7, 16); Socrates' *Apology*. In 3:15 "to make a defense" is commanded of every believer.
- **hagiazo** (37): "to make holy, consecrate, sanctify." 3:15 "in your hearts *honor as holy* (hallow) Christ the Lord," the same verb as "Hallowed be thy name." Calvin's editor makes this exact link.
- **phylake** (5438): "a guarding, guard, watch" and usually "prison" (34x). Calvin famously suggests "watchtower" in 3:19.
- **kerysso** (proclaim/herald), not euangelizo (preach the gospel) in 3:19: MacArthur, Barnes and others lean on this.
- **prosago** (4317): "to bring or lead to." 3:18 "that he might bring us to God" (court/temple language of access, ushering someone into a king's presence; cf. noun prosagoge "access," Rom 5:2; Eph 2:18; 3:12). Leighton: Christ "takes it by the hand... leads it unto his Father; as the word [prosagage] imports."
- **antitypos** (499): "struck back, corresponding to." 3:21 baptism "which corresponds to this," the *antitype* of the flood water. Calvin: "a likeness... a correspondence."
- **eperotema** (1906): "an inquiry, a demand." Only here in the NT (3:21). Rendered "answer" (KJV), "appeal" (ESV, NASB), "pledge" (NIV, BSB). Debate: an *appeal* to God for a clean conscience, or a *pledge* made to God from a good conscience (the latter used in later papyri of a contract stipulation/answer). Leighton: "the asking or questioning of conscience, which comprises likewise its answer." MacArthur: "a technical term for making a pledge."

## 2. Outline

1. **3:13-17. Suffering for righteousness is blessed.** "Now who is there to harm you if you are zealous for what is good? But even if you should suffer for righteousness' sake, you will be blessed" (echo of Matt 5:10). Isaiah 8:12-13: "Have no fear of them, nor be troubled, but in your hearts honor Christ the Lord as holy, always being prepared to make a defense to anyone who asks you for a reason for the hope that is in you; yet do it with gentleness and respect, having a good conscience..." 3:17: better to suffer for doing good than for doing evil, "if that should be God's will."
2. **3:18. The pattern and ground: Christ's unique suffering.** "For Christ also suffered once for sins, the righteous for the unrighteous, that he might bring us to God, being put to death in the flesh but made alive in the spirit." (once-for-all; substitution; purpose = access to God; death and vindication)
3. **3:19-20. The proclamation to the spirits in prison.** "...in which he went and proclaimed to the spirits in prison, because they formerly did not obey, when God's patience waited in the days of Noah, while the ark was being prepared, in which a few, that is, eight persons, were brought safely through water."
4. **3:21. Baptism as antitype.** "Baptism, which corresponds to this, now saves you, not as a removal of dirt from the body but as an appeal to God for a good conscience, through the resurrection of Jesus Christ."
5. **3:22. Enthroned Lord.** "...who has gone into heaven and is at the right hand of God, with angels, authorities, and powers having been subjected to him." The suffering Christ ends enthroned; the persecuted few in the ark end saved. That is the point for the readers.

## 3. Interpretive questions

### Q1. Who are "the spirits in prison," and when and what did Christ proclaim? (3:19-20)
The main views, with the voices:
- **(a) Christ preached *through Noah*, by the Spirit, to people then alive who are now "spirits in prison."** Augustine (Letter 164 to Evodius, c. 414) proposes this after laying out at length why the descent-to-hell reading perplexes him (why only Noah's generation?). The building of the ark "was itself in a certain sense a preaching of mercy." Held by Aquinas, Beza, Pearson, Leighton (in his main text; see below), Matthew Poole ("preached; viz. by Noah"), Gill ("The plain and easy sense of the words is, that Christ, by his Spirit... went in the ministry of Noah"), Matthew Henry Concise ("As to the old world, Christ sent his Spirit; gave warning by Noah"), Benson, Barnes, the Geneva notes, Calvin's 19th-century English editor (footnote preferring Beza's view), Wayne Grudem (Trinity Journal 1986 article "Christ Preaching Through Noah"; Tyndale commentary). The Reformation Study Bible (Sproul, gen. ed.) lists this view first. Third Millennium (Joseph Nally): "This is the most reasonable interpretation." Strength: 1:11 already says "the Spirit of Christ" spoke in the prophets; 2 Peter 2:5 calls Noah a herald; "formerly disobeyed... while God's patience waited" fits people being called to repent. Weakness: "went" seems to parallel "has gone into heaven" in 3:22 (same participle poreutheis), suggesting a post-resurrection journey.
- **(b) Christ, between death and resurrection, went to the realm of the dead and preached.** Ellicott says this is "(practically) all the Fathers" (Clement of Alexandria, Origen and others held that he offered salvation there). Augustine reports and questions it. Catholic "Limbus Patrum" version: Christ freed the OT saints (Gill rejects it). **Calvin's own view is unusual and needs to be stated carefully.** He explicitly rejects that the text is about the descent into hell ("the words mean no such thing"), rejects the "apostles preached to the spiritually dead" reading, and rejects any second chance for unbelievers. His positive view: Christ's grace and the "vital power of the Spirit" was *manifested to the godly spirits* of the dead (the OT saints), who were in a "watchtower" (phylake = watch) longing for the promised salvation; the "disobedient" clause he reads as describing the time when the faithful were "almost hidden" among the unbelieving multitude. He admits the Greek grammar strains against his reading. Luther (finally), Bellarmine and Bengel are cited by Ellicott for versions of (b).
- **(c) Christ proclaimed victory/judgment to fallen angels** (the "sons of God" of Gen 6, imprisoned per 2 Pet 2:4, Jude 6, 1 Enoch). Either during the descent (MacArthur: "When Jesus body was dead, His spirit was alive and He went down where they were bound and He announced His triumph over them") or at the ascension (Dalton, Schreiner per secondary report, Jarvis Williams of SBTS: proclamation "by means of his death, resurrection, and exaltation/ascension"). Strengths: "spirits" unqualified in the NT usually means non-human spirits; kerysso, not euangelizo; 3:22 subjection of angels; Jewish background. Weakness (Grudem): angels are never said to have disobeyed "during the building of the ark"; God's patience waiting fits human repentance.
- **Common ground (good for the host):** every Reformed voice, whatever the view, agrees there is no second chance after death (Calvin: "there is no hope left for those who continue to death unbelieving"; Augustine: refuse to think "a Church had been established there as well as on earth"; Third Millennium: "In no case was Peter suggesting that Christ offered deceased unbelievers an opportunity"). And the pastoral point is the same: the faithful are few, the scoffers many, but Christ is Lord, the judgment came, and the eight were kept.
- **Leighton changed his mind.** His commentary gives the Noah view in the main text, but a footnote (his later note) says "Thus I then thought, but do now apprehend another sense, as probable, if not more": the risen Christ by his Spirit preaching through the apostles to captive "spirits in prison" (Isa 61:1), contrasting Noah's eight with "multitudes of all nations." A nice touch: even a careful Puritan-era expositor held his view humbly.

### Q2. "Baptism... now saves you" (3:21). In what sense?
- **Presbyterian/Westminster:** baptism is a sign and seal of the covenant of grace (WCF 28.1); grace and salvation are "not so inseparably annexed unto it" that none can be saved without it or that all baptized are regenerated (28.5); "the efficacy of baptism is not tied to that moment of time wherein it is administered" yet the grace promised is "really exhibited, and conferred, by the Holy Ghost" to those to whom it belongs in God's time (28.6). Calvin: don't separate sign and thing signified, but don't tie the Spirit to water; Peter's "not the removal of dirt" shows "the outward sign of itself avails nothing." Leighton (paedobaptist, writing about infant baptism): "neither empty signs to those who believe, nor effectual causes of grace to those who believe not."
- **Baptist (charitably):** MacArthur reads "baptism" here as immersion into Christ (the ark) through the judgment that fell on him; eperotema is "a pledge," a believer's covenant appeal to God; water baptism is the outward expression of that. Gill (Baptist) also stresses the answer of a good conscience. Both traditions agree on Peter's own qualification: not the water, but what it signifies, "through the resurrection of Jesus Christ."
- **The fathers** read it typologically and sacramentally: Justin connects the eight with the "eighth day" of resurrection; Tertullian sees the dove returning to the ark as the Spirit descending on the baptized, the Church as "the typified ark." Calvin pushes back on any view that "transfer[s] the glory of his death to the water."
- **Calvin's twist on the ark:** the ark was a kind of grave; Noah "obtained life through death"; so baptism (death and resurrection with Christ) is entrance into life, and few are saved then as now.

### Q3. 3:14-15: what does "honor Christ as holy" have to do with fear and apologetics?
Calvin: we panic because "we ascribe to mortal man more power to injure us than to God to save us"; hallowing Christ in the heart is the cure for fear, and the readiness to answer flows out of that. Peter quotes Isaiah 8, where Ahaz's Judah feared the conspiracy and ran to Assyria; the LORD said, Fear me, not them. Calvin notes Peter turns "their fear" from Judah's fear to the persecutors' intimidation. Sproul: apologia means a defense, not an apology; "the only leap of faith we are to take is out of the darkness and into the light." Calvin also says not every believer must answer every hard question, only be ready to show that their faith is reasonable and godly, and "except faith dwells within, the tongue prattles in vain." Gentleness and respect (Greek phobos, "fear/reverence," respect toward God and people) plus a good conscience (3:16): the life must back up the defense.

## Paraphrase-only notes
- **Ellicott (Bible Hub, 3:19)** reports that the Noah view was held by Pearson, Hammond, Barrow, Leighton, Aquinas and Beza, and that the descent view is "(practically) all the Fathers, and of Calvin, Luther (finally), Bellarmine, Bengel." Ellicott's lumping of Calvin with the descent view is imprecise: Calvin denies it is about the descent of Christ's soul, though he does say the Spirit's power reached the dead.
- **Calvin also rejects** (a) the reading that Christ preached through the apostles to the "spiritually dead," (b) any salvation for unbelievers after death ("there is no hope left for those who continue to death unbelieving"), and (c) the view that some of Noah's contemporaries repented as the flood came.
- **Gill** surveys and rejects: Christ's soul preaching in hell; the Catholic Limbus Patrum; preaching to Gentiles via apostles; the "watch" (Calvin's) reading. He calls the Noah view "the plain and easy sense."
- **Grudem's** Trinity Journal article "Christ Preaching Through Noah: 1 Peter 3:19-20 in the Light of Dominant Themes in Jewish Literature" (TrinJ 7.2, 1986) is paywalled at galaxie.com; only the title is verified. The quoted Grudem material comes via Third Millennium.
- **Schreiner** (1, 2 Peter, Jude, NAC 2003; CSC 2020) holds the fallen-angels view per secondary sources (Jarvis Williams cites Schreiner pp. 209-216 for the Jewish background). Not directly verified; say only "Schreiner and others" in general terms, if at all.
- **Piper** preached "Strengthened to Suffer: Christ, Noah, and Baptism" on 1 Peter 3:18-22 (Sep 25, 1994) per the Desiring God series listing in search results; desiringgod.org returned 500/403 and the BibleGateway devotional returned 403. Not quoted.
- **Sproul** has a Renewing Your Mind episode "Imprisoned Spirits" (1 Peter series) exploring the views; transcript not available, so his own conclusion is not verified. The 2011 lecture "Defending the Faith" is on 1 Peter 3:15 (description only).
- **Leighton's later view (footnote)**: the risen Christ by the Spirit sent on the apostles preached to "captive spirits" (Isa 61:1), so that where Noah saved eight, Christ saves "multitudes of all nations," "by the seal of baptism, the resurrection of Christ being represented in the return from the water."
- **Leighton on infant baptism**: he laments that baptism, "being but once administered, and that in infancy, is very seldom and slightly considered," and urges parents presenting infants to renew their own faith. Useful for a PCA application (paraphrase, or check OCR before quoting).
- **MacArthur's angelology** (60-37): fallen angels are "loose" or "bound"; the bound ones of 2 Pet 2:4 and Jude 6 are those who sinned in Gen 6; he holds that the "sons of God" are angels and the Sethite view is wrong. He ties 3:19 to Col 2:15.
- **Third Millennium (Joseph R. Nally Jr.)** summary: "Jesus didn't preach the gospel in Hell, but rather he preached the gospel through Noah to unbelievers, presently in Hell, while they were still upon the earth."
- **Isaiah 8 history** (Syro-Ephraimite crisis, c. 734 BC; Ahaz's appeal to Tiglath-pileser, 2 Kings 16:7) is standard background; Calvin's comment that the Jews "sought to fortify themselves by the aid of the Gentile world" is verified in the Calvin raw file.
- **Tertullian** (On Baptism ch. 8) also notes "the world returned unto sin; in which point baptism would ill be compared to the deluge," a warning against post-baptismal presumption.
