## Setting and background

### Where we are in the story
- Jeremiah 43 ended with the remnant under Johanan arriving at Tahpanhes "for they did not obey the voice of the LORD" (ESV 43:7), and Jeremiah burying stones in the pavement at Pharaoh's palace as a sign that Nebuchadnezzar "my servant" would set his throne there and "strike the land of Egypt" (43:10-11). 43:3 had blamed Baruch: "Baruch the son of Neriah has set you against us."
- Chapter 44 is Jeremiah's last recorded sermon. Ligonier's Tabletalk devotional "Trouble in Egypt" says: "Chronologically, this chapter represents the last oracle Jeremiah spoke during his lifetime to be recorded in Scripture." (https://learn.ligonier.org/devotionals/trouble-egypt)
- Date: after the fall of Jerusalem (July 586 B.C.) and the murder of Gedaliah (seventh month, probably 586 or, on some reckonings, 582). How long after the arrival in Egypt is not stated. Ellicott dates the prophecy "circ. B.C. 580" (Ellicott on 44:30, https://biblehub.com/commentaries/jeremiah/44-30.htm). Ellicott on 44:1 adds that "there had been time for the Jews to disperse and settle in the three or four cities here named."

### The four places in 44:1 (the Jews are spread across all of Egypt)
- **Migdol**: Easton: "A strongly-fortified place 12 miles from Pelusium, in the north of Egypt"; Ezekiel's "from Migdol to Syene" means "from Migdol in the north to Syene in the south, in other words, the whole of Egypt." (https://biblehub.com/topical/m/migdol.htm)
- **Tahpanhes**: the eastern Delta frontier town of ch. 43 (Petrie's pavement was covered last episode).
- **Memphis (Hebrew Noph)**: Easton: "one of the most ancient and important cities of Egypt ... It was the capital of Lower Egypt." (https://biblehub.com/topical/n/noph.htm)
- **Pathros**: Easton: "The name generally given to Upper Egypt (the Thebaid of the Greeks), as distinguished from Matsor, or Lower Egypt." (https://biblehub.com/topical/p/pathros.htm). Keil and Delitzsch say "in Pathros" tells "where the gathering took place, viz., in a district of Upper Egypt" and infer from the many women present that "the assembly was a festival in honour of the queen of heaven" (https://biblehub.com/commentaries/jeremiah/44-16.htm). Barnes imagines the scene: Jeremiah meets a procession "to worship the moon-goddess" and "makes the procession halt upon its way" (https://biblehub.com/commentaries/jeremiah/44-16.htm).
- Teaching point: north, capital, frontier, far south. In a few years the refugees had scattered over the whole country and taken up its worship.

### The queen of heaven (44:15-19, 25; compare 7:18)
- Hebrew *melekheth hashamayim*, "queen of the heavens" (Bible Hub interlinear 44:19). Some older interpreters (Kimchi, Calvin, Poole) read a variant, *meleketh*, meaning roughly work, frame or fabric of heaven, that is, the whole host of heaven. Calvin: "I do not therefore consider that one starts meant, but the very heavens or all the stars" (CCEL 44:17; "starts" is an OCR slip for "star is" on the CCEL page).
- ISBE (T. Nicol): "if, as seems probable from the cakes which were offered, she is to be identified with the Assyrian Ishtar and the Canaanite Astarte, the worship itself was of a grossly immoral and debasing character." (https://biblehub.com/topical/q/queen_of_heaven.htm). Ligonier's devotional simply says "the goddess Astarte."
- The cakes "bearing her image" (ESV 44:19): Hebrew *leha'atsivah*, a rare Hiphil of *'atsab*. Keil and Delitzsch take it as "to form the moon-goddess (queen of heaven) in or on the sacrificial cakes" and suggest the cakes "probably had the form of a crescent, or even of the full moon" (https://biblehub.com/commentaries/jeremiah/44-19.htm). Barnes: "The cakes ... were made in the shape of a crescent to represent the moon."
- Who speaks in 44:19? The ESV supplies "And the women said" with the footnote "Compare Syriac; Hebrew lacks *And the women said*." Most commentators (Henry, Keil and Delitzsch, Pulpit, Cambridge, Ellicott) take it as the women's words, appealing to Numbers 30, where a husband's silence ratified his wife's vow. Calvin dissents and thinks the men speak, "without our men" meaning "the princes who bear rule" (CCEL 44:19). Gill notes both views.
- The husbands' role: 44:15 says "all the men who knew that their wives had made offerings." Calvin: connivance equals guilt (quote bank). Geneva note on 44:19: "This teaches us what a great danger it is for the husbands to permit their wives anything of which they are not assured by God's word."
- Patristic echo: Gill identifies the goddess with "Coelestis" and cites Tertullian calling her "pluviarum pollicitatrix", "the promiser of rains" (Gill on 44:17). Augustine (City of God 2.4) recalls attending as a young man the obscene festivals "of the virgin Cœlestis" at Carthage. (https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/120102.htm). This is a real link: the same sky-goddess cult was alive in North Africa a thousand years later.
- Epiphanius and the Collyridians: UNVERIFIED. A secondary source says Epiphanius (Panarion 79.8) quoted Jeremiah's queen of heaven against the Collyridians, women who offered bread cakes to Mary. I could not fetch a public-domain English text of Panarion 79. Catholic Answers (https://www.catholic.com/magazine/print-edition/collyridianism) confirms Epiphanius condemned them ("offer up a sacrifice of bread rolls in the name of the ever-Virgin Mary") but does not show him citing Jeremiah. Treat the Jeremiah link as PARAPHRASE/unverified. The Geneva Bible note on 44:17 makes the Marian application directly (quote bank).

### Pharaoh Hophra (Apries) and the sign of 44:29-30
- Hophra (Greek Apries) reigned about 589-570 B.C. (Cambridge Bible on 44:29: "reigned from b.c. 589 to c. 570"). He is the Pharaoh whose army briefly lifted the siege of Jerusalem in 588 (Jer 37:5). Josiah's killer Neco II was his grandfather (Neco, then Psammetichus II, then Apries).
- His end, from Herodotus 2.169 (Godley translation, Perseus): "Apries, they say, supposed that not even a god could depose him from his throne, so firmly did he think he was established; and now, defeated in battle and taken captive, he was brought to Saïs ... But presently the Egyptians complained that there was no justice in keeping alive one who was their own and their king's bitterest enemy; whereupon Amasis gave Apries up to them, and they strangled him." (https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0126%3Abook%3D2%3Achapter%3D169)
- Note the fit with 44:30, "into the hand of his enemies and into the hand of those who seek his life." Jamieson-Fausset-Brown: "the words are accurately chosen, so as not to refer to Nebuchadnezzar." Keil and Delitzsch quote Nägelsbach on Apries' boast "that even God could not dethrone him."
- Older commentators (Calvin, Gill, Poole, the Geneva notes) followed Josephus (Antiquities 10.9.7), who has Nebuchadnezzar invade Egypt in his 23rd year and kill the king. Herodotus has Amasis's party kill him. Henry: "the former is related by Herodotus, the latter by Josephus." Modern reconstructions combine the two. Amasis's revolt was around 570. Apries apparently tried to come back with Babylonian help about 567, and Nebuchadnezzar's own campaign against Amasis followed in 568/567. I saw the Amasis Elephantine stela cited for this but did not fetch it; UNVERIFIED detail.

### Chapter 45: the word to Baruch, dated 605/604 B.C.
- 45:1: "when he wrote these words in a book at the dictation of Jeremiah, in the fourth year of Jehoiakim". This is the year of the scroll in 36:1-4. The 4th year of Jehoiakim is 605/604 B.C. The scroll was read in the 5th year, ninth month (36:9), December 604. So ch. 45 is about 18 years older than ch. 44 (Poole and Gill both say "eighteen years").
- **Literary hinge:** 46:2 dates Carchemish to the same year: "in the fourth year of Jehoiakim." So ch. 45 sits between two "fourth year" markers (45:1 and 46:2), and its "I am bringing disaster upon all flesh" (45:5) introduces the oracles against "all the nations" (46:1). That observation is mine, not a commentator's. Keil and Delitzsch see the chapter as "an appendix to those predictions of Jeremiah which concern Judah and Israel"; Barnes on 45:4: "It was not Judaea only but the whole known world that was thrown into turmoil by Nebuchadnezzars energy." See Interpretive Question (b) for the placement.
- Baruch's family: Cambridge Bible on 45:5 notes he was "grandson of Maaseiah who was governor of Jerusalem in Josiah's reign (2 Chronicles 34:8)," and his brother Seraiah was Zedekiah's quartermaster (51:59). Expositor's Bible on 45 notes Seraiah ben Neriah "must be Baruch's brother."
- Baruch bulla ("Berekhyahu son of Neriyahu the scribe"): DO NOT USE AS PROOF. Search results show Goren and Arie (BASOR 2014) argued both known bullae are modern forgeries and van der Veen, Deutsch and Barkay (2016) replied without claiming to prove them authentic. The question is still open, and both bullae came from the antiquities market. (Abstracts seen via search; e.g. https://repositorio.uca.edu.ar/handle/123456789/7509)

### Chapter 46: start of the oracles against the nations (46-51)
- 46:1 is the heading for the whole collection (Pulpit, Keil and Delitzsch, Henry). Benson notes the Septuagint places these oracles after 25:13. Egypt comes first. Henry: "How judgment began at the house of God we have found in the foregoing prophecy and history; but now we shall find that it did not end there."
- **46:2-12: Carchemish, 605 B.C.** Babylonian Chronicle (ABC 5, Jerusalem Chronicle), Livius translation (https://www.livius.org/sources/content/mesopotamian-chronicles-content/abc-5-jerusalem-chronicle/):
  "[Obv.2] mustered the Babylonian army and took command of his troops; he marched to Karchemiš which is on the bank of the Euphrates, [Obv.3] and crossed the river to go against the Egyptian army which lay in Karchemiš. [Obv.4] They fought with each other and the Egyptian army withdrew before him. [Obv.5] He accomplished their defeat and beat them to non-existence. As for the rest of the Egyptian army [Obv.6] which had escaped from the defeat so quickly that no weapon had reached them, in the district of Hamath [Obv.7] the Babylonian troops overtook and defeated them so that not a single man escaped to his own country."
  Compare ESV 46:6, "The swift cannot flee away, nor the warrior escape; in the north by the river Euphrates they have stumbled and fallen." The Chronicle then dates Nabopolassar's death to 8 Abu (15 August 605) and Nebuchadnezzar's accession to 1 Ululu (7 September 605). He was crown prince when he won at Carchemish and king within weeks.
- Why "vengeance" (46:10)? Neco had killed Josiah at Megiddo in 609 (2 Kings 23:29). Calvin, Gill (citing Kimchi), JFB and Poole all connect 46:10 with Josiah's death.
- **46:13-26: Nebuchadnezzar strikes Egypt, 568/567 B.C.** Cuneiform fragment BM 33041 (Nebuchadnezzar's 37th year), ANET-style translation reproduced at https://www.jhalsey.com/jerusalem-book/standard/egypt/bm33041.html: "... [in] the 37th year, Nebuchadnezzar, king of Bab[ylon] mar[ched against] Egypt (Mi-ṣir) to deliver a battle. [Ama]sis (text: [...]-a(?)-su), of Egypt, [called up his a]rm[y] ..." and later "... he put his trust ...". Compare 46:25, "upon Pharaoh and those who trust in him." The text is badly broken. Ligonier's devotional "Egypt's Judgment and Israel's Salvation" attributes the 568 invasion to Herodotus. That attribution is wrong: the evidence is this Babylonian tablet. Do not repeat the Ligonier attribution.
- 46:26b: "Afterward Egypt shall be inhabited as in the days of old." The oracle limits the judgment. Calvin reconciles this with 46:28 (see question d).
- **46:27-28** repeats 30:10-11 almost word for word. Barnes: "with those slight variations which Jeremiah always makes when quoting himself." Some critics (Pulpit, Ellicott) think it was placed here editorially. Keil and Delitzsch argue it is genuine and purposeful: 46:26 says God will punish "those who trust in him" (Pharaoh), and "In order to prevent such a misconception" that Judah shares Egypt's doom, "Jeremiah adds a word for Israel."

### Elephantine (optional color, use carefully)
- Ligonier ("Egypt's Judgment and Israel's Salvation") says: "Many descendants of the Jews whom God forbade to go to Egypt (chap. 42) finally settled in the Egyptian city of Elephantine, where they built a temple to Yahweh and engaged in corrupt worship, both of which the Mosaic law forbids (Deut. 12)." (https://learn.ligonier.org/devotionals/egypts-judgment-and-israels-salvation). The descent claim is the devotional's inference. The Elephantine colony is attested from the 5th century B.C. and may go back to soldiers settled before 586. Search results (not fetched) report an Elephantine oath "in the name of Anat-Yahu" and scholars connecting it to Jer 44 (Mondriaan 2013; ASOR blog). UNVERIFIED in a fetched primary text, so use only as "scholars have noted."
- Keil and Delitzsch (on 44:29-30) discuss Nägelsbach's objection that Egypt later had many Jews, and answer that the Alexandrian Jews came later under Alexander and Ptolemy. That bears on 44:27-28: the community that went down under Johanan disappears from history. Henry: "we hear no more of these Jews in Egypt, and therefore conclude them, according to this prediction, lost there."

### Jeremiah's end (tradition)
- Jerome, Against Jovinianus 2.37: "Jeremiah announced the captivity and was stoned by the people." (https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/30092.htm). Ellicott on 44:30 cites Tertullian and Jerome for "the Egyptian Jews, irritated by his reproaches, rose up against him and stoned him to death," and suggests Hebrews 11:37. This was used last episode; mention only briefly.

**Jeremiah 44: last sermon to the Judeans in Egypt; the open refusal**
1. 44:1-6: Remember Jerusalem. ESV 44:4-5: "Yet I persistently sent to you all my servants the prophets, saying, 'Oh, do not do this abomination that I hate!' But they did not listen or incline their ear." (Hebrew *velo shame'u*, shama again.)
2. 44:7-10: Why harm yourselves? 44:7: "Why do you commit this great evil against yourselves" (*ra'ah gedolah*, a "great" evil; see the "great" thread below). 44:10: "They have not humbled themselves even to this day."
3. 44:11-14: Sentence. The remnant that "set their faces to come to the land of Egypt" will be consumed, "except some fugitives."
4. 44:15-19: The reply. ESV 44:16-17: "As for the word that you have spoken to us in the name of the LORD, we will not listen to you. But we will do everything that we have vowed." 44:17-18 gives their argument from experience: "For then we had plenty of food, and prospered, and saw no disaster. But since we left off making offerings to the queen of heaven ... we have lacked everything." 44:19 is the women's appeal to their husbands' approval.
5. 44:20-23: Jeremiah's answer. It was not because you stopped, it was because you did it. ESV 44:23: "It is because you made offerings and because you sinned against the LORD and did not obey the voice of the LORD ... that this disaster has happened to you, as at this day."
6. 44:24-28: God takes them at their word. ESV 44:25: "Then confirm your vows and perform your vows!" 44:26: "I have sworn by my great name ... that my name shall no more be invoked by the mouth of any man of Judah in all the land of Egypt." 44:27: "Behold, I am watching over them for disaster and not for good." 44:28: "and all the remnant of Judah ... shall know whose word will stand, mine or theirs."
7. 44:29-30: The sign of Hophra. "my words will surely stand against you for harm."

**Jeremiah 45: the word to Baruch (605/604 B.C.)**

- 45:4: "Behold, what I have built I am breaking down, and what I have planted I am plucking up—that is, the whole land."
- 45:5: "And do you seek great things for yourself? Seek them not, for behold, I am bringing disaster upon all flesh, declares the LORD. But I will give you your life as a prize of war in all places to which you may go."

### Hebrew features (Bible Hub interlinear and Strong's)
1. **Shama closes (44:5, 16, 23).** 44:16 reads *'enennu shom'im elekha*, literally we are not listening to you (Qal participle plural, Bible Hub interlinear 44:16; YLT: "we are not hearkening unto thee"). Strong's 8085 *shama'*: "to hear intelligently (often with implication of attention, obedience, etc." The thread through 35-43 ends in a flat, present-tense refusal. 44:5 has God's past complaint, "they did not listen" (*lo shame'u*), and 44:23 the verdict, "did not obey the voice of the LORD" (*lo shema'tem beqol YHWH*). Poole and Benson: in ch. 43 Johanan "only denied that God had spoken"; now "these women and men rise higher" and admit it is God's word yet refuse. This is the end of the line: they no longer pretend.
2. **Qum, "stand," as the chapter's last word (44:25, 28, 29).** 44:25: *haqem taqimenah et nidrekhem*, literally you will surely make your vows stand (Hiphil of *qum*; the interlinear glosses it as standing, you will make stand). 44:28: *devar-mi yaqum, mimmenni umehem*, literally whose word will stand, from me or from them. 44:29: *qom yaqumu devarai*, "my words will surely stand" (infinitive absolute plus imperfect). So: you have made your word stand; we will see whose word stands. Strong's 6965 *qum*: "to arise, stand up, stand" and also "confirm ... perform." Keil and Delitzsch call 44:25 "unmistakeable irony." Compare 46:15, 21: Egypt's mighty men "do not stand" (*'amad*).
3. **Shaqad, "watching" (44:27).** *hineni shoqed 'alehem lera'ah velo letovah*, "I am watching over them for disaster and not for good." It is the same participle as 1:12 ("I am watching over my word to perform it") and 31:28. The almond (*shaqed*) and watching (*shoqed*) wordplay from last episode returns here as a threat. The Cambridge Bible cross-references 1:12 and 31:28. Strong's 8245: "to be alert, i.e. Sleepless."
4. **"Great" (*gadol*) as a thread.** 44:7 "this great evil" (*ra'ah gedolah*); 44:26 "I have sworn by my great name"; 45:5 "do you seek great things for yourself?" (*gedolot*); and back at 33:3, "Call to me and I will answer you, and will tell you great and hidden things" (*gedolot*). The remnant does a great evil. God swears by his great name. Baruch must not seek great things for himself, and the great things worth having are the ones God tells. (Bible Hub interlinear confirms Strong's 1419 at 33:3, 44:7, 45:5.)
5. **Build / break down, plant / pluck up (45:4).** *asher baniti ani hores ve'et asher nata'ti ani notesh*. It uses the commissioning verbs of 1:10 and the offer of 42:10 ("I will build you up and not pull you down; I will plant you, and not pluck you up"). The participles say "I am breaking down ... I am plucking up," which is happening now. Pulpit: "comp, Jeremiah 1:10" [sic]. Keil and Delitzsch cf. 1:10; 31:28.
6. **"Your life as a prize of war" (45:5).** *nafshekha leshalal*, literally your soul as spoil. It was promised before to whoever surrendered (21:9; 38:2) and to Ebed-melech (39:18). Baruch gets the Ebed-melech promise. Calvin and Henry both explain the image (quote bank).
7. **"Noisy one who lets the hour go by" (46:17).** *Par'oh melekh Mitsrayim sha'on he'evir hammo'ed*. *Sha'on* (Strong's 7588): "a roar ... din, crash, uproar," and by implication "destruction." *He'evir hammo'ed* is Hiphil, "he has let the appointed time pass." The Pulpit Commentary insists on this: "'passed' cannot be correct, as the verb is in the Hifil or causative conjugation." The Cambridge Bible notes that *he'evir* is thought to pun on the name Hophra (Hebrew *Hofra'*). It is a mock throne-name, like "Magor-missabib" in 20:3 and "Rahab who sits still" in Isaiah 30:7.
8. **"A beautiful heifer ... a biting fly" (46:20).** *'eglah yefeh-fiyyah Mitsrayim, qerets mitsafon ba ba*. *Qerets* occurs only here (Strong's 7171; BDB "perhaps nipping"). Many now read "gadfly." KJV has "destruction," the Vulgate a "goader." Ellicott, JFB, Henry and Gill hear an allusion to the Apis bull of Memphis: the nation looks like its god. Ellicott: "The nation is like its god."
9. **"Day of the Lord ... a day of vengeance ... a sacrifice" (46:10).** *yom neqamah lehinnaqem mitsarav ... ki zevach la'Adonai YHWH tseva'ot*. Calvin and Barnes stress the adversative force: "But that day" belongs to the LORD, not to Pharaoh. Isaiah 34:6, 8 lies behind it (Ellicott, Keil and Delitzsch).
10. **"Full end" (46:28).** *kalah* (Strong's 3617): "completion, complete destruction ... annihilation." "I will make a full end (*'e'eseh khalah*) of all the nations ... but of you I will not make a full end." "In just measure" is *lammishpat*, literally according to justice or judgment. The KJV has "in measure."

## Interpretive questions

### (a) The argument from experience (44:17-18): when we served the queen of heaven we had plenty, and since we stopped we have had disaster
- The facts behind the argument. Josiah's reform (622) suppressed the cult (2 Kings 23). Josiah then died at Megiddo (609), and after that came Neco's tribute, Babylon, three deportations and the fall of Jerusalem. To ordinary people the order of events looked like proof. Keil and Delitzsch name the fallacy: "post hoc, ergo propter hoc." Barnes: "many may even have ascribed to it his defeat at Megiddo." Cambridge Bible (quoting Cheyne): "Jeremiah regarded the misfortunes of his country as proofs of the displeasure of Jehovah: these Jews on the other hand of His impotence." It adds that the passage "shews the popular opposition to Josiah's reform and its superficiality."
- Answers the commentators give:
  1. **The history is false.** Calvin: they had been punished all through the idolatrous years, under Ahaz and Manasseh. "And yet it is certain that they said what was untrue, for God had often chastised them" (CCEL 44:17).
  2. **Prosperity was patience, not approval.** Calvin's long paragraph on God's forbearance. Henry: "during the day of his patience it was perhaps, as they said, well with them." Benson: it was "to be ascribed to the goodness and long-suffering of God waiting for their repentance."
  3. **The judgment was for the earlier sin, delayed.** Jeremiah's own answer in 44:21-23, "did not the LORD remember them?" Henry: "'No,' says he, 'it was because you had formerly done it, not because you had now left it off.'" Calvin calls it "a retort": Jeremiah grants the facts and turns them against them.
  4. **Even if true, it would not justify idolatry.** Henry: "But, supposing all to be true, yet this does not at all excuse them from idolatry; it is the law of God that we must be ruled and judged by, not the practice of men." Henry also: if serving God brought hardship, "was that a reason why they should revolt from him again? That was as much as to say that they served not him, but their own bellies." The Geneva note says the same: "esteem religion by the belly."
  5. **Providence is read by the Word, not the reverse.** Poole: "So bad interpreters are those of God's providences, who indulge their lusts in opposition to God's law." JFB: "it is the curse of impiety not to perceive the hand of God in calamities."
  6. **The pagan Roman parallel.** Ellicott, Calvin and Henry all cite how pagans blamed Christians for Rome's disasters. Tertullian, Apology 40: "Away with the Christians to the lion!" Augustine, City of God 2.3, quotes the proverb "Drought and Christianity go hand in hand" and asks why the gods allowed disasters "before the preaching of Christ's name offended them." This gives the episode a good bridge from the patristic world.
- Pastoral note for a PCA audience: the queen-of-heaven argument is about worship by preference ("whatsoever thing goeth forth out of our own mouth," KJV 44:17). Calvin draws the regulative principle from it directly: "The right rule then as to the worship of God is, to adopt nothing but what he prescribes." Henry's seven "marks of a true church" (antiquity, authority, unity, universality, visibility, mother-church, prosperity) is a pointed Protestant reading. Use it lightly; the Geneva note on 44:17 goes further against Marian devotion ("Salbe Regina" [sic]).

### (b) Why is chapter 45 (dated 605/604) placed after chapter 44 (after 586)? What were Baruch's "great things"?
**Placement.** The commentators give several answers, and they combine well:
- Calvin: the book is a collection of summaries, not a diary. "this book we have now in our hands was not written by Jeremiah himself, but that it contains collected summaries, afterwards formed into one volume." (The same principle as last episode: arranged by message, not calendar.)
- Private word placed after public words. Henry: it was "thrown into the latter end of the book, as St. Paul's epistle to Philemon is put after his other epistles." Gill: "it being written to a private person, is postponed to this place." JFB (citing Grotius) and Poole make the same Philemon comparison.
- Keil and Delitzsch: it closes the prophecies "which concern Judah and Israel," as "an appendix."
- Canonical and thematic reasons (my synthesis, not quoted):
  1. 43:3 had accused Baruch of setting Jeremiah against the people. Ch. 45 shows what kind of man Baruch was and what God had promised him.
  2. Ch. 44 ends with a remnant that sought to save its life in Egypt and will lose it (44:12-14, 27). Ch. 45 ends with a man told not to seek great things, whose life is given back "as a prize of war in all places to which you may go," Egypt included. The juxtaposition says: seek your life and lose it; surrender it and keep it (Mark 8:35, which the TGC piece quotes).
  3. 45:5 "disaster upon all flesh" opens out to 46:1 "concerning the nations," and 45:1 and 46:2 are both dated to "the fourth year of Jehoiakim."
  4. If Baruch helped compile the book (Expositor's Bible suggests "the amanuensis might very easily become the editor"), then this is the editor placing God's rebuke of himself at the hinge of the book. That is a humbling thought, but speculative.

**What were the "great things"?** The views range widely:
- *Safety and freedom from trouble* (Calvin, the main view): he "apprehended danger to himself," "was too much given to care for himself," and should have "presented his life as a sacrifice to God." The "great things" were being exempt while the temple burned: "Shall thy state be eminent while the temple is burnt with fire ...?" Calvin rejects the prophetic-gift view: "this fiction ... has nothing to support it."
- *Advancement at court* (Gill; Henry): Gill thinks the reading of the roll might have brought "preferring him at court." Henry: "Being an ingenious man, and a scholar, he stood fair for preferment." Cambridge mentions his grandfather the governor.
- *The gift of prophecy* (Jewish tradition via Gill and Poole): Targum on 45:3 reads "and I found not prophecy." Rashi and Kimchi tell it like this: Joshua served Moses and Elisha served Elijah, so why not me? Poole thinks it possible ("he might expect to have been a prophet, as Elisha was"). Calvin rejects it.
- *Simply exemption from shared suffering* (Poole, Keil and Delitzsch, Pulpit): "Dost thou expect what none meeteth with ... who feareth God? Never think of it." (Poole). Keil and Delitzsch: "an individual man must not make great demands, but be content with saving his life."
- *Honest uncertainty*: Poole: "impossible to be determined with any certainty." Cambridge: "can only be matter of conjecture."
- Note the timing. Calvin and Henry see a man who had already shown courage (he read the scroll) and faltered afterward. Calvin: "he vacillated in the middle of his course." That is a useful pastoral angle: mid-course fatigue, not first-day cowardice.
- Spurgeon's life application: the voice on Midsummer Common, when he gave up college (quote bank). This is a strong story for the episode; it is his own account.

### (c) The "sign" of Hophra (44:29-30)
- Problem: a sign usually comes first, as with Gideon's fleece. Here the sign (Hophra's fall) would be fulfilled years later, and the people who saw it would themselves be perishing.
- Calvin: two kinds of signs. Some "precede events," like Gideon's. Others "shew that when the time is fulfilled the events have been truly predicted," like Exodus 3:12 ("you shall serve God on this mountain"). Hophra is the second kind: "Jeremiah seals his prophecy by adding a sign which yet was to be coincident with it." Poole and Benson agree, citing Exodus 3:12, Isaiah 37:30, Luke 2:12.
- JFB: "He calls it 'a sign unto you,' because God's prediction is equivalent to the event, so that they may even now take it as a sign. When fulfilled it would cease to be a sign to them: for they would be dead."
- Why Hophra? They trusted Egypt as their protector. Calvin: the Jews "thought that they dwelt, as they say, within the circle of the moon," that is, out of reach. He also reasons from the greater to the less: if God did not spare Zedekiah, who sat on David's throne, he will not spare Pharaoh. Henry: "it would be more than a presage of their ruin, it would be a step towards it, if he were gone."
- The irony with Herodotus: Apries "supposed that not even a god could depose him." Keil and Delitzsch report Nägelsbach's suggestion that the sign answers this boast. K&D think that "too remote," so present it as a striking coincidence, not as the meaning.
- Critics (Hitzig, Graf) call 44:29-30 a prophecy written after the event. Keil and Delitzsch answer them. Cambridge: "It has been inferred, but unnecessarily ... that these two verses are an interpolation."
- Calvin on Jeremiah's courage in saying it: "We then have here a singular example of magnanimity; for the Prophet hesitated not to risk his own life while obeying God."

### (d) 46:28: "of you I will not make a full end"
- Same promise as 30:11, now set against Egypt, the nation the refugees trusted. Keil and Delitzsch say it was added so that no one would think Judah must share Egypt's fate.
- Apparent tension: 44:27 says the Egypt-remnant will be consumed "until there is an end of them," and 46:26 says Egypt will be "inhabited as in the days of old." Calvin answers both:
  - Egypt's survival is not grace: "when God mitigates his rigor towards the unbelieving, he is not yet propitious to them."
  - The image of the tree: the nations stand like a tree whose root is dead, and God's people are a dry trunk with a living root (quote bank).
- Who is "Jacob my servant"? Calvin: "not on the ground of obedience, but because he had chosen him." This matters straight after ch. 44, where Jacob's descendants have just refused to obey. The promise rests on election, not on the remnant's performance. That is the gospel turn of the passage, and it stays inside the text.
- In just measure (ESV) or in measure (KJV). Henry: "they shall be corrected, but not cast off." Poole on the difference between God's punishments of his church and of the wicked: "the rod of the wicked shall not always lie upon the backs of the righteous." Spurgeon's Faith's Checkbook on the twin text 30:11: "He gives us love without measure but chastisement 'in measure.'"
- Fulfillment views: Gill: "the Jews to this day remain a people." Henry extends it to "the gospel church, God's spiritual Israel." JFB citing Calvin: "God, as it were, raises His people from the dead (Ro 11:15)." Keep the application to the covenant people. For the listener: discipline, yes; destruction, never.

### Extra threads worth one line each
- 44:10 "They have not humbled themselves even to this day" contrasts with 44:28 "shall know." Calvin on 44:28: "he does not refer to true and sincere knowledge, but to experience." They will know by experience what they refused to know by faith.
- 44:26: they still swore "As the Lord GOD lives" while serving the queen of heaven. Calvin: they "retained a form of swearing" to "seem to be a holy nation." God takes even that from them. This is the last stage of the shama thread: God's name is gone from mouths that would not listen.
- 46:11 "balm in Gilead" for Egypt echoes 8:22 for Judah. Egypt has the same disease and has no cure either.
- 46:18 "As I live, declares the King, whose name is the LORD of hosts." Pharaoh is "noise," and the real King swears by his life. It pairs with 44:26 ("sworn by my great name").

# Research: Song of Songs 1 — Book Introduction and the Opening Song

Prepared 2026-10-20. Every quotation below was copied from a page or file fetched during this research session, and the URL is given. ## Book introduction and setting

**Text used:** ESV, Song of Songs 1 — https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Song+of+Songs+1&version=ESV. The ESV adds speaker headings (She / He / Others). Its footnote on 1:2 says: "The translators have added speaker identifications based on the gender and number of the Hebrew words."

**Author.** 1:1: "The Song of Songs, which is Solomon's." The ESV cross-reference is 1 Kings 4:32, where Solomon "spoke 3,000 proverbs, and his songs were 1,005." Two older views on the phrase:
- Gill: "Wrote by Solomon, king of Israel, as the "amanuensis" of the Holy Ghost; and not by Hezekiah and his men, as the Jews say" (citing Bava Batra 15a).
- Gill also notes the Hebrew can mean "concerning Solomon."
- PARAPHRASE: modern commentators split. Some take the phrase as authorship ("by Solomon"). Others read it as dedication or subject ("for" or "about" Solomon). Duguid's publisher blurb does not settle the question.

**Date.** If the Song is Solomon's, it comes from his reign, roughly the 10th century BC (c. 970–930 BC; PARAPHRASE, standard chronology).
- Gill: "the Song rather seems to have been written in the middle part of his life, when in the most flourishing circumstances as to body, mind, and estate."
- Gill reports Lightfoot's opinion that it was written about the thirtieth year of Solomon's reign.
- Critical scholars often argue for a later date from the language (PARAPHRASE).

**Title as a Hebrew superlative.** The Hebrew is *Shir hashirim*. It works like "holy of holies" or "King of kings."
- Gill: "it is called "the Song of songs", because the most excellent, as the Holy of holies, King of kings, &c. which, with the Hebrews, express a superlative."
- Matthew Henry (Concise): "This is the Song of songs, excellent above any others."
- Bernard, Sermon 1, makes the same point at length (see the Quote bank).

**Structure.** There is no agreed outline. Older readers saw a single drama or a continuous story. Origen called it a marriage song written "in the manner of a drama" (*dramatis in modum*). That phrase is reported by scholarly summaries in search results; Origen's text itself was not fetched. Modern readers range from "an anthology of love poems" to "one unified poem."
- Kyle Dillon (TGC) frames the questions: "Is this a single poem with a coherent plot, or an anthology of multiple, independent love poems? How many main characters are there? What is Solomon's role in the Song?"
- PARAPHRASE: A workable teaching outline uses the refrain to the daughters of Jerusalem: "do not stir up or awaken love until it pleases" (2:7; 3:5; 8:4). The refrains mark off large sections:
  - 1:2–2:7: longing, and the first meeting
  - 2:8–3:5: spring invitation, search, and finding
  - 3:6–5:1: royal procession, wedding, and the garden
  - 5:2–8:4: absence, search, and renewed praise
  - 8:5–14: love "strong as death," and the closing scenes
- Chapter 1 sits inside the first unit (1:2–2:7).

**History of interpretation (compact).**

1. **Jewish allegory (God and Israel).**
   - Rabbi Akiva (c. AD 100), Mishnah Yadayim 3:5: "all the writings are holy but the Song of Songs is the holy of holies" (full quote in the Quote bank).
   - The Targum and the Midrash read the bride as Israel and the King as God, and trace Israel's history from the Exodus onward. Britannica 1911 (fetched): "The spouse is Israel, her royal lover the divine king, and the poem is explained as tracing the great events of the people's history from the Exodus to the Messianic glory and final restoration."
   - Age limit for readers: Henry and Durham both report that Jewish teachers kept the Song from the young until about age thirty. Henry: "the Jewish doctors advised their young people not to read it till they were thirty years old." Spurgeon also mentions the custom.
   - **Passover:** Jews read the Song during Passover. Practice varies (MyJewishLearning, fetched):
     - Ashkenazim read it on the intermediate Shabbat.
     - Sephardim read it before Mincha on the eighth day (seventh in Israel).
     - Italian Jews read it at Maariv on the first two days.
     - Many communities read it at the end of the seder.
   - Reasons given: the spring setting, the Pharaoh reference in 1:9, and the reading of the Exodus as God's love story with Israel.
2. **Origen (3rd c.).**
   - He wrote a 10-volume Commentary and two Homilies on the Song.
   - Jerome (fetched) said: "Origen, whilst in his other books he has surpassed all others, has in the Song of Songs surpassed himself."
   - Origen set the Christian pattern: the bride is the church or the individual soul, and the bridegroom is Christ (the Word).
   - Britannica 1911 adds that the church-as-bride view "must be regarded as the standard of orthodox exegesis."
   - The Commentary survives in Rufinus' Latin. No public-domain English text was found online; the standard translation is Lawson, ACW 26, which is copyrighted.
3. **Theodore of Mopsuestia (d. 428/9).** He was the Antiochene "literal" reader.
   - Britannica 1911 says Theodore "regarded the Canticles as a poem written by Solomon in answer to the complaints of his people about his Egyptian marriage; and this was one of the heresies charged upon him after his death, which led to his condemnation at the second council of Constantinople (553 A.D.)."
   - Gill says the same: "Theodore of Mopsuest, condemned calling it in question by the second council at Constantinople, in 553."
   - Caveat for the episode: the Song view was one charge among several. The main issue was his Christology (the Three Chapters controversy). Do not imply he was condemned for the Song alone.
4. **Gregory of Nyssa (4th c.).**
   - He wrote fifteen Homilies on the Song (1:1–6:9), reading it as the soul's endless ascent into God.
   - No free English text was accessible. Norris's SBL translation (2012) is in print. No quote is given.
5. **Bernard of Clairvaux (1090–1153).**
   - He preached 86 sermons on the Song (PARAPHRASE: standard count). They reach only the beginning of chapter 3.
   - Sermons 1–8 cover the title and 1:2 ("the kiss"). Quotes are in the Quote bank.
   - Britannica 1911 notes that among medieval mystical commentators "S. Bernard holds a pre-eminent place."
6. **Reformation and Puritans.**
   - **Westminster Annotations** (1645; enlarged 1651/1657): the Assembly-era Bible notes treat the Song as "a divine Parable." Quotes are in the Quote bank, verified against archive.org scans.
   - **James Durham**, *Clavis Cantici* (1668): the Song is to be taken "spiritually, figuratively and allegorically." He adds that its "literal sense is mediate," meaning it is reached through the Spirit's intended figure. He also distinguishes an allegory from a type.
   - **Richard Sibbes**, *Bowels Opened* (sermons on Song 4:16–6:3; Sibbes d. 1635, published posthumously; editions of 1641 and 1648 are recorded): the Song "contains the mutual joys and mutual praises betwixt Christ and his church."
   - **John Gill**: a large *Exposition* of the Song (1728), plus the Song section of his whole-Bible commentary (fetched).
   - **Matthew Henry**: "It is an allegory, the letter of which kills those who rest in that and look no further, but the spirit of which gives life."
7. **The literal / marriage reading.**
   - Castellio (1544) wanted the book removed from the canon as impure.
   - Grotius (1644) read it mainly as conjugal speech between Solomon and Pharaoh's daughter, with a possible secondary sense (Britannica 1911).
   - From the 19th century on, most scholars read the Song as love poetry about a man and a woman.
   - Britannica 1911 (W. Robertson Smith, critical) argues that true allegories carry "internal marks of their allegorical design," and says the Song has none.
8. **Modern Reformed approach.**
   - The plain sense is God's gift of married love. Through the Bible's marriage typology (Hos 1–3; Ezek 16; Eph 5:31–32; Rev 19), it also points to Christ and the church.
   - R. C. Sproul (via a Ligonier devotional) affirms the literal sense first, then an "illustrative" application to Christ and the church.
   - Iain Duguid (Tyndale OTC 2015; Reformed Expository Commentary 2016) "steers a middle way between allegorical and literal approaches" (publisher's description).
   - Kyle Dillon (TGC; Covenant Seminary MDiv) argues the reading is warranted "because marriage itself is designed as an allegory of Christ and the church."

**Canon note.** PARAPHRASE:
- God's name does not clearly appear in the Song. Some read 8:6 "the very flame of the LORD" as a possible exception.
- The NT never quotes it directly.
- Durham answered both points (OCR text, verified): citation in the NT "doth not give authority to them. They are cited as having authority, and not to get it."

## Interpretive questions

### (a) Allegory or literal? How should we read the Song?

**For a mainly spiritual (allegorical) reading:**
- The Jewish tradition from Akiva and the Targum onward.
- Origen, Bernard, and nearly the whole church until the 1800s.
- The Westminster Annotations ("not as an history, or prophecy... but as a divine Parable").
- Durham, Sibbes, Henry, Gill, Spurgeon, and Hudson Taylor.
- Their arguments:
  1. The canon and Jewish/Christian consensus.
  2. Ps 45, which Henry calls "the best key to this book."
  3. The marriage metaphor for God and his people across Scripture.
  4. NT echoes (Henry and Durham list them).
  5. Moral oddities if the Song is read only as Solomon's own romance. This point is PARAPHRASE, from a Purely Presbyterian blog that cites Durham, Poole and Hengstenberg. For example: Solomon's many wives (6:8), and the bride wandering the streets at night.

**For the literal (marriage) reading:**
- Theodore of Mopsuestia, Castellio and Grotius in the past. Most modern scholarship.
- Sproul, as reported by Ligonier: the church "was embarrassed by the sensuous imagery of the Song of Solomon and read it allegorically to get around its approval of marital intimacy."
- The Ligonier devotional warns that "interpreters have often found fanciful meanings in the Song of Solomon through allegorizing the text and disregarding the context of the original biblical passages" (e.g., the woman as Mary).
- Britannica 1911 (Robertson Smith): the Song gives no internal signal that it is an allegory.

**A balanced Reformed answer:**
1. **Begin with the plain sense.** This is a Spirit-inspired song of love between a bride and groom, and marriage and married love are God's good gifts (Gen 2; Prov 5:18–19). Sproul: we may apply it "illustratively" to Christ only if we first read it "in its plainest, literal sense."
2. **Then follow the Bible's own marriage typology.** "This mystery is profound, and I am saying that it refers to Christ and the church" (Eph 5:32). Dillon (TGC): the Song is rightly read as pointing to Christ and the church "because marriage itself is designed as an allegory of Christ and the church." Duguid's summary: "The Song challenges all of us as failed lovers and points us to the perfect Lover, who has loved us and given himself for us" (quoted in a review).
3. **Use the older interpreters' own guardrails.**
   - Durham separates "an allegorick exposition of Scripture" (allegorizing plain history, which he faults Origen for) from "an exposition of allegorick Scripture." The quoted wording comes from the Purely Presbyterian blog and was not checked against the OCR; Durham's statement that the "literal sense is mediate" was verified.
   - The Westminster Annotations add: "Allegories are not to be strained too much, that similitudes answer not in all lines, but in the cheifest" (1657 ed., OCR checked).
   - So: do not make every henna blossom a doctrine. Follow the main lines (longing, union, beauty given by the beloved, communion, and its loss and restoration) to Christ.
4. **Practical upshot for chapter 1.** It is legitimate:
   - to teach from chapter 1 about a wife's longing and a husband's delight;
   - to hear in 1:2–4 the believer's desire for Christ's love "better than wine," and the prayer "Draw me" (John 6:44);
   - to hear in 1:5–6 the "sinner yet saint" paradox.
   Both readings stand. The marriage reading comes first, and the Christ reading rests on it.

### (b) Who is speaking?

- The Hebrew marks gender and number. Masculine or feminine forms, and singular or plural, tell us which voice is speaking. The ESV headings rest on this (see its footnote at 1:2).
- **She (the woman / bride):** 1:2–4a, 1:5–7, 1:12–14, 1:16–17.
- **He (the man / king / shepherd):** 1:8–10, 1:15.
- **Others (the daughters of Jerusalem, or friends):** 1:4b ("We will exult...") and 1:11 ("We will make for you ornaments..."). She speaks to the daughters of Jerusalem directly in 1:5.
- **King or shepherd?** He is called "the king" (1:4, 12) and is also pictured pasturing a flock (1:7–8).
  - PARAPHRASE: Two theories exist. The "two-man" or shepherd theory says a country girl resists Solomon for her shepherd lover. The "one-man" view says the king is pictured as a shepherd. The ESV headings ("Solomon and His Bride Delight in Each Other") take the one-man view.
  - The Purely Presbyterian blog notes that "Solomon was not a shepherd (1:7)." It uses this as an argument for allegory.
- **How the older interpreters assign the voices:**
  - Westminster Annotations 1645 (OCR) on 1:2: "This is spoken in the person of the Church, or of the faithfull soul, enflamed with the desire of more inward familiarity with Christ."
  - Henry calls the "daughters of Jerusalem" "professors not yet established in the faith" (Concise).
  - Gill calls them "young converts, it may be not yet members of the visible church."
  - Hudson Taylor reads 1:5 as dialogue. He gives "I am black" to the bride and "But comely" to the Bridegroom, who "interjects." That is his devotional reading; the Hebrew and the ESV give all of 1:5 to her.

### (c) 1:5–6: "very dark, but lovely" and the unkept vineyard

- **Plain sense.**
  - She is sunburned from field work ("the sun has looked upon me"). In that culture, pale skin meant a life of leisure and tanned skin meant labor.
  - Her brothers ("my mother's sons," a family conflict) put her to work in the vineyards.
  - So she has neglected "my own vineyard," most naturally her own person and appearance. The image returns in 8:12: "My vineyard, my very own, is before me."
  - Kedar's tents were black goat-hair Bedouin tents. Gill quotes Dr. Shaw's travel account: "such hair cloth as our coal sacks are made of."
  - Solomon's curtains were rich and beautiful. The two images are set side by side (dark like Kedar's tents, lovely like Solomon's curtains).
  - Care point: the text is about sun and labor, not ethnicity. Avoid the old misuse of this verse, and the KJV "black, but comely" reading that took "but" as apology.
- **Older spiritual readings:**
  - Henry: "True believers are black in themselves, but comely in Christ... a believer is a sinner and yet a saint."
  - Gill: black "through original sin and actual transgression... but comely in the eyes of Christ... through his comeliness put upon her, the imputation of his righteousness."
  - Henry also reads the blackness as suffering and persecution, and the unkept vineyard as the church's own fault: "I am justly made a slavish keeper of men's vineyards, because I have been a careless keeper of the vineyards God has entrusted me with."
  - Gill cites Augustine, "Nigra per naturam, formosa per gratiam" ("black by nature, beautiful by grace"), and Ambrose, "Fusca per culpam, decora per gratiam." These are cited by Gill and were not checked in Augustine or Ambrose.
  - Bernard (Sermon 3) applies 1:5 to the penitent woman of Luke 7: "There the Ethiopian changed her skin..."
- **The unkept vineyard as a warning to busy servants.**
  - Spurgeon (Sermon 990) and Hudson Taylor both read it this way: tending others' vineyards while neglecting your own soul.
  - Strong pastoral application for church workers. See the Quote bank.

## Interpretive questions

### (a) "My Father is working until now" (5:17): God's Sabbath and providence

- **Genesis 2:2 and providence.** God's rest in Gen 2:2 means he finished creating. It does not mean he stopped governing.
  - Calvin: "In six days, therefore, the creation of the world was completed, but the administration of it is still continued."
  - Chrysostom: the Father's working is that "He cares for, He holds together all that has been made."
  - Ryle restates the idea in Jesus' voice: the Father "has never rested for a moment from His providential government of the world."
  - Philo had already said that God "never ceases from making something or other."
- **What kind of defense this is.**
  - Calvin notes that Jesus does *not* argue that the Sabbath was temporary. He argues that works of God do not violate the Sabbath.
  - Chrysostom: a mere servant could not excuse himself by pointing to the King. "From the charges... from which you absolve God, absolve Me also" only works "since the dignity is equal."
  - The Ligonier devotional points out that Jesus did not appeal to the lawfulness of mercy here (as in the Synoptics). He appealed to "His Father's sovereign prerogative."
  - So the defense is itself a claim to deity, and the leaders understood it that way (5:18).
- **Pastoral application (PCA).**
  - Ryle strongly denies that the passage abolishes the fourth commandment. The passage puts the Sabbath "on the right foundation": works of necessity and mercy are allowed (compare WSC Q.60).
  - Calvin agrees that God's rest "is not idleness, but true perfection."
  - Augustine sees a mystery in it: God "both works while at rest, and rests while working."
  - Augustine also reads the seventh-day rest as pointing to Christ's rest in the tomb after finishing his work on the cross (Tract. 17.15; PARAPHRASE).

### (b) "The Son can do nothing of his own accord" (5:19, 5:30) and full equality

- **The Arian reading.** Arius took this verse as proof that the Son is inferior.
- **The Nicene answers.**
  1. **Augustine (Tract. 18-20): inseparable operations.** There are not two sets of works, one by the Father and one by the Son. "The Father doing every work whatsoever by the Son." The Son's "can do nothing of himself" points to his eternal *origin*, not to inferiority: "I am equal in that wise that He begot me; I am equal in that wise that He is not from me, but I from Him." Augustine also notes, on 5:18, that "the Jews understand what the Arians do not understand."
  2. **Chrysostom (Hom. 38):** "nothing of Himself" means "nothing in opposition to the Father, nothing alien from, nothing strange to Him." In other words, it describes perfect agreement. He also uses 5:21 ("whom he will") against the Arian reading: to give life by one's own will is authority, not weakness.
  3. **Hilary (De Trin. IX.46):** "whatever things the Father does, that the Son does likewise." If the Son can do everything the Father does, where is the weakness? And "one and the same honour is demanded for Him and for the Father" (5:23).
  4. **Calvin's different approach.** Calvin says both the Arians and the Fathers misread the verse, because the discourse concerns "the Son of God, so far as he is manifested in the flesh," that is, Christ as Mediator sent by the Father. This is worth flagging for listeners. Calvin is not denying the Nicene doctrine. He argues *against* the Arians on 5:18. He simply reads 5:19-26 as being about the incarnate Redeemer's office.
- **Synthesis for a Reformed audience.**
  - The Son's dependence (5:19, 30) describes his eternal relation as Son (he is "from" the Father) and his willing obedience as the incarnate Mediator.
  - His equality appears in the same sentences: "whatever the Father does," "gives life to whom he will," "life in himself," "honor the Son, just as they honor the Father."
  - WCF 2.3: "of one substance, power, and eternity... the Son is eternally begotten of the Father."
  - WCF 8.2: "very and eternal God, of one substance and equal with the Father."
  - On "life in himself" (5:26): Chrysostom says "has given" introduces only the Father/Son distinction, "but declares that all the rest is equal and exactly alike."

### (c) Two resurrections (5:24-25 and 5:28-29), and "those who have done good"

- **Two resurrections.**
  - **5:24-25 is spiritual and present.** Note "and is now here." This is regeneration and conversion: the spiritually dead hear Christ's voice through the gospel.
  - **5:28-29 is bodily and future.** Note "in the tombs," "all," and that "now is" is left out.
  - Augustine: "they rise now in the mind, then in the flesh."
  - Calvin: "It is generally agreed that he speaks of spiritual death," and on 5:28 he observes that "that limitation — and now is — is not again added."
  - Ryle: 5:25 is "the quickening of souls, the resurrection of conversion."
  - Mathison (Ligonier) calls this "inaugurated eschatology."
  - Spurgeon: "In regeneration lies the essence and major portion of resurrection."
  - Possible tie-back to last episode (PARAPHRASE/observation): the healed man of 5:1-15 heard Jesus' word "Get up" and rose. That healing is a small picture of 5:25.
- **"Those who have done good" vs. justification by faith alone.**
  - Calvin: Christ "points out believers by good works, as he elsewhere teaches that a tree is known by its fruit." Here "Christ does not now treat of the cause of salvation, but merely distinguishes the elect from the reprobate by their own mark." Even believers' good works are accepted only because "God pardon the sins which belong to it."
  - Ryle on 5:24 stresses the "presentness" and "completeness" of justification: "It is not a far distant thing which he is to have at last, if he does his duty and is good."
  - Augustine (Tract. 22) explains that "does not come into judgment" (5:24) refers to judgment as *condemnation*, not as *discrimination*. Believers still appear before Christ's judgment seat (2 Cor 5:10) (PARAPHRASE of Tract. 22.5).
  - WCF 33.1: all will "receive according to what they have done in the body," but WSC Q.38 says believers will be "openly acknowledged and acquitted."
- **Son of Man as judge (5:27).**
  - Augustine: the Father "will not be seen in the judgment." The Judge will appear in "the form of a servant." "That form will judge which was judged."
  - This connects Daniel 7 to the cross.

## Summary

- **Movement of the passage.** John 5:16-30 turns a Sabbath dispute into Jesus' fullest statement in the Gospels of his relation to the Father.
- **5:17.** "My Father is working until now" rests on a teaching Jews already held: God's Gen 2:2 rest ended his creating but not his providence (Philo; Calvin; Chrysostom; Ryle). By saying "and I am working," Jesus claims the divine right to that work, and his opponents rightly hear a claim to equality (5:18; Augustine: "the Jews understand what the Arians do not").
- **5:19.** "Nothing of his own accord" is not a confession of weakness. It speaks of one shared work and the Son's eternal origin from the Father (Augustine, Chrysostom, Hilary). Calvin adds a twist worth discussing: he reads the passage of Christ as incarnate Mediator.
- **5:21-27.** The Son gives life, judges, has "life in himself," and receives equal honor. These are divine prerogatives (WCF 2.3, 8.2).
- **Two resurrections.** 5:24-25 is the spiritual resurrection happening now through the gospel ("and is now here"). 5:28-29 is the bodily resurrection of all at the last day (Dan 12:2; "now is" omitted). Augustine: "now in the mind, then in the flesh."
- **"Those who have done good."** Calvin answers: works are the mark of the justified, not the cause of justification. This fits 5:24, where life comes by hearing and believing (Ryle on the "presentness" and "completeness" of justification; WSC 38).
- **Bridge from last episode.** The man at Bethesda heard Jesus' word and rose. 5:25 says that the same voice now raises dead souls and will one day empty the tombs.

## Setting and background

- **Where we are.** Last episode covered 1 Peter 1:17-25: living as exiles in reverent fear, ransomed "with the precious blood of Christ," and "born again, not of perishable seed but of imperishable, through the living and abiding word of God" (1:23), with Isaiah 40:6-8 ("the word of the Lord remains forever"). Peter closes: "And this word is the good news that was preached to you" (1:25). Chapter 2 picks up straight from there.
- **Text used:** ESV, 1 Peter 2:1-10 — https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1+Peter+2%3A1-10&version=ESV. The ESV heading for 2:1-10 is "A Living Stone and a Holy People."
- **The "So" of 2:1.** "So put away all malice and all deceit and hypocrisy and envy and all slander" (2:1). The "so" (KJV "Wherefore") draws a conclusion from 1:22-25. You were born again by the word, so live like newborns and feed on the word. Calvin: "After having taught the faithful that they had been regenerated by the word of God, he now exhorts them to lead a life corresponding with their birth." Leighton makes the same link: the word that begot them is the word that feeds them (quotes below).
- **The wordplay in 2:1-2.** 2:1 says to put away all *dolos* ("deceit," "guile"). 2:2 says to long for milk that is *adolos* ("pure," "without guile"), the same root with a negative prefix. The milk is also *logikos*, which can mean "rational/spiritual" (as in Rom 12:1, "spiritual worship") or "of the word" (*logos*, picking up 1:23-25). Bengel's Gnomen (biblehub, 2:2): "λογικὸν) Derived from λόγος, the Word, ch. 1 Peter 1:23. The milk of the Word is a periphrasis for the Word itself." And: "ἄδολον, without guile) The antithesis to guile in 1 Peter 2:1." Bengel also says the milk "is the same as that which is before called seed, ch. 1 Peter 1:23." (https://biblehub.com/commentaries/1_peter/2-2.htm)
  - Calvin took *logikos* the "rational" way. His 19th-century editor disagreed in a footnote and argued for "milk of the word" (both quoted below). That disagreement is a good on-air moment.
  - The ESV's "pure spiritual milk" keeps both options open. The KJV's "sincere milk of the word" picks "of the word."
- **"Grow up into salvation."** The KJV ends 2:2 at "that ye may grow thereby." Older Greek manuscripts add "into salvation," and modern versions include it. Bengel's Gnomen: "The copies of greatest authority have long read, ΕἸς ΣΩΤΗΡΊΑΝ" (with an editor's note listing "ABC Vulg. and almost all Versions"). Salvation here is the future goal (compare 1:5, 1:9).
- **2:3 quotes Psalm 34:8.** ESV Ps 34:8: "Oh, taste and see that the LORD is good! Blessed is the man who takes refuge in him!" Peter applies "the LORD" (YHWH) to Christ, because 2:4 goes on "As you come to him, a living stone." The same Psalm is quoted again at 1 Peter 3:10-12. (https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Psalm+34%3A8%3BIsaiah+28%3A16%3BPsalm+118%3A22%3BIsaiah+8%3A14-15%3BMatthew+16%3A18%3BHebrews+5%3A12-14%3B1+Corinthians+3%3A1-2&version=ESV)
- **The metaphor shifts at 2:4-5.** Babes become stones. Christ is "a living stone rejected by men but in the sight of God chosen and precious," and believers "like living stones are being built up as a spiritual house, to be a holy priesthood, to offer spiritual sacrifices acceptable to God through Jesus Christ." The verb *oikodomeisthe* can be read as a statement ("are being built") or a command ("be built"). Calvin notes the ambiguity, Pulpit Commentary prefers the imperative, and JFB/Bengel prefer the indicative (quotes below).
- **Three Old Testament stone texts (2:6-8).**
  - Isaiah 28:16 (ESV): "Behold, I am the one who has laid as a foundation in Zion, a stone, a tested stone, a precious cornerstone, of a sure foundation: 'Whoever believes will not be in haste.'" Peter follows the Greek: "will not be put to shame." Paul quotes the same verse in Rom 9:33 and 10:11. Isaiah's context is Judah's leaders trusting in a "covenant with death" and in Egypt (PARAPHRASE of the Isaiah 28 context; Ligonier's devotional summarizes it the same way).
  - Psalm 118:22 (ESV): "The stone that the builders rejected has become the cornerstone." Jesus applied this to himself (Matt 21:42), and Peter used it before the Sanhedrin (Acts 4:11).
  - Isaiah 8:14-15 (ESV): "And he will become a sanctuary and a stone of offense and a rock of stumbling to both houses of Israel... And many shall stumble on it. They shall fall and be broken." In Isaiah the stone is the LORD of hosts himself (8:13). Peter applies it to Christ.
- **Peter's own name.** ESV Matt 16:18: "And I tell you, you are Peter, and on this rock I will build my church, and the gates of hell shall not prevail against it." (ESV footnote: "The Greek words for Peter and rock sound similar.") Peter wrote about stones and building, but he puts Christ, not himself, as the cornerstone. The church is made of many "living stones." Ligonier's devotional and the Pulpit Commentary both make this point (quotes below). The Pulpit Commentary adds that Peter once became a "rock of offense" himself (Matt 16:23).
- **Milk elsewhere in the NT.** In 1 Cor 3:1-2 and Heb 5:12-14, milk means elementary teaching, and staying on it is a rebuke ("You need milk, not solid food"). Peter's use is different. See interpretive question (a).
- **2:9-10** (not in today's reading, but its payoff) quotes Exodus 19:6 ("a royal priesthood, a holy nation") and Hosea. It is worth previewing because 2:5's "holy priesthood" points ahead to it.

## Outline with key verses

1. **Put off the old (2:1).** "So put away all malice and all deceit and hypocrisy and envy and all slander." This follows from the new birth (1:23). All five vices are sins against the "sincere brotherly love" of 1:22. Calvin: the list is a sample, not a full catalogue.
2. **Crave the word like a newborn (2:2-3).** "Like newborn infants, long for the pure spiritual milk, that by it you may grow up into salvation — if indeed you have tasted that the Lord is good." The word that begot you is also the word that feeds you. The appetite comes from a first taste of Christ's kindness (Ps 34:8).
3. **Come to the Living Stone (2:4).** "As you come to him, a living stone rejected by men but in the sight of God chosen and precious." The participle "coming" is present tense, an ongoing approach (Spurgeon's 1877 sermon is built on this). Men's verdict is set against God's verdict.
4. **Be built up as God's house and priesthood (2:5).** "You yourselves like living stones are being built up as a spiritual house, to be a holy priesthood, to offer spiritual sacrifices acceptable to God through Jesus Christ." The church is now the temple and the priests of the temple. The sacrifices are acceptable only "through Jesus Christ."
5. **Scripture's three stones (2:6-8).**
   - 2:6, Isa 28:16, the cornerstone laid by God in Zion: "whoever believes in him will not be put to shame."
   - 2:7a, "So the honor is for you who believe" (KJV "Unto you therefore which believe he is precious").
   - 2:7b, Ps 118:22, the rejected stone is vindicated.
   - 2:8a, Isa 8:14, "A stone of stumbling, and a rock of offense."
   - 2:8b, "They stumble because they disobey the word, as they were destined to do."
   - The same stone is either foundation or ruin. Calvin: "we must either build on him, or be dashed against him."

## Interpretive questions

### (a) What is the "milk," and does milk imply immaturity?

- **The milk is the word of God.** The "of the word" reading of *logikos*, the link back to 1:23-25 ("the living and abiding word... this word is the good news that was preached to you"), and the *dolos*/*adolos* wordplay all point the same way. Bengel, Poole, Gill, Leighton, Calvin's editor, Ligonier and Piper all identify the milk with the word or the gospel.
  - Calvin himself took *logikos* as "rational." For him the point is that the milk is guileless but not mindless, and he ties it to "Be ye not children in understanding, but in malice" (1 Cor 14:20). His editor's footnote disagrees: "the word is the milk."
  - Clement of Alexandria (*Paedagogus* I.6) reads the milk christologically: the Word himself is the nourishment.
- **Milk here does not mean beginner material.** Peter does not set milk against solid food as Paul (1 Cor 3:1-2) and Hebrews (5:12-14) do. His contrast is between newborn appetite and the "ancientness of the flesh" (Calvin), or between pure food and deceit (the wordplay).
  - Calvin is explicit that this milk is "a perpetual aliment" because "the infancy of the new life is perpetual."
  - Clement's chapter title makes the same point: "The Name Children Does Not Imply Instruction in Elementary Principles."
  - Ligonier: "Peter does not use the term 'milk' in this way."
  - Piper: "meaning the word, not distinguishing it from meat at all."
- **Augustine (Tractate 98 on John)** is useful on the 1 Cor 3 / Heb 5 side. For him milk and solid food are often the same Christ crucified, taken in differently by the weak and the mature: "what both heard in the same measure when it was publicly spoken, each apprehended in his own measure." This helps explain how Paul can rebuke "milk" while Peter commends it.
- **Pastoral angle.** The question is about appetite, not level. Mature Christians should never outgrow craving the word. The evidence of new birth is a new taste ("if indeed you have tasted"). Piper: "This is what the new birth gives you: a new taste." The Westminster Larger Catechism (Q. 155) names the "reading, but especially the preaching of the word" as the means by which the Spirit is "building them up in grace." Leighton, a preacher, applies 2:2 to the hearer in the pew.

### (b) The priesthood of believers: what it means and what it does not mean

- **What it means.**
  - The temple has moved. God's house is now the people, built on Christ, and every believer has priestly access and priestly service. Calvin: "a singular honor, that God should not only consecrate us as a temple to himself... but that he should also make us priests."
  - The spiritual sacrifices are, first, ourselves (Rom 12:1), then "prayers, thanksgiving, almsdeeds, and all the duties of religion" (Calvin). Hebrews 13:15-16 is close by.
  - The priesthood is corporate. *Hierateuma* means "a multitude of priests" (Bengel). Calvin: "he constructs one house from the whole number of the faithful."
  - Luther pressed the doctrine against the medieval clergy/laity divide, using 1 Peter 2:9 ("we are all consecrated as priests by baptism").
- **What it does not mean.**
  1. It does not mean another atoning priesthood. Christ's priestly sacrifice is unique and "altogether incommunicable" (Leighton). There is no sacrifice of the Mass (JFB; Poole: never "appropriated to gospel ministers").
  2. It does not mean every believer holds church office. Presbyterian polity keeps a called, ordained ministry of the word. Westminster Larger Catechism Q. 158: "The Word of God is to be preached only by such as are sufficiently gifted, and also duly approved and called to that office."
     - Even Luther in the same treatise: "no man may put himself forward or take upon himself, without our consent and election, to do that which we have all alike power to do."
     - Leighton (an Anglican bishop, later archbishop) speaks of "the peculiar holiness of the ministry" in the same breath as "all you who are Christians... are priests unto God."
     - JFB on biblehub: the minister "merely for order's sake leads the spiritual services of the people," and the NT never calls ministers *hiereus*.
     - So the doctrine abolishes a mediating priestly caste, not ordained ministry. The ordained presbyter is not a priest standing between the people and God. He is a servant of the word among a priestly people.
  3. It does not mean individualism. "Every man his own priest" misses the point. The priesthood is built together into one house. Ligonier: "we cannot love Christ without also loving His church."

### (c) 2:8: "as they were destined to do." What is appointed, the stumbling or the disobedience?

- **Grammar.** "To which (*eis ho*) also they were appointed (*etethēsan*)." The antecedent can be:
  1. the stumbling, as the judicial result of disobedience;
  2. the disobedience/unbelief itself;
  3. (an older view Calvin reports and Poole lists first) their being appointed to *believe*. The Jews were outwardly called to the covenant, which makes their unbelief worse.
- **Calvin** allows both of the first two, but leans toward appointment to unbelief: "the other sense is equally suitable, that they had been appointed to unbelief; as Pharaoh is said to have been set up for this end... And what inclines me to this meaning is the particle καὶ (also)." He also insists on human responsibility: Christ is a stone of stumbling "to none for destruction, but to those who, through headstrong wickedness and obstinacy, rush against the word of God," and it is "as it were an accidental thing that he becomes a rock of offense."
- **Calvin's editor (footnote 24)** prefers that what they were destined to was the stumbling: "they being disobedient or unbelieving were destined to stumble at the word, and thereby to fall and to be broken."
- **Bengel** also ties it to the stumbling, and he stresses order: "This appointment follows unbelief and stumbling." God actively "appoints" Christ and the elect (2:6, "I lay"), while unbelievers "are said to be appointed" in the passive.
- **Pulpit Commentary** prefers the stumbling: "it is God's ordinance that disobedience should end in stumbling."
- **Poole** lists the outward-covenant view first. Second, he gives the reprobation reading: God "appointed them to this stumbling, in his decreeing not to give them faith in Christ, but to leave them to their unbelief, and to punish them justly for it."
- **Leighton** sees God's decree here but refuses to speculate past it: "I will rather stand on the shore, and silently admire it, than enter into it."
- **Westminster Confession 3.7** gives the confessional frame: God was pleased "to pass by; and to ordain them to dishonor and wrath *for their sin*." WCF 3.8 asks that the doctrine be "handled with special prudence and care."
- **Ligonier:** "This unbelief does not surprise the Lord but is part of His eternal decree... God is not culpable for such evil."
- **Suggested framing for the episode.** Both main readings sit comfortably within Reformed theology, and Calvin himself holds them loosely. The safest exegesis follows the sentence's own logic. "They stumble because they disobey the word." Disobedience is the stated cause, and the stumbling, the judicial outcome, is what God appointed (editor, Bengel, Pulpit). The wider Reformed point stands either way: even unbelief is not outside God's purpose, and the guilt is entirely the unbeliever's (WCF 3.7, "for their sin").
- **Peter's purpose is pastoral.** He wants to steady suffering exiles who see the "builders" reject Christ, not to fuel speculation. Calvin: "lest the contempt or the rejection of him should move us from the faith." Poole: "to keep weak Christians from being offended at the multitude of unbelievers."

## Summary

1 Peter 2:1-8 turns the new birth "through the living and abiding word" (1:23) into a way of life.

- **2:1-3.** Put off the deceit (*dolos*) that sours community, and crave the guileless (*adolos*) milk of the word with a newborn's hunger. That hunger comes from having tasted that the Lord (Christ, read through Ps 34:8) is good.
- **2:4-5.** Coming to Christ, the rejected but chosen and precious Living Stone, believers are built together into God's temple and serve as its priests, offering spiritual sacrifices that are acceptable only through Christ.
- **2:6-8.** Three OT stone texts (Isa 28:16; Ps 118:22; Isa 8:14) show that the same Christ is either the believer's foundation and honor or the unbeliever's ruin.

**Key interpretive conclusions:**
- **(a) The milk.** It is the word itself, and here it is a lifelong diet, not beginner's fare. Calvin, his editor, Bengel, Clement, Ligonier and Piper all say Peter's contrast is not 1 Cor 3 / Heb 5. Augustine helps explain those other passages.
- **(b) The priesthood of believers.** It abolishes a mediating priestly caste and gives every believer access and spiritual sacrifice (Calvin, Leighton, Luther). It does not abolish called and ordained ministry (WLC 158; Leighton; even Luther's "without our consent and election"), and it is corporate, not individualist.
- **(c) "As they were destined."** Calvin leans toward appointment to unbelief. His editor, Bengel and the Pulpit Commentary tie the appointment to the stumbling that follows disobedience. All hold that God is sovereign and the unbeliever is guilty (WCF 3.7 "for their sin"). Peter's aim is pastoral: to steady believers who see Christ rejected.

**Strongest on-air material:**
- Calvin's "we must either build on him, or be dashed against him."
- Calvin's "the infancy of the new life is perpetual."
- Leighton's "I will rather stand on the shore, and silently admire it."
- Spurgeon's first-ever sermon at sixteen on 2:7.
- Spurgeon's "the whole Christian life is drawn in one line— coming unto Christ."
- The Peter/*petra* irony (Pulpit Commentary; Ligonier).
