## Lamentations 2 (Old Testament reading)

### Setting and background
- Second of five poems; written after the fall of Jerusalem, fifth month (Av) of 586 B.C., Nebuzaradan burning the temple (Jer 52:12-14). Traditional author Jeremiah (LXX preface; confessional tradition). Calvin lectures on it as Jeremiah's (Lectures 4-8 of Lamentations).
- Second "eikah" acrostic, 22 stanzas, mostly three lines each; 2:19 has four lines (Keil and Delitzsch note the irregularity and refuse to cut it: "we must simply acknowledge the irregularity").
- pe before ayin (2:16 pe, 2:17 ayin) — chapters 2, 3, 4 all do this; chapter 1 does not. Gill passes on: Grotius (Chaldean manner), Lightfoot (ayin = 70, the seventy years), Bedford (prophet's confusion), Jarchi/Rashi from the Talmud (Sanhedrin 104b): "they spoke with their mouths what they saw not with their eyes; 'pe' signifying the mouth, and 'ain' an eye." URL: https://biblehub.com/commentaries/lamentations/2-16.htm
- Archaeology: Izbet Sartah abecedary (early 12th century B.C., early Israelite site) has pe before ayin; same in an 8th-century abecedary at Kuntillet Ajrud; BAR: "It also appears three times in the Bible: in the acrostics that make up the Book of Lamentations 2–4." (https://library.biblicalarchaeology.org/sidebar/the-abcs-of-an-abecedary/ via WebFetch; use as background, not quote). So likely an alternative ancient alphabet order, not a mistake.
- Chapter 1: narrator then Zion; focus on Zion's sin and lack of comforter. Chapter 2: focus on the Lord as actor. "The Lord" is subject of almost every verb in 2:1-8. Chapter 1's key word: menachem. Chapter 2: bala' "swallow" (2:2, 2:5 twice, 2:8 "from destroying" = mibbalea', 2:16 enemies: "we have swallowed her"). Also "without pity" (2:2, 17, 21), "day of his anger" (2:1, 21, 22).
- Lord's "footstool" = ark (1 Chr 28:2; Ps 99:5; 132:7). Ark disappears from history here.
- 2:6 "his booth" sok/sukko — the word for a booth (Ellicott: "like those erected in the Feast of Tabernacles"), "festival and Sabbath" forgotten. 2:7 "clamor in the house of the Lord as on the day of festival"; 2:22 "summoned as if to a festival day my terrors on every side" — magor missabib, Jeremiah's signature phrase (Jer 6:25; 20:3 Pashhur renamed; 20:10; Ellicott). The festival vocabulary is inverted. CONNECTION: John 7 is the Feast of Booths (Sukkot).
- 2:8 measuring line: builder's tool used for demolition (2 Kings 21:13; Isa 34:11; Amos 7:7-8) — Keil and Delitzsch.
- 2:9 "the law is no more; her prophets find no vision" (Ps 74:9). Ezekiel 7:26 similar.
- 2:14 "false and deceptive visions... not exposed your iniquity to restore your fortunes (shub shebut)... oracles (massa'ot, burdens; Jer 23:33-40) false and misleading". Calvin's editor: meduchim maybe "airy things."
- 2:15 quotes Psalm 48:2 ("joy of all the earth") and Psalm 50:2 ("perfection of beauty").
- 2:17 "carried out his word which he commanded long ago" = Leviticus 26, Deuteronomy 28. 2:20 cannibalism = Deuteronomy 28:53-57; Leviticus 26:29; seen at Samaria (2 Kings 6:28-29); Josephus on Titus's siege (Calvin).

### Interpretive questions
1. Is God Israel's enemy? 2:5 "like an enemy" (ke'oyev). Gill: Broughton renders "the Lord is become a very enemy" taking caph as reality not similitude; Gill himself: "God sometimes appears as if he was an enemy to his people, when he is not." Henry: "God is not really an enemy to his people, no, not when he is angry with them and corrects them in anger." Calvin: the forgetting refers "to the thoughts of men." Keil and Delitzsch: no pause in the accents — paraphrase: they reject Gerlach's view that the poet hesitates. Host: the "like" (as in 1:1 "like a widow") is real, but do not soften — he acts exactly as an enemy acts; for the faithful remnant it is fatherly displeasure (WCF 11.5); for the hardened, real wrath. Ligonier devotional: God "fights not only against blatant enemies but also against those who bear His name when they refuse to repent"; Israel name double entendre.
2. Who swallowed Jerusalem? Enemies say "we have swallowed her" (2:16); the poem answers, 2:17, the Lord did what he purposed. Calvin on 2:17: the wicked are God's scourges. Henry: "They are but the sword in God's hand". Henry: "there is an exact agreement between the judgments of God's hand and the judgments of his mouth". One NT move: Isaiah 25:8 "he will swallow up death forever" (bala'), quoted by Paul in 1 Corinthians 15:54.
3. The word of God in the chapter: no law, no vision (2:9); false visions (2:14); God carried out his word (2:17). Calvin on 2:9: promise "like a small light kindled in darkness." Gregory the Great on 2:14: "the language of reproof is the key of discovery." Poole: false prophets "tickled your humours instead of discovering your sins." Calvin on 2:14: mark to distinguish faithful servants from impostors.
4. Can you argue with God? 2:20 "Look, O Lord, and see! With whom have you dealt thus?" Calvin: they set before God his covenant ("the gratuitous election of God must have been abundantly sufficient to draw forth mercy"). Poole: "let thy former relation to us... prevail with thee." 2:19 command to pray. Calvin on 2:18: not every cry is repentance; on 2:18 the walls weep where hearts would not.

# Research: Song of Songs 7 (ESV 7:1-13)

All quotations below were checked by script, word for word, against page text saved in `/home/user/old-paths-podcast/work/raw/song7_*.txt` (whitespace normalized only). Durham's text comes from an archive.org OCR of the 1723 Glasgow edition and keeps its long-s spelling ("fhe" = she, "defire" = desire). Each Durham quote is given exactly as the OCR has it, followed by a bracketed **normalized reading**. That reading changes spelling only and must be labeled as modernized if used on air.

## 1. Setting and background

- **Place in the Song.** Chapter 7 follows the bride's restoration after the night-search of 5:2-6:3. In 6:4-10 the Bridegroom re-praises her, then comes the call of 6:13: "Return, return, O Shulammite... Why should you look upon the Shulammite, as upon a dance before two armies?" (ESV; Heb. "the dance of Mahanaim"). Chapter 7 opens with a third *wasf* (descriptive praise-song; the earlier ones are 4:1-7 and 6:4-7). It runs **from the feet upward** (7:1-5), unlike 4:1ff., which runs from the head down. Ellicott ties the order to the dance of 6:13: "because the feet of a dancer would first attract attention" (song7_bh7_1). Commentators disagree about who speaks in 7:1-5. Pulpit and Keil-Delitzsch give it to the watching court ladies. Henry, Gill and Durham give it to the Bridegroom himself. From 7:6-9 the King speaks ("How beautiful and pleasant you are, O loved one"). The bride breaks in at 9b, and 7:10-13 is entirely hers.
- **Verse numbering.** English 6:13 = Hebrew 7:1, so English 7:1-13 = Hebrew 7:2-14. BDB cites the *teshuqah* line as "Cant 7:11" and the mandrakes as "Cant 7:14" (song7_h8669, song7_h1736). Keep this in mind when reading Hebrew-based sources.
- **Second refrain stage.** 7:10 is the third form of the mutual-possession refrain (2:16, "My beloved is mine, and I am his"; 6:3, "I am my beloved's and my beloved is mine"; 7:10, "I am my beloved's, and his desire is for me"). Hudson Taylor and Durham both treat the progression as a deepening of assurance. Keil-Delitzsch note that "we miss the 'and my beloved is mine'" of 6:3.
- **Place names in 7:4-5:**
  - *Heshbon*: the old Amorite royal city of Sihon (Num 21:26), later Reubenite, then Moabite. It sits on the plateau east of the north end of the Dead Sea. Ellicott and Cambridge note a large ancient reservoir at Hesban outside the walls. "The point of comparison is the soft shimmer of the eyes" (Cambridge).
  - *Bath-rabbim*: "daughter of many," a gate of Heshbon. Either it led toward Rabbah (Gill) or the name means "populous" (K-D, Henry: "because a great thoroughfare").
  - *Tower of Lebanon looking toward Damascus*: a watchtower on the Hermon/Amana spurs (Pulpit, quoting Delitzsch). Henry reads it as "the boldness of the church in facing its enemies."
  - *Carmel*: the headland "crown of the country, towering over sea and land in solitary majesty" (Cambridge). Some take *karmel* as "crimson" (*karmil*). K-D reject this, and Ellicott suggests a wordplay that leads into "purple" (Carmel being near Tyre's dye industry).
  - *Purple (argaman) hair*: glossy blue-black tresses (Cambridge, Pulpit). "A king is held captive in the tresses."
- **Mandrakes (dudaim, 7:13).** The word is from the root *dod*, "love," so "love-apples" (Ellicott, JFB). It occurs elsewhere only in Gen 30:14-16, Reuben's mandrakes that Rachel bargained for. The plant is *Mandragora*, with fragrant yellow-to-ruddy fruit ripening at wheat harvest (May) and was reputed to be an aphrodisiac and a fertility aid. Cambridge thinks it is "mentioned here as denoting the time of year... or for its pleasant smell, not, as in Genesis 30:14-16, as an aphrodisiac." Pulpit notes the plant was abundant in Galilee, the bride's home country. There is also a wordplay with *dodai*, "my love(s)," at the end of 7:12.

## 2. Outline

1. **7:1-5 The praise of the "noble daughter" (bat-nadib), from feet to hair.** Sandaled feet, thighs like jewels, navel and belly (a heap of wheat encircled with lilies), breasts like twin fawns, neck like an ivory tower, eyes like the pools of Heshbon, nose like the tower of Lebanon, head like Carmel. Key line, 5b: "a king is held captive in the tresses."
2. **7:6-9a The King's delight and resolve.** "How beautiful and pleasant you are, O loved one, with all your delights!" Her stature is a palm, and he says, "I will climb the palm tree and lay hold of its fruit."
3. **7:9b-10 The bride's answer and assurance.** The wine "goes down smoothly for my beloved." Then the key verse, **7:10**: "I am my beloved's, and his desire is for me."
4. **7:11-13 The bride's invitation.** "Come, my beloved, let us go out into the fields and lodge in the villages," early to the vineyards (budding vine, pomegranates), "There I will give you my love." Mandrakes give fragrance, and over the doors are "all choice fruits, new as well as old, which I have laid up for you, O my beloved."

## 3. Interpretive questions

### Q1. What do we do with so physical a wasf? Literal, allegorical, or both?
- **Keil-Delitzsch / Pulpit / Cambridge (natural-marital reading first).** They read 7:1-5 as the court ladies' praise of the dancing queen. Pulpit urges restraint about allegorizing details: "We must not expect to find a symbolical meaning for all the details of such a description. The general intention is to set forth the beauty and glory of the bride." Pulpit also calls the description "perfectly chaste," and it still applies the whole to the Church.
- **Henry / Gill / Durham / Poole (Christ and the church).** They allegorize part by part: feet = gospel walk (Eph 6:15), thighs = the joints of the body (Eph 4:16), eyes = understanding, and so on. Durham asks four questions about *why* this third praise comes now, why it is longer, why the images differ, and why it starts at the feet. His answer: Christ is "not kind only at fits," and the change of order is "a piece of his sovereignty" (spelling normalized from the OCR). Henry says the new similitudes show "the beauty of holiness is such as nothing in nature can reach."
- **A pastoral middle path for the podcast.** Read it first as the Bible's frank celebration of married love. Then read it as a window on how Christ regards a bride He has made beautiful. Note that 7:1-13 follows a restoration (5:2-6:13): the praise is fuller *after* failure, not before.

### Q2. Teshuqah (7:10) and Genesis 3:16. Is the curse reversed?
- The noun *teshuqah* occurs only three times: Gen 3:16 (woman toward man), Gen 4:7 (sin toward Cain), and Song 7:10 (man toward woman). Only here is it unambiguously positive (BDB; Bible Hub Topical Lexicon).
- **Older voices see an echo or reversal.** Poole: our duty is to desire Him "according to God's sentence, Genesis 3:16, but it is a wonderful condescension in him, that his desire is towards me." Durham reads it the same way: Christ condescends to love "as a woman hath to her husband." Clarke: "This was a part of the woman's curse. Now here it seems to be reversed." Henry calls it "a periphrasis of the conjugal relation, Gen. iii. 16." K-D: "The second line here refers back to Genesis 3:16."
- **Theological point.** In Genesis the woman's longing meets "he shall rule over you." In the Song the man's longing is turned toward her, and there is no word of rule. That makes it a glimpse of marriage restored, and for the typological reading, of Christ's love that *seeks* His bride (Henry: Christ's desire "will again bring him from heaven to earth to receive them to himself"). Caution: whether Gen 3:16 *teshuqah* means a desire to control is disputed among Reformed scholars (the Foh/Collins view vs. its critics, e.g. TGC "Problems with a New Reading of an Old Verse"). The podcast can make the reversal point without settling that debate.

### Q3. "Let us go out to the fields" and "new as well as old": what is the bride asking for?
- **Communion in retreat and in labor.** Henry: those who would converse with Christ "must go forth from the world." Spurgeon (MTP 605) finds three things: self-examination *with Him* ("let us see if the vine has budded"), active service *with Him* ("lodge in the villages"), and a store of fruit laid up *for Him*. Poole and Benson read the vineyards as congregations, an inquiry into "the success of our labours." Gill and JFB add the outward, missionary reading (fields and villages = the Gentile world, Acts 8).
- **Assurance leads to diligence, not ease.** Gill: assurance "has no tendency to licentiousness, but excites to duty." JFB: "Assurance fosters diligence, not indolence." Durham: the clearer the interest, "the greater will their defire be of more near communion with him."
- **New and old.** Poole: the fruits of this year and the former, "spiritually meant of the various fruits and operations of the Spirit." Durham links it to the scribe of Matt 13:52 and reads it as growth in grace: old grace kept up only by new fruit. Gill (alternative): "the doctrines of the Old and New Testament; which, for matter and substance, are the same." Spurgeon: new resolves, prayers and labors, and also old fruit: "first love," "first faith," old remembrances of God's faithfulness, old repentances. "But here is the point—they are all to be for Christ."

# Research: John 7:1-13 (ESV): "My Time Has Not Yet Come"

All quotations below were copied from page text downloaded to `work/raw/john7_*.txt` and checked with `grep -F` against those files. Raw files are named next to each source.

## 1. Setting and background

**Chronology.** John 6:4 put the feeding of the 5,000 near Passover (spring). John 7:2 brings us to Booths/Tabernacles (15-21 Tishri, plus an eighth day of assembly, Lev 23:34-36), which falls in late September or October. That is about six months later, and John passes over it in silence. The sources give the gap differently: Chrysostom says "the five months" (Hom. 48), the Pulpit Commentary says "six months had elapsed", and MacArthur says "about seven months later". The safe on-air wording is "about six months". Calvin and Ryle both note that John is selecting events, not giving a continuous narrative. This is Jesus' next-to-last trip to Jerusalem (Bengel: "This journey to the Feast of Tabernacles was His last journev but one to Jerusalem" [sic, "journev" is in the source]). The next Passover will be the cross. Luke's travel narrative (Luke 9:51ff., through Samaria) probably fits into this window (MacArthur).

**Why Jesus is in Galilee.** "The Jews were seeking to kill him" (7:1; cf. 5:18). The fathers stress that this withdrawal was not weakness. Chrysostom: "as man He fled, as God He appeared". Augustine: He "offered an example to our infirmity". Calvin: "while Christ avoided dangers, he did not turn aside a hairsbreadth from the course of duty."

**The Feast of Booths (Sukkot).**
- Lev 23:33-43: seven days living in booths, "that your generations may know that I made the people of Israel dwell in booths when I brought them out of the land of Egypt." It was also the ingathering, a harvest-home (Exod 23:16; Deut 16:13-15: "you shall be altogether joyful"). It was one of the three pilgrim feasts that males were required to attend (Deut 16:16). That is why the brothers assume everyone is going, and why the caravans from Galilee were large (Luke 2:44).
- Josephus called it the holiest and greatest feast (cited by Ellicott). Cambridge calls it "the most joyous of the Jewish festivals".
- Zech 14:16: the nations will go up "to keep the Feast of Booths". Cyril works this text into his reading of 7:8.
- **Ceremonies (useful for 7:37-39 and 8:12 next time).** Mishnah Sukkah 4:9 describes the water libation. A golden jug was filled "with water from the Siloam pool" and carried in with trumpet blasts, and a priest poured it into silver basins on the altar. Mishnah Sukkah 5:2-4 describes the lamp-lighting. Golden candelabra stood in the Women's Court, "the light from the candelabra was so bright that there was not a courtyard in Jerusalem that was not illuminated". Pious men danced with torches, and Levites stood on the fifteen steps (matching the Songs of Ascents). Sukkah 5:1: "One who did not see the Celebration of the Place of the Drawing of the Water never saw celebration in his days." (Sefaria, William Davidson Edition English, CC-BY-NC, https://www.sefaria.org/Mishnah_Sukkah.5.1, raw file john7_sukkah5.txt / john7_sukkah49.txt.) Augustine (Tr. 28.9) reads the feast typologically: the church is now "in the wilderness in tabernacles", a pilgrim people.

**The brothers.** Their names are given in Matt 13:55 / Mark 6:3: James, Joseph (Joses), Simon, Judas. They are unbelieving here and in Mark 3:21, 31. After the resurrection they are believers: 1 Cor 15:7 (the risen Lord "appeared to James"), Acts 1:14 (with Mary "and his brothers" in prayer), Gal 1:19, 1 Cor 9:5. James led the Jerusalem church and wrote the epistle. Jude wrote the epistle that calls himself "brother of James." Chrysostom marvels at this: "one became first Bishop of Jerusalem, the blessed James."
- *Perpetual virginity debate (mention briefly only).* Augustine (Tr. 28.3) takes the Jerome line: "brethren" means "blood relations of the Virgin Mary", that is, cousins. Calvin also says "brethren" covers "all cousins and other relations" (raw john7_calvin.txt). Cyril (following the Epiphanian view) calls them "reputed brethren", sons of Joseph. Helvidius (c. 380) argued for uterine brothers, and Jerome answered him (Against Helvidius). The modern Protestant reading, which Sproul and MacArthur both hold, takes them as younger children of Joseph and Mary (Matt 1:25, "until"). The Cambridge Bible notes that 7:5 is "fatal to the common theory, that these 'brethren' are really our Lord's cousins, the sons of Alphæus", because at least one son of Alphaeus was already an apostle. **Suggested on-air handling:** one sentence. These are Jesus' own family, most naturally his younger half-brothers, and the point John makes is that nearness without faith saves no one.

**"My time" (kairos) vs. "my hour" (hōra).** In 7:6 and 7:8 Jesus uses *ho kairos ho emos*, "my time/season". Vincent: "The appropriate season or juncture." Elsewhere John uses *hōra*, "hour", for the appointed hour of the cross and glory (2:4; 7:30; 8:20; 12:23; 13:1; 17:1). Bengel distinguishes the two verses: 7:6 "refers to His time for going up to the feast; but in this verse [7:8], as it seems, it refers to His time of suffering". Poole and Calvin both take 7:6 narrowly as the time of the journey ("There are some who erroneously interpret this as referring to the time of his death", Calvin). Augustine and Chrysostom read it more broadly: the time of glory (Augustine) or the cross (Chrysostom). In 7:8 the verb is *peplērōtai* (has not been fulfilled, "fully come"), which carries the sense of a divinely appointed schedule.

**Muttering (gongysmos, 7:12).** This is the same word group as Israel's wilderness grumbling (LXX Exod 16; Num 14), and John uses it in 6:41, 43, 61 ("the Jews grumbled about him"). Here, though, it is less complaint than buzz. Expositor's Greek Testament: "not 'murmuring,' as R.V, but rather 'whispering,' suppressed discussion in low tones, in corners, and among friends". Bengel: "Their speech not venturing to break out into open expression on either side". "Leading the people astray" (*planā ton ochlon*) is the charge of deceiver, the same one made later in Matt 27:63 ("that impostor") and in Deut 13 (the false prophet who leads astray). In the Talmud (b. Sanh. 43a) the tradition about Jesus' execution charges him with "leading Israel astray." Mention that only with care.

**"The Jews" (7:1, 11, 13).** Here this means the Jerusalem authorities. The crowds in 7:13 are themselves Jewish and fear "the Jews". Calvin: "By the Jews he here means the rulers, who had the government in their hands." Worth a short clarifying line on air.

## 2. Outline with key verses

1. **The hostile setting (7:1-2).** Jesus walks in Galilee because Judea seeks his life. Booths is near.
2. **The brothers' counsel (7:3-5).** "Leave here and go to Judea... show yourself to the world." John's aside: "For not even his brothers believed in him" (7:5). This is unbelief that looks like support: they treat him as a celebrity to be managed (see Sproul and Crossway below).
3. **Jesus' reply: two calendars and two relations to the world (7:6-8).**
   - "My time has not yet come, but your time is always here" (7:6). He lives by the Father's schedule. They have no appointed hour.
   - "The world cannot hate you, but it hates me because I testify about it that its works are evil" (7:7). The world hates him for his witness against it.
   - "You go up to the feast. I am not going up to this feast, for my time has not yet fully come" (7:8).
4. **He stays, then goes privately (7:9-10).** "Not publicly but in private." He is not deceiving them. He refuses their kind of going up: the pilgrim caravan and the public bid for acclaim.
5. **The feast-crowd in suspense (7:11-13).** "Where is he?" Muttering: "He is a good man" / "No, he is leading the people astray." "Yet for fear of the Jews no one spoke openly of him" (7:13). Verse 14 follows: in the middle of the feast he goes up and teaches openly.

**Big idea for the episode:** Jesus will not be hurried by unbelieving family, flattered by the world, or frightened by its hatred. He keeps the Father's time. The question is what we will say about him "openly".

## 3. Interpretive questions

### Q1. Did Jesus lie or change his mind in 7:8 ("I am not going up to this feast") and then go up (7:10)?

**The textual variant.** Many manuscripts read *oupō*, "not yet" (KJV "I go not up yet"; P66, P75, B and the majority). Others read *ouk*, "not" (Sinaiticus, D, the Vulgate; adopted by ESV, NA28). Ellicott: "The 'yet' is of doubtful authority, though it is found in some early MSS. and versions, and the more so because it removes an apparent difficulty." Cambridge: "'Yet,' though very ancient, is possibly no part of the original text: it may have been inserted to avoid the charge of the heathen critic Porphyry, that Jesus here shews fickleness or deceit, and therefore cannot be Divine." Gill: the omission "occasioned Porphyry, that great enemy of Christianity, to reproach Christ, as guilty of inconstancy, or of an untruth, since he afterwards did go up" (raw john7_bh8.txt).

**Porphyry and Jerome.** Porphyry (3rd c., *Against the Christians*) used the verse to charge Jesus with *inconstantia*, fickleness. Jerome reports and answers the charge in *Dialogue against the Pelagians* 2.17. The NPNF synopsis summarizes that section as "vii. 10. Was irresolute about going up to the Feast of Tabernacles." (raw john7_jerome_pel2.txt, newadvent.org/fathers/30112.htm). The English NPNF text of 2.17 is abridged, so **I could not download Jerome's actual wording.** The Latin is commonly cited as *"Iturum se negavit, et fecit quod prius negaverat. Latrat Porphyrius, inconstantiae ac mutationis accusat..."*, but **this is unverified; do not quote it on air** unless it is checked against PL 23. Expositor's Greek Testament confirms the term: "not the 'inconstantia' alleged by Porphyry". Jerome's line in context (Pelagian debate) is that Christ's human nature shared our creaturely limitations. That approach is less helpful for a Reformed audience than the solutions below.

**How the voices answer:**
- **Chrysostom (the "not now / not with you" view):** "He said not, once for all, I go not up, but, now, that is, not with you." He also says Jesus went up "not... that He might suffer, but that He might instruct them."
- **Augustine (the "not to *this* feast as you mean it" view):** "Go up to this feast. What means to this? Where ye seek human glory." Jesus did not go up on the first days they wanted. He went "on the middle of the feast", and he went to teach, not for "temporal glory".
- **Cyril (an explicit denial of lying):** Jesus goes up "not saying one thing and doing the contrary to what He says (for that would be lying, albeit guile, that is, falsehood is said not to have been found at all in His Mouth) but minded to what He promised." Cyril sets the secret going-up (to rebuke and teach) against the open, festal Palm Sunday entry.
- **Bengel / Ellicott (present tense):** "Ἀναβαίνω, I go up, is to be taken strictly in the present." Ellicott: "The verb is in the present, and its meaning does not exclude a going up afterwards." Ellicott also notes that the real ESV-type reading says only "when his brothers had gone up to the feast, then he also went up", not that he went "to the feast" as a pilgrim.
- **Cambridge:** "He means 'I am not going now; not going publicly in the general caravan of pilgrims; not going with you, who do not believe on Me.' He does not say 'I shall not go.'"
- **Poole:** "I have some particular reasons why as yet I will not go to be there at the beginning of it."
- **Hamilton (Crossway / ESV Expository Commentary):** the brothers urge a triumphal-entry style public arrival. "Jesus does not mean he is not going at all." The time for *that* kind of going is John 12.
- **MacArthur:** "No one forces Jesus' hand." He went later, not in the caravan, probably via Samaria (Luke 9:51-56).

**Take-away for the episode:** Under either reading, Jesus refuses the brothers' *kind* of going up, which is public, with the caravan, and seeking acclaim. He goes later, privately, to teach, and "his time" for a public going up (to die at Passover) is still ahead. No deception: his words are about what they meant, and he did not do what they meant.

### Q2. Why did his own brothers not believe (7:5)?
- **Chrysostom:** "From their evil mind, and from envy; for superiority among kindred is wont somehow to be envied by such as are not alike exalted." He reads their counsel as a charge of "cowardice and vainglory", yet marvels that James and Jude later believed.
- **Augustine:** "Why did they not believe in Him? Because they sought human glory." "The flesh spoke to the flesh; but the flesh without God, to the flesh with God."
- **Cyril:** they did not recognize "God the Word indwelling in His Holy Flesh"; they "persuade Him to seek after vain glory."
- **Poole and Ryle (Reformed: sovereign grace):** Poole: "Certainly there cannot be a greater proof and demonstration that faith is not of ourselves, nor a thing in our own power, no, not with all the external aids of gospel doctrine and arguments, than is in this text." Ryle: "That great Scriptural doctrine, man's need of preventing and converting grace, stands out here, as if written with a sunbeam." Compare 6:44, 65. MacArthur: "At this point, the Father had not drawn them."
- **Calvin and Gill (flesh relation counts for nothing):** Calvin: "Hence we infer how small is the value of carnal relationship". Gill: "it matters not, if men have known Christ, or have been allied to him after the flesh, unless they are new creatures in him".
- **Sproul and Hamilton (the false-faith angle):** the brothers were not exactly mockers. They backed a Messiah of their own design, a political deliverer, so their "trust... is a false trust" (Sproul). Hamilton reads the tone as misguided encouragement rather than derision. Barnes and Calvin take it as derision ("they mocked at Christ", Calvin). Both readings are possible, and a combination works on air: they half-believed in a Jesus he refused to be.
- **Pastoral comfort (Ryle):** believers who stand alone in their families. "Our blessed Master has truly learned by experience how to sympathize with all his people who stand alone." And the brothers' later conversion (1 Cor 15:7; Acts 1:14) gives hope for unbelieving relatives.

### Q3. Why does the world hate Jesus but not his brothers (7:7)?
- **Jesus' own answer:** "because I testify about it that its works are evil."
- **Ryle:** the offence was his moral witness more than his doctrine. "In short, they could have tolerated His opinions if He would only have spared their sins."
- **Chrysostom:** "how should it hate those who desire, and who run for the same objects as itself?" It was "the open rebuke, not the violation of the Sabbath, which caused the hatred."
- **Cyril:** like loves like. "Ever dear therefore (saith He) to each is that which is akin to it, and identity of habit wondrously bringeth together unto agreement."
- **Calvin:** "peace with the world can only be purchased by a wicked consent to vices and to every kind of wickedness." On 7:9: "accursed be that peace which costs so high a price as to leave and abandon the Son of God."
- **Bengel:** "Those who please all men at all times, ought deservedly to look on themselves with suspicion."
- **Pulpit Commentary:** "The world hates its censor; it repels the judgment passed upon it."
- Connect to John 3:19-20 (men loved darkness), 15:18-19 ("If the world hates you, know that it has hated me before it hated you"), and 17:14.

*(Possible bonus Q, 7:13: who kept silent? Augustine says the friends did: "they who said, He is a good man". Pulpit and Cambridge say both sides were cowed. Chrysostom: the people were "sound indeed in judgment, but not having that proper courage". Note "He is a good man" as an inadequate verdict, because a mere good man would not make his claims. MacArthur: "Good men, not enough.")*

## 1. Setting, background, and place in the letter's argument

**Author, place, date.** Peter writes "from Babylon" (5:13), almost certainly a code name for Rome, with Silvanus (5:12) and Mark. The usual date is about AD 62-64, just before or at the start of Nero's persecution. The Great Fire of Rome broke out on 19 July 64. MacArthur's sermon on this passage opens with it ("On July 19 in the year 64 AD, Rome burned while Nero fiddled..." — pet4_gty44). Tacitus (*Annals* 15.44) tells how Nero blamed the fire on "a class hated for their abominations, called Christians by the populace." Those arrested were "convicted, not so much of the crime of firing the city, as of hatred against mankind," and some "were doomed to the flames and burnt, to serve as a nightly illumination." If the letter was written after the fire, the word *pyrosis* ("burning") carries a heavy irony. Most commentators still read it as metallurgical imagery (refining fire, as in 1:7; Prov 27:21; Zech 13:9 LXX). They do not take it as a literal reference to Nero's torches.

**Recipients.** The "elect exiles of the dispersion" in five provinces of Asia Minor: Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia, Bithynia (1:1). About fifty years later (c. AD 111-112) Pliny the Younger, governor of Bithynia-Pontus, wrote to Trajan (*Ep.* 10.96). He was unsure "whether the name itself, even without offenses, or only the offenses associated with the name are to be punished." He executed people who kept confessing "Christian" when asked a third time. This is the best-documented case of "suffering *as a Christian*" (4:16), and it happened in the same region Peter addressed. It is later than the letter, so treat it as an illustration of where things were heading, not as the original setting.

**Place in the argument.**
- The letter's "suffering" theme builds in stages. First, 1:6-7: "various trials" test faith as fire tests gold. Then 2:18-25: unjust suffering, with Christ as the example. Then 3:13-17: "even if you should suffer for righteousness' sake, you will be blessed." Then 3:18-4:6: Christ's suffering and victory. Then 4:7-11: "the end of all things is at hand," so love one another and serve.
- 4:12 starts the last major movement with "Beloved" (as at 2:11). The Expositor's Greek Testament: "ἀγαπητοί marks the beginning of the third division of the Epistle in which Peter having cleared the ground faces at last the pressing problem." Some scholars think the earlier sections speak of suffering as a possibility ("if," 3:14, 17) and 4:12ff as a present reality. Pulpit Commentary: "The present participles imply that the persecution was already beginning." Others say the change is one of tone, not situation.
- **Verbal link to 4:4:** *xenizontai*, "they are surprised" that you no longer join their excesses. In 4:12, *mē xenizesthe*, "do not be surprised" at the fiery trial. The pagans think the Christians strange; the Christians must not think their suffering strange.
- The section prepares for ch. 5: elders shepherd the flock under pressure (5:1-4), "humble yourselves... casting all your anxieties on him" (5:6-7), resist the devil, "the same kinds of suffering are being experienced by your brotherhood throughout the world" (5:9), and "after you have suffered a little while" God will restore you (5:10).

**Key words and cross-references.**
- *pyrōsis* (v. 12): "burning." It appears elsewhere in the NT only in Rev 18:9, 18 (Babylon's burning). In the LXX: Prov 27:21 ("fire for gold"). It continues the refining image of 1:7.
- *xenizō* (v. 12; cf. 4:4): to be surprised, to treat as foreign or strange.
- *koinōneite tois tou Christou pathēmasin* (v. 13): "you share Christ's sufferings" (cf. Phil 3:10; Col 1:24; 2 Cor 1:5).
- "the Spirit of glory and of God rests upon you" (v. 14): echoes Isa 11:2 LXX, *anapausetai ep' auton pneuma tou theou*, "the Spirit of the LORD shall rest upon him." Gill notes this explicitly ("alluding to Isaiah 11:2"). MacArthur reads it as the Shekinah glory now resting on suffering believers. Some manuscripts add "on their part he is blasphemed, but on your part he is glorified" (KJV/TR). The ESV omits it.
- *allotriepiskopos* (v. 15): appears here first in all extant Greek literature. It is built from *allotrios* ("belonging to another") and *episkopos* ("overseer"). Proposed meanings: meddler or busybody; embezzler, "covetous of another's goods" (Calvin, Vulgate's sense); "a bishop in another man's diocese" (Gill's playful gloss); political agitator (MacArthur leans this way). Bengel and Meyer note that the extra *hōs* ("as") sets it apart from the three criminal terms.
- *Christianos* (v. 16): occurs only 3x in the NT (Acts 11:26; 26:28; 1 Pet 4:16). It was originally an outsiders' label, probably given in derision (Ellicott, Pulpit, Cambridge, MacArthur). Pliny and Tacitus show it as a legal and popular charge. Tertullian, *Apology* 3, protests that the name alone is condemned.
- "judgment to begin at the household of God" (v. 17): Ezek 9:6, "And begin at my sanctuary.' So they began with the elders who were before the house." See also Jer 25:29 ("I begin to work disaster at the city that is called by my name"), Mal 3:1-5 (the Lord suddenly comes to his temple as a refiner's fire to purify the sons of Levi), and Isa 10:12 (cited by Calvin and Poole). "Household/house of God" = the church as temple (1 Pet 2:5).
- v. 18: quotes Prov 11:31 LXX word for word: *ei ho men dikaios molis sōzetai, ho asebēs kai hamartōlos pou phaneitai*. The Hebrew (MT) reads differently: "If the righteous is repaid on earth, how much more the wicked and the sinner!" Ellicott, Cambridge and Expositor's all note this.
- *paratithesthōsan tas psychas* (v. 19): "let them entrust/deposit their souls." It is the same verb as Jesus' last word in Luke 23:46 ("Father, into your hands I commit my spirit," quoting Ps 31:5 LXX). Paul uses it for entrusting a deposit (1 Tim 1:18; 2 Tim 2:2). MacArthur calls it "a banking term." *ktistēs* ("Creator") occurs only here in the NT (Cambridge, Pulpit).

## 2. Outline with key verses

1. **Expect it: the fiery trial is not strange (v. 12).** "Beloved, do not be surprised..." Its purpose is testing (*pros peirasmon*), not destroying (cf. 1:6-7).
2. **Rejoice in it: fellowship with Christ now, glory later (v. 13).** "Rejoice insofar as you share Christ's sufferings, that you may also rejoice and be glad when his glory is revealed." Joy now, and greater joy at his appearing.
3. **Know you are blessed: the Spirit of glory rests on you (v. 14).** "If you are insulted for the name of Christ, you are blessed." The reason is the Isa 11:2 Spirit resting on the people of the Messiah.
4. **Examine it: suffer for Christ, not for crime (vv. 15-16).** Not as murderer, thief, evildoer, or meddler (*allotriepiskopos*). "Yet if anyone suffers as a Christian, let him not be ashamed, but let him glorify God in that name."
5. **Understand it: judgment begins at God's house (vv. 17-18).** The church's suffering is God's purifying judgment beginning with his own people (Ezek 9:6; Mal 3). The argument runs from lesser to greater: "what will be the outcome for those who do not obey the gospel of God?" "If the righteous is scarcely saved, what will become of the ungodly and the sinner?" (Prov 11:31 LXX).
6. **Entrust yourself: commit your soul to the faithful Creator while doing good (v. 19).** Suffering "according to God's will" is under his sovereign hand. The response is to deposit your life with him, as Jesus did at the cross (Luke 23:46), and keep doing good.

Possible sermon handle (as MacArthur frames it, pet4_gty45): expect it, rejoice in it, evaluate it, entrust yourself to God.

## 3. Interpretive questions and how the voices answer

### Q1. What is the "fiery trial": official, empire-wide persecution or local, social hostility?

- **Neronian / official reading.** MacArthur ties the letter directly to the fire of 64 and Nero's scapegoating of Christians. He grants that "who knows that some of the very Christians to whom Peter was writing were suffering under the terrible reign of terror of Nero." Benson takes the word literally as well: "including both martyrdom itself, which frequently was by fire."
- **Local, social hostility (the majority of modern exegetes).** Most commentators note that there is no evidence of an empire-wide legal ban before Domitian or Trajan. The language of 4:4 ("they malign you"), 2:12 ("speak against you as evildoers") and 4:14 ("insulted") points mainly to slander, social ostracism, and sporadic local action. Matthew Henry's full commentary even separates the two: "From the fiery trial the apostle descends to a lower degree of persecution—that of the tongue by slander and reproach." The Ligonier devotional (unsigned, 2005) leaves the question open and stresses purpose over intensity: "fiery trials" "refers not so much to the intensity of the Christian's sufferings... but to the fact that these sufferings purify faith just like fire purifies precious metals."
- **Calvin** distinguishes kinds of suffering rather than its legal source: "sometimes he speaks generally of troubles common to man's life; but here he speaks of wrongs done to the faithful for the name of Christ."
- **Gill** offers two options: the coming destruction of Jerusalem (since he takes the readers to be Jewish Christians), or general persecution "for the sake of Christ and his Gospel."
- **Expositor's Greek Testament** reads *en hymin* as "the ordeal which is in your midst or rather in your hearts," that is, "the test is internal—in what frame of mind will they meet it?"
- **Possible podcast angle.** Pliny (c. 112) shows the trajectory. What began as slander and suspicion in Peter's day became a capital charge for "the name itself" within two generations, in the same provinces.

### Q2. "It is time for judgment to begin at the household of God" (v. 17): what does it mean?

- **Purifying chastisement of the church, as the first stage of one eschatological judgment.** This is the dominant Reformed reading.
  - Calvin: the whole church is "especially and mainly to be chastised by the hand of God," and "it is a sweet consolation, that God does not execute his judgments on us as on others, but that he makes us the representatives of his own Son." Calvin doubts a single proof-text is meant. He hears "the common and constant teaching of Scripture," that God corrects his own household first "as the head of a family corrects his own children rather than those of strangers" (citing Isa 10:12).
  - Poole: "temporary, and for good, in opposition to the destructive judgment"; "the afflictions that befall God's people come in the time appointed, and so are never unseasonable."
  - Gill: "not punishment for sin, strictly speaking, because Christ has endured this in the room and stead of his church and people... but afflictions and persecutions, and which are fatherly chastisements." He adds rabbinic parallels: "it begins at the righteous."
  - MacArthur: "Peter's not speaking of condemnation when he uses judgment. He's speaking of chastening, of testing, of purifying, of purging." He links it to Ezek 9:6, "Begin at My sanctuary."
  - Bengel: "It is one and the same judgment from the time of the preaching of the Gospel by the apostles until the last judgment... Judgment begins from this with a mild beginning."
  - Pulpit and Cambridge cite 1 Cor 11:32: we are judged now so that we will not be condemned with the world.
- **Ezekiel 9 background.** Expositor's Greek Testament: the phrase "is a deduction from the vision of Ezekiel 9." There the slaughter begins at the sanctuary, but those marked with the *tav* are spared (Ezek 9:4, 6). For the church, the "mark" is the blood of Christ and the Spirit (cf. 4:14). Judgment that falls as wrath on apostate Jerusalem in Ezekiel becomes refining fire for the marked people in Peter (Mal 3:2-3 is the bridge: "he is like a refiner's fire").
- **Gill's alternative:** "the house of God" = the Jerusalem temple soon to be destroyed in AD 70. Few moderns follow this.
- **The "how much more" argument (17b-18).** Poole: "If he spare not his children, much less will he his enemies." Calvin: "for if God spares not his own children whom he loves and who obey him, how dreadful will be his severity against enemies and such as are rebellious!"

### Q3. "If the righteous is scarcely saved" (v. 18): does this undermine assurance?

- **Calvin (assurance-preserving, present-life reading).** "Scarcely" refers to the hardships of the road, not to doubt about the outcome at the final judgment: "it is the present and not the future time that Peter refers to." His image is a ship sailing among rocks that reaches port only through "[a] thousand deaths," yet "we are in no danger of shipwreck as long as we have him as our pilot." He calls interpreters who read it of a near-miss at the final judgment "absurd."
- **Leighton (via Ellicott's citation).** "Scarcely" "imports not... any uncertainty or hazard in the thing itself to the end, in respect of the purpose and performance of God, but only the great difficulties and hard encounters in the way." Matthew Henry's Concise Commentary paraphrases Leighton almost word for word.
- **Gill:** the righteous are "completely saved; Christ has wrought out and finished a complete salvation for them," and "scarcely" is "nor as if their salvation was doubtful; for though they are scarcely, yet certainly saved; for they are chosen to salvation" (pet4_bh18.txt).
- **Matthew Henry (full):** "Let the absolute necessity of salvation balance the difficulty of it."
- **Spurgeon (Sermon 3047, "If So—What Then?")**. The difficulty is not "because there is any deficiency in Jesus Christ... or any lack of efficacy in His atoning Sacrifice." It comes from "the strictness of Divine rule" (judgment beginning at God's house) and from the real conflicts of the Christian life. He draws a pastoral inference for anxious believers: the struggle is itself a mark of life ("A dead fish finds no difficulty in floating down the stream..."). He also warns presumptuous professors who find salvation "so very easy."
- **Dissent, useful as a foil.** Ellicott (Anglican) pushes back on Leighton: "This is only partly true. The Apostle is rather thinking of the final judgment than of the life of trial; and he means that there was but little margin left." A Reformed host can answer with Calvin and Bengel, who point to 2 Pet 1:11's "rich entrance." The difficulty is real; the outcome is secured by the faithful Creator of v. 19.
- **Text note.** Peter follows the LXX, which differs from the Hebrew of Prov 11:31. Calvin thought Peter was perhaps "repeat[ing] a common and a proverbial saying." Cambridge notes that the LXX omits "upon earth."

## Notes on quotations as delivered in the script
- Durham, Clavis Cantici on Song 7:1, from https://archive.org/download/claviscant00durh/claviscant00durh_djvu.txt.
