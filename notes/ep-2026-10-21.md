## 2. Setting and background

### Date
- 51:59 places the delivery of the Babylon oracles in Zedekiah's fourth year, 594/593 BC, about seven years before Jerusalem fell (587/586). So when Jeremiah wrote it, Babylon was at its height and Nebuchadnezzar had another 30-odd years to reign (died 562). This is the same prophet who told the exiles to "seek the welfare of the city" (29:7) and urged surrender to Babylon. Ellicott makes this point: "For the most part, he appears as the supporter of the Chaldæans, opposing the policy of the kings and rulers who were bent on resistance, bidding the exiles to pray for the peace of Babylon (Jeremiah 29:7)." (Ellicott dates the composition loosely, "probably as the closing vision of his life", but 51:59-60 shows it was written down and sent by 594/593.)
- Keil & Delitzsch on authenticity, quoting Graf: "The prophecy contains nothing which Jeremiah could not have written in the fourth year of Zedekiah". K&D argue the case against Jeremianic authorship rests on the rationalist axiom that prophets "could merely prophesy of things which lay within the political horizon."
- Babylon's oracle is the longest in the book (50:1-51:58, 110 verses) and sits last among the nations, matching 25:26 ("after them the king of Babylon shall drink"). Henry: Babylon "is put last of Jeremiah's prophecies against the Gentiles because it was last accomplished; and when the cup of God's fury went round (25:17) the king of Sheshach, Babylon, drank last."

### Babylon and the Chaldeans
- "Chaldeans" (Kasdim) were the dynasty and people of Nabopolassar and Nebuchadnezzar (Neo-Babylonian empire, 626-539). 50:1 pairs "Babylon" with "the land of the Chaldeans."
- After Assyria fell (Nineveh 612), Babylon was the capital of the Near East (Livius: "After the fall of the Assyrian empire (612 BCE), Babylon became the capital of the ancient Near East, and king Nebuchadnezzar adorned the city with several famous buildings."). https://www.livius.org/articles/place/babylon/
- 50:17 is a two-empire summary: Assyria "devoured" the northern kingdom (722), Nebuchadnezzar "gnawed his bones" (597, 587).

### Bel and Merodach (50:2)
- Merodach = Marduk, chief god of Babylon, patron of the city; "Bel" ("lord," cognate of Baal) was his title. Ellicott: "Strictly speaking these, as found in the inscriptions, were names of the same deity (see Note on Isaiah 46:1)." He notes Nebuchadnezzar styled himself "worshipper of Marduk" and named his son Evil-merodach (Jer 52:31). Compare Isa 46:1 "Bel bows down; Nebo stoops."
- Biblical names built on Marduk: Merodach-baladan (Isa 39:1), Evil-merodach (2 Kgs 25:27; Jer 52:31).
- 50:38 "it is a land of images, and they are mad over idols" (the word for idols here, 'emim, means "terrors"; Henry and K&D both note this).
- Henry: "Bel and Merodach were their two principal deities; they shall be confounded, and the images of them broken to pieces."

### The nation from the north: Medes and Persians, Cyrus, 539 BC
- 50:3, 9, 41 "out of the north", "a gathering of great nations, from the north country", "a mighty nation and many kings". 51:11, 28 name "the kings of the Medes." Persia proper lay east/southeast, but Media lay north, and the 539 campaign came down the Tigris from the north (Opis, then Sippar). The Ligonier devotional (below) makes the same point: Persia "used a northern approach."
- Irony: "out of the north" was the phrase for Babylon's own coming against Judah (1:14; 4:6; 6:22). Ellicott: "It is significant that the very phrase which had described the danger that threatened Judah from Babylon... is now used for the danger that threatened Babylon itself from Media." And 50:41-43 literally recycles 6:22-24 with "daughter of Babylon" put in place of "daughter of Zion."
- **Nabonidus Chronicle (ABC 7)**, Livius translation, https://www.livius.org/sources/content/mesopotamian-chronicles-content/abc-7-nabonidus-chronicle/ (fetched). Col. iii 12-18 (line numbers omitted below): Cyrus fought at Opis on the Tigris, "He carried off the plunder (and) slaughtered the people. On the fourteenth day Sippar was captured without a battle. Nabonidus fled. On the sixteenth day, Ugbaru, governor of Gutium, and the army of Cyrus, without battle they entered Babylon." Then "On the third day of the month Arahsamna, Cyrus entered Babylon." And "There was peace in the city." (October 539 BC.) Also notes no interruption of rites in Esagil (Marduk's temple).
- **Cyrus Cylinder**, Livius translation, https://www.livius.org/sources/content/cyrus-cylinder/cyrus-cylinder-translation/ (fetched). Line 17: Marduk "made him enter his city Babylon without fighting or battle; he saved Babylon from hardship. He delivered Nabonidus, the king who did not revere him, into his hands." Line 22: Cyrus calls himself one "whose rule Bêl and Nabu love." Line 32 (translator's note "i.e., in Babylon" omitted): "I returned the images of the gods, who had resided there, to their places and I let them dwell in eternal abodes. I gathered all their inhabitants and returned to them their dwellings."
  - **Preaching point**: Cyrus's own propaganda credits Marduk ("Bel") with handing him Babylon. Jeremiah says Bel is put to shame and Merodach dismayed (50:2). Same event, two theologies of who did it. The Cylinder's policy of sending people and gods home is the setting of Ezra 1:1-4.
- **Herodotus** (via Keil & Delitzsch on 50:24): "According to Herodotus (i. 191), Cyrus took Babylon by diverting the Euphrates into a trench he had dug. By this stratagem the Persians threw themselves so unexpectedly on the Babylonians... that when the outmost portions of the city had been already seized, those who lived in the middle had not observed at all that they were captured." K&D say 50:24 ("you did not know it") "has been literally fulfilled." Compare 50:38 "A drought against her waters"; Henry: "Cyrus drew the river Euphrates into so many channels as made it passable for his army." Daniel 5 (Belshazzar's feast) gives the same picture of a city surprised at night. Caution: the cuneiform sources say "without battle" and do not mention the river diversion; Herodotus wrote about a century later. Present Herodotus as the Greek tradition, not as cuneiform record.

### Merathaim and Pekod (50:21)
- ESV footnotes: Merathaim "means double rebellion"; Pekod "means punishment."
- Strong's/BDB (Bible Hub 4850): "Dual of mar feminine; double bitterness; Merathajim, an epithet of Babylon"; BDB: perhaps “double rebellion,” another name for Bab. https://biblehub.com/hebrew/4850.htm
- Strong's 6489 Pekod: "From paqad; punishment; Pekod, a symbolic name for Bab."; BDB: "a people in S.E. Bab." https://biblehub.com/hebrew/6489.htm
- Ellicott's explanation: Merathaim is probably a play on Aram-Naharaim ("land of two rivers") turned into "land of two rebellions": "It was, he seems to say, the country, not of rivers, but of rebellions". Pekod was a real Babylonian people (Ezek 23:23; Ellicott: in inscriptions, "in the form Bukudu"), chosen “for the sake of its meaning, “visitation.”” (Ellicott's wording) So real place-names bent into verdicts: Babylon is "Double Rebellion" and "Punishment/Visitation."
- Calvin renders "the land of the exasperating" and "inhabitants of visitation."
- Also note herem language ("devote them to destruction," 50:21, 26), the Joshua-era ban now applied to Babylon (Ellicott: "connected with the Hebrew Cherem").

### The cup of Jeremiah 25 and "Sheshach"
- 25:15-29: Jeremiah takes "this cup of the wine of wrath" and makes the nations drink; the list ends "And after them the king of Babylon [Sheshach] shall drink" (25:26).
- 51:7: Babylon herself had been "a golden cup in the Lord's hand." The cup that served wrath to others is now made to drink.
- Ellicott: before 50-51, Jeremiah had only hinted at this, "veiled beneath the cypher of the mysterious Sheshach (Jeremiah 25:26)."
- Rev 18:6 picks this up: "mix a double portion for her in the cup she mixed."

### Did 539 match the violence of chapter 50? The critical question
- **The problem**: 50:3, 13, 39-40 describe total desolation, uninhabited "for all generations," like Sodom. But in 539 Cyrus took Babylon "without battle" (Nabonidus Chronicle) and kept it as a royal capital. Babylon stayed a great city for centuries.
- **The later history** (Livius, https://www.livius.org/articles/place/babylon/): revolts under Darius I (Nidintu-Bel, Arakha) and Xerxes (484 BC). Livius warns that Greek reports of Xerxes punishing Babylon "are often misinterpreted... the cult in the Esagila continued," though "several Babylonian archives end in 484, and it is possible that when Xerxes captured Babylon, the city was looted." Alexander took the city in 331 and planned to make it his residence; he died there (323). Seleucus I founded Seleucia nearby and moved Europeans out of Babylon; the Parthians conquered Babylonia in 141 BC; "it appears that the city's decline had begun. When the Roman emperor Trajan invaded Babylonia in 116-117, he was disappointed by the ruins." Cuneiform writing continued to the late 2nd century AD.
- So the desolation came gradually, over about 600 years, through decline and depopulation more than one violent sack.
- **How conservative commentators handle it**:
  1. **Calvin**: openly says it was "not immediately fulfilled," lists Cyrus, the revolt, Alexander, and Augustus, and argues that the prophets extend each judgment "to the last overthrow"; each historical blow is a "prelude" of final destruction (see Quote Bank, 50:39-40).
  2. **Poole**: "The fulfilling of this we have not in holy writ, only the beginning of its accomplishment"; traces Cyrus (tribute), Darius Hystaspes (walls pulled down), Seleucus (city drained), Hadrian's time (only "some pieces of walls").
  3. **Keil & Delitzsch**: the prophet sees the steps "taken together into one view" though they "will actually follow successively in long periods" (said of 50:4-5, but it is their general principle); and they argue 50:24 (surprise capture) was literally fulfilled.
  4. **Henry and Gill**: read the oracle as also typological/eschatological, aimed at "New-Testament Babylon" (Henry) or "mystical Babylon" (Gill) in Revelation 17-18.
  5. A fair summary for the episode: the 539 fall was the decisive turn that ended Babylon's empire and freed the exiles (the main point of 50:33-34). Babylon's total emptiness came slowly but did come. The prophets compress near and far events into one picture (prophetic foreshortening), and the New Testament takes up the far end in Revelation 18.
- **Archaeology** (keep it light, sourced): the city's ruins were excavated by Robert Koldewey (1899-1917); the Ishtar Gate is reconstructed in Berlin's Pergamon Museum (Livius gallery shows the Ishtar Gate and Procession Street lions). The Cyrus Cylinder is in the British Museum (BM 90920; museum page blocked to fetch, number from memory, so do not cite on air without checking). Not fetched/verified: details of Saddam Hussein's reconstruction and Babylon's 2019 UNESCO listing; leave them out or check first.

### Seraiah son of Neriah
- 51:59: Seraiah, son of Neriah son of Mahseiah, "the quartermaster" (Hebrew sar menuchah, Strong's H8269 + H4496, confirmed on Bible Hub interlinear 51:59; roughly "officer of the resting place", i.e. of the camp stops; note the rest word menuchah, which fits the rest thread). He went with Zedekiah to Babylon in 594/593. Baruch is also "son of Neriah son of Mahseiah" (32:12), so the two are brothers.

## 3. Key Hebrew words (Bible Hub Strong's pages, fetched)

Interlinear checks: https://biblehub.com/interlinear/jeremiah/50-34.htm (and 50-5, 50-6, 50-7, 50-20, 50-21, 50-33, 50-44; 47-6; Isa 14-7; Exod 9-2).

- **go'el, "Redeemer" (50:34), from ga'al, Strong's H1350**. https://biblehub.com/hebrew/1350.htm
  - Strong's: "A primitive root, to redeem (according to the Oriental law of kinship), i.e. To be the next of kin (and as such to buy back a relative's property, marry his widow, etc.)". BDB short definition: "to redeem, act as kinsman". ## 4. Interpretive questions

### Q1. If God used Babylon as his instrument, how can he punish it? (50:7, 11, 14, 24, 29; compare Isaiah 10)
- The text gives Babylon's defense in 50:7: "We are not guilty, for they have sinned against the Lord." Jeremiah himself had called Nebuchadnezzar "my servant" (25:9; 27:6), and Nebuzaradan says the same thing at 40:2-3.
- The answer has three parts in the text:
  1. **Motive**: 50:11 "you rejoice... O plunderers of my heritage." They acted for plunder and pleasure, not justice. Isaiah 10:7 says the same about Assyria: "he does not so intend... it is in his heart to destroy"; 10:15 "Shall the axe boast over him who hews with it?" 50:18 makes the Assyria comparison explicit.
  2. **Excess**: 50:17 "gnawed his bones"; 50:33 "refuse to let them go"; Zech 1:15 ("while I was angry but a little, they furthered the disaster").
  3. **Pride against God himself**: 50:24 "you opposed the Lord"; 50:29 "she has proudly defied the Lord, the Holy One of Israel"; 50:31-32 "O proud one." Attacking God's people and temple (50:28 "vengeance for his temple") is fighting God.
- Voices: Calvin (the Chaldeans were God's "scourges" but "it was not their purpose to punish the Jews as they deserved"); Henry ("God does not afflict his people willingly, and therefore takes it very ill if the instruments he employs afflict them willingly"); Ligonier devotional (Babylon "was the servant of the Creator purely in a formal sense"). Reformed frame: Westminster Confession 5.4 (God's providence reaches even sinful acts, "yet so, as the sinfulness thereof proceedeth only from the creature, and not from God, who, being most holy and righteous, neither is nor can be the author or approver of sin"; fetched from https://www.opc.org/wcf.html).
- Gill adds a pastoral note: "though they sinned against the Lord, they had not sinned against them." Trapp: "God will have it so; {#Jer 40:2} but this was no good plea."

### Q2. "Iniquity... sought, and there shall be none" (50:20): how can this be?
- It does not mean the remnant became sinless. Gill: "not that they will be wholly free from sin... but that they will not be found upon them, so as to be charged on them in a judicial way". The ground is in the verse itself: "for I will pardon (salach) those whom I leave as a remnant."
- Readings:
  - **Forensic/forgiveness** (Calvin, Gill, Henry, Spurgeon, Watson): sin removed from the record; search and find nothing. Links to 31:34 ("I will remember their sin no more") and 33:8. Ellicott and the Pulpit Commentary both call it a New Covenant/"evangelical" promise.
  - **Reformational** (Poole, Henry, Calvin's editor): after the exile Israel never returned to idolatry, so "if any seek for idols... there shall be none" (Henry). Poole distinguishes temporal punishment remitted to the nation from eternal pardon given to the truly penitent.
  - Both can stand: the promised pardon produced a purged people. The first point is the main one.
- Note the remnant word (sha'ar) and the pairing "Israel... Judah" (both kingdoms, as in 50:4 and 50:33).
- Calvin's pastoral angle: unbelievers want relief from pain; believers want reconciliation. Sin is the disease and exile the symptom.

### Q3. When and how literally fulfilled? (539 BC vs. later; Revelation 17-18)
- See section 2 above. The options: (a) the near fulfillment in 539 is the main point, and the desolation language is conventional curse imagery that came true over time; (b) prophetic foreshortening, near and far seen together (K&D, Calvin); (c) typological, pointing to final judgment on the world-system "Babylon" (Henry, Gill, Revelation 18).
- Calvin's sentence is the best one to use on air: the prophets "ever extend what they say to the last overthrow."
- Be fair to the 539 data: the Nabonidus Chronicle and Cyrus Cylinder show a takeover without battle. But 50:24 (surprise, "you did not know it") and 50:2 (the gods humiliated: Babylon's own god was claimed by the conqueror) fit well. The empire was gone overnight even though the city was not.

### Q4 (optional). Israel and Judah together, weeping (50:4-5)
- The north had been in exile since 722; Jeremiah repeatedly promises both houses (3:18; 31:31). Henry thinks some of the ten tribes joined the returnees (Ezra 6:17 offers twelve goats "according to the number of the tribes of Israel"). Gill: the literal return under Cyrus did not include Israel in any great number, so he pushes it to a future conversion. Calvin: "The Prophet includes here all the tribes" and ultimately reads it of "Christ's spiritual kingdom."
- "Weeping as they come": tears of repentance (Henry: "godly sorrow"; Poole: sorrow for sin or joy at mercy; Trapp: "Tears of sorrow for their sins, and tears of joy for their deliverance by Cyrus, but especially by Christ").
- Spurgeon builds a whole sermon on this: mourners, inquirers, covenanters. Old feuds dissolve: Judah and Israel come "together." Trapp: "misery bred unity, as it did also between Hooper and Ridley, when they were both in prison for the truth."
- Covenant renewal under Ezra/Nehemiah (Neh 9:38; 10:29), mentioned by Calvin's editor and by Poole.

## 6. Application ideas and threads

### Application
1. **The world's hammers are tools in God's hand, and he breaks them when the work is done (50:23).** Henry: "The God of the whole earth will break the hammer of the whole earth." Comfort for a church that feels pressed by powers greater than itself. Ligonier's line that the success of the wicked "is only temporary" applies here.
2. **Being God's instrument is no excuse for cruelty (50:7, 11).** Parents, pastors, magistrates, employers: God may use your discipline, but he weighs the motive. "We are not guilty, for they have sinned" is the oppressor's excuse. Henry: God "takes it very ill if the instruments he employs afflict them willingly."
3. **A strong Redeemer, next of kin, who pleads the case (50:33-34).** Captors "held them fast" but "their Redeemer is strong" (same root). Christ, our near kinsman by the incarnation (Heb 2:11-17), is both redeemer and advocate (1 John 2:1). MacLaren's sermon is the resource here. Use the NT sparingly; Hebrews 2 is warranted by the go'el/kinship idea.
4. **Sought and not found (50:20).** Assurance for the troubled conscience: Spurgeon's searchers (conscience, unbelief, the world, Satan, God himself) and Watson's cancelled debt book. Calvin's corrective: don't just want the symptoms gone; want God reconciled.
5. **Mourners, inquirers, covenanters (50:4-5).** Returning to God involves tears, asking the way, and binding yourself to him. "Faces turned toward it" (Henry, Trapp). Old enmities (Israel/Judah) dissolve in shared repentance (Spurgeon, Trapp's Hooper and Ridley).
6. **Forgotten fold, forgetful shepherds (50:6).** Pastors who lead astray will answer for it (Calvin: this does not excuse the sheep). Christ is the resting place (Gill, Spurgeon, Owen). A fit word for a PCA audience on the duty of shepherds.
7. **Come out of Babylon (50:8).** Edwards: Abraham was called out of "the land of graven images"; Israel is called out again; the church is called out in Rev 18:4. Be first out, like the male goats before the flock (Henry: "It is a graceful thing to be forward in a good work").

# Research: Song of Songs 4:1-7, "You Are Altogether Beautiful"

Podcast position: read it plainly first, as a husband's praise of his bride inside marriage. Then move to Christ and the church through Ephesians 5, with restraint, and don't press the allegorical details. (Calvin wrote no commentary on the Song of Songs, so nothing here is attributed to him.)

All quotes below were copied from pages fetched during this research session, not from memory. Quote aggregators were not used.

## 1. Text and Structure

**ESV, Song of Songs 4:1-7**, from https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Song+of+Songs+4%3A1-7&version=ESV

> Behold, you are beautiful, my love, / behold, you are beautiful! / Your eyes are doves / behind your veil. / Your hair is like a flock of goats / leaping down the slopes of Gilead. / Your teeth are like a flock of shorn ewes / that have come up from the washing, / all of which bear twins, / and not one among them has lost its young. / Your lips are like a scarlet thread, / and your mouth is lovely. / Your cheeks are like halves of a pomegranate / behind your veil. / Your neck is like the tower of David, / built in rows of stone; / on it hang a thousand shields, / all of them shields of warriors. / Your two breasts are like two fawns, / twins of a gazelle, / that graze among the lilies. / Until the day breathes / and the shadows flee, / I will go away to the mountain of myrrh / and the hill of frankincense. / You are altogether beautiful, my love; / there is no flaw in you.

ESV footnote on 4:4 ("rows of stone"): "The meaning of the Hebrew word is uncertain."

### Genre: wasf (descriptive praise song)
- A *wasf* (Arabic for "description") is a song that praises the beloved's body part by part. The Song has others: the woman describes the man in 5:10-16, and the man describes her again in 6:4-7 and 7:1-9.
- The Cambridge Bible (Harper) credits Wetzstein's 19th-century study of Syrian wedding customs with recognizing the form: "The whole song is evidently modelled, as several of the succeeding songs are, on the wasf or description of the bride, which is so prominent a thing at marriage festivals in Syria to this day." (https://biblehub.com/commentaries/cambridge/songs/4.htm)
- Setting: the passage follows 3:6-11, Solomon's wedding procession, "on the day of his wedding" (3:11). The Pulpit Commentary places 4:1ff. "in the bridal procession". In 4:8 he calls her "bride" for the first time, and 4:16-5:1 is the consummation. So this is a husband's praise on the wedding day.

### Inclusio
- 4:1 "Behold, you are beautiful, my love, behold, you are beautiful!"
- 4:7 "You are altogether beautiful, my love; there is no flaw in you."
- SIL Translator's Notes (https://tips.translation.bible/?p=68615): 4:7 "ends the inclusio, which began there." The Hebrew of 4:7a is literally "all of you [is] beautiful," and 7b strengthens 7a by stating it negatively ("no flaw").
- Durham and Spurgeon both say the closing summary exists so that nobody imagines the parts he left unmentioned were defective. Their quotes are in the Quote Bank.

### Seven images, from head down
1. Eyes: doves behind the veil (4:1)
2. Hair: flock of goats on Gilead (4:1)
3. Teeth: shorn, washed ewes, all with twins (4:2)
4. Lips and mouth: scarlet thread (4:3a)
5. Cheeks or temples: halves of a pomegranate behind the veil (4:3b)
6. Neck: tower of David hung with a thousand shields (4:4)
7. Breasts: twin fawns of a gazelle grazing among lilies (4:5)

Keil & Delitzsch count the same seven, following Hengstenberg, and speak of "the sevenfold beauty of the bride." The Pulpit Commentary says the same: "her perfections being sevenfold." Matthew Henry (Complete) calls seven "a number of perfection." Seven images followed by "altogether beautiful" works as a completeness formula, but **don't push the number symbolism**.

### 4:6 echoes 2:17
- In 2:17 she says "Until the day breathes and the shadows flee…" and asks him to come to her like a gazelle "on cleft mountains." Here the same phrase returns, together with "mountain of myrrh / hill of frankincense."
- Most read the phrase as evening ("until the day breathes" means until the cool evening breeze), not dawn. The KJV's "until the day break" leans toward dawn. Ellicott: "Until the day breathe = when evening is come."
- Ellicott (https://biblehub.com/commentaries/ellicott/songs/4.htm) takes the mountain and hill as the woman herself: "Commentators have tried to identify the mountain of myrrh and hill of frankincense, but these only carry on the thought of Song of Solomon 4:5 under another figure." Taken that way, 4:6 is the bridegroom's intention to be with his bride that night, and it leads into 4:16-5:1. Many modern commentators read it the same way.
- **Who speaks 4:6 is disputed.** Keil & Delitzsch and the Pulpit Commentary give it to the bride, who modestly withdraws from his praise. Durham, Henry, Gill, Ellicott and the ESV layout give it to the bridegroom. Durham reads it as Christ deliberately repeating her own prayer from 2:17 back to her as his answer.

### The images in their culture
- **Doves behind the veil.** Doves suggest gentleness, softness and quick shimmering movement. Delitzsch: her eyes are compared "as to their colour, motion, and lustre, to a pair of doves." The veil (*tsammah*) half-hides her face, so the eyes stand out. Brides were veiled (cf. Gen 24:65, Rebekah).
- **Goats on Gilead.** Gilead is the hilly grazing country east of the Jordan. Palestinian goats were mostly black, and sheep mostly white. Seen from a distance, a herd streaming down a slope looks like long dark hair falling in waves over the shoulders. The point is motion, sheen and abundance, not resemblance to animals.
- **Shorn ewes, all with twins.** The washed sheep are bright white. "All bear twins, none bereaved" means a full set of teeth, matched upper to lower, with none missing, which was no small thing in the ancient world. There is wordplay in Hebrew: *shekkullam* ("all of them") and *shakkulah* ("bereaved one") (Cambridge).
- **Scarlet thread.** Thin red lips. *Shani* is the crimson dye made from the kermes insect (cf. Rahab's scarlet cord, Josh 2:18, though do not allegorize that connection).
- **Halves of a pomegranate.** A cut pomegranate shows a reddish blush, so the image is rosy cheeks or temples glimpsed through the veil. The pomegranate is also a fertility and love fruit throughout the Song (4:13; 6:7, 11; 7:12; 8:2).
- **Tower of David with a thousand shields.** The neck is tall, straight and dignified, hung with layered necklaces the way a fortress tower was hung with soldiers' shields (cf. Ezek 27:11: "They hanged their shields upon thy walls…they have made thy beauty perfect"). The word *talpiyyot* is a hapax and uncertain. Proposed meanings include "armoury," "in courses/terraces," and "for trophies." Delitzsch notes that up to this point the images are a shepherdess's, and here they become a king's: "She who has eyes like doves is in form like a born queen." The image says dignity, strength, inaccessibility and honour, not shape.
- **Twin fawns among the lilies.** Matched, young, soft and lively, with an air of delicacy and fragrance. Lilies are the Song's flower of beauty and delight (2:1-2, 16; 6:2-3).
- **Mountain of myrrh, hill of frankincense.** Both are expensive imported aromatics. Delitzsch notes they were exotics in Palestine, imported from Arabia and India. In the Song they belong to the beloved's body and the bed (1:13, "a sachet of myrrh…between my breasts"; 3:6; 5:5, 13). Henry and Delitzsch both mention Mount Moriah and temple incense as an *echo*. Delitzsch explicitly says "Without wishing to allegorize." Hold that one loosely.

### The Hebrew word for "flaw" and Ephesians 5:27
- Hebrew: **מוּם *mum*** (Strong's H3971), "blemish, defect." https://biblehub.com/hebrew/3971.htm. Strong's glosses it "a blemish (physically or morally)," KJV "blemish, blot, spot."
- Its main use is **Levitical**. Priests with a *mum* could not approach the altar (Lev 21:17-23). Sacrificial animals had to be without *mum* (Lev 22:20-21; Deut 15:21; 17:1). It is also used of moral stain (Job 31:7; Deut 32:5) and physical perfection (2 Sam 14:25 of Absalom; Dan 1:4).
- **The LXX of Song 4:7** is ὅλη καλὴ εἶ, πλησίον μου, καὶ **μῶμος** οὐκ ἔστιν ἐν σοί (https://www.ellopos.net/elpenor/greek-texts/septuagint/chapter.asp?book=28&page=4). *Mōmos* ("blemish") is the root of *amōmos*.
- **Eph 5:27** (https://biblehub.com/text/ephesians/5-27.htm): ἵνα παραστήσῃ αὐτὸς ἑαυτῷ ἔνδοξον τὴν ἐκκλησίαν, μὴ ἔχουσαν **σπίλον** (*spilos*, G4696, "spot") ἢ ῥυτίδα… ἀλλ' ἵνα ᾖ ἁγία καὶ **ἄμωμος** (*amōmos*, G299, "without blemish"). The verbal link is real at the level of the Greek Bible: Song 4:7 LXX "no *mōmos*," and Eph 5:27 "*a-mōmos*." *Amōmos* is also the LXX word for unblemished sacrifices, so the word joins Song 4:7, the Levitical "no blemish" and Ephesians 5. **Caution:** Paul does not quote the Song. This is a shared vocabulary of "unblemished," not a citation.
- **Commentators who make the link** (all verified):
  - **John Gill** on 4:7 cites Eph 5:27 directly.
  - **Matthew Henry** (Complete) on 4:7 cites Eph 5:27.
  - **James Durham** on 4:7 cites Eph 5:25ff. as Christ's "design."
  - **Pulpit Commentary** on 4:7 cites Eph 5:27, with the careful phrase "whether borrowed from this book or not."
  - **Gregory of Nyssa** ties "no flaw" to Christ's death ("the lamb…that takes away the sin of the cosmos") rather than to Ephesians 5.

## 2. Interpretive Questions

### Q1. Why such strange comparisons? Teeth like sheep, a neck like a tower?
- **The images are not pictures; they are evocative.** Draw them literally and you get a cartoon monster. Wasf images work by *association*. They transfer the feeling, value and impression of one thing (abundance, brightness, completeness, dignity, delicacy, preciousness) onto the beloved. Goats streaming down Gilead are not hair-shaped, but they move, shine and pour the way her hair does. A tower doesn't look like a neck, but it says *stately, strong, honoured, adorned*.
- The images are also **pastoral and royal**. They come from the world the couple shared (flocks, gardens, Gilead) and from the glory of the kingdom (David's tower, warriors' shields, imported spices). His praise uses *their* world, which is part of the lesson for husbands.
- **Gregory of Nyssa** made the same observation in reverse: the images don't work literally ("I do not understand how anyone could say that praise is being given her teeth by comparing them with prolific flocks"). He used that as his reason to allegorize. A modern reader notices the same oddity and concludes that the poetry is evocative, not visual. **Good discussion point:** the ancient allegorist and the modern literary reader agree the images aren't literal pictures. They disagree about what follows from that.
- **Matthew Henry** (Complete) likewise argues the comparisons are "not proper indeed to represent any external beauty, for they were not designed to do so." The podcast can respectfully disagree. They are perfectly proper for external beauty *in poetry*.
- The Pulpit Commentary's humility is a good model: "We can scarcely expect to make out every particular in an Eastern description."

### Q2. "No flaw in you": is it true of a real woman? Of the church?
- **Of a real woman.** This is love's verdict, not a medical report. In the eyes of a husband who loves her and has bound himself to her by covenant, she is complete. She is not sinless, and he is not blind. His love chooses to see and name what is lovely, and it covers rather than catalogues (1 Pet 4:8; Prov 10:12). Earlier she called herself "dark" and sun-scorched (1:5-6). His praise answers her insecurity. Duguid, as reviewed by Estes, reads the couple as "idealized" yet "necessarily imperfect, because they live in a fallen world". So the Song presents an ideal that real marriages aim at and fall short of, and that shortfall points beyond itself.
- **Of the church.** The Reformed tradition answers carefully and in several layers. **Durham** is the best model. He rejects (a) the "popish" sense, that the church is sinless, and (b) the "Antinomian" sense, that her sins aren't really sins. He then affirms that she is "all fair" in four respects: (1) **justification**: legally clean, no sin imputed; (2) **sanctification**: renewed in every part, though not perfect in degree; (3) **Christ's acceptance**: he "reckons not" her spots; (4) **Christ's design**: he *will* present her "without spot or wrinkle" (Eph 5:27). **Gill** makes the same justification and sanctification distinction. **Henry**: "no spot in thee, as far as thou art renewed." So *already* in justification and *not yet* in glorification. Eph 5:25-27 has the same already and not yet: Christ *gave himself* and *washed* her, *so that* he might *present* her spotless.
- **Hudson Taylor's** mirror image is a memorable illustration. The bride is spotless where she reflects the Bridegroom's own image.

### Q3. How the Puritans and older Reformed read it, compared with modern readers
- **Older tradition (Origen and Gregory through Bernard, the Puritans and Gill).** The Song is about Christ and the church, or Christ and the soul, and each image maps onto something. Common Puritan mappings: teeth are ministers who "chew" the Word for others (Gregory; Henry: "Ministers are the church's teeth; like nurses, they chew the meat for the babes of Christ"); eyes are ministers or the eye of faith; neck is faith joining body to Head (Henry), or ministers (Gregory: Paul was a "neck"); the two breasts are the two Testaments or two sacraments (Henry lists these as options); the mountain of myrrh is Moriah or the temple.
  - **James Durham, *Clavis Cantici* (1668):** a Scottish Covenanter. He reads the Song as entirely spiritual, Christ to the Bride, but with careful doctrinal precision, especially on 4:7.
  - **Richard Sibbes, *Bowels Opened* (1639):** twenty sermons on Song **4:16-6:3**, so he does not expound 4:1-7 directly. His treatment of the church's beauty (on 5:9, "fairest among women") gives the Puritan theology of "borrowed beauty," which applies directly to 4:7.
  - **Matthew Henry:** two different tones. The *Concise* (Bible Hub "mhc") is strikingly restrained ("great mistakes are made by fanciful guesses"). The *Complete* commentary ("mhcw") allegorizes each image at length but offers alternatives ("Some apply…others…"). **Note for hosts:** the Concise quote is a great endorsement of the podcast's restraint *from inside the allegorical tradition*.
  - **John Gill:** thoroughgoing allegory, with strong justification theology on 4:7.
  - **Spurgeon and Hudson Taylor:** devotional and Christ-and-believer readings, which stress Christ's present delight in his people.
- **Modern readers.** Since the 19th century (Wetzstein's discovery of the wasf, and Delitzsch's still-reverent literal reading), most commentators read the Song first as love poetry about human marriage and sexuality. Evangelical voices then differ on how to move to Christ:
  - **Typological or wisdom approach (Duguid):** the Song shows ideal married love; our failure to measure up drives us to Christ. Estes: "This theological sense lies beyond the text of the Song rather than being derived from within the Song."
  - **Both-and or canonical-allegory approach (Hamilton; Kyle Dillon at TGC):** marriage itself is designed to picture Christ and the church (Eph 5:32), so the Song may be read that way. **Note:** Dillon is willing to use the word "allegory." The podcast's line is more restrained. It is better to say *analogy grounded in Ephesians 5*, without mapping individual images.
- **Suggested podcast stance.** Agree with the Puritans on the *gospel substance* of 4:7 (Christ's people are "all fair" in him: justified now, perfected at the end). Agree with modern readers on the *method*: the images are about a woman's beauty, and the Christward move happens at the level of the whole relationship (Eph 5:25-32), not the parts. Henry's Concise is your bridge.

## 4. Practical Applications

1. **Husbands, praise your wife specifically, out loud, and in your own world's language.** He doesn't say "you look nice." He goes feature by feature, with images taken from the life they share (flocks, hills, the king's tower). Praise that notices takes attention. A wife who called herself "dark" and sun-scorched (1:5-6) hears a man insist she is beautiful. Make the wasf a habit: name what you see and what it means to you. Notice that all of this comes *before* 4:16-5:1. Tender, unhurried words come before intimacy.
2. **Love's verdict covers.** "No flaw in you" is not denial. It is a covenant decision about what to dwell on and what to say aloud. Durham's "Christ is no severe interpreter of his people's actions" is a direct pattern for husbands (Eph 5:25: "as Christ loved the church"). Ask: do my words to my wife read like a list of defects or like a wasf?
3. **How God sees his people in Christ (justification).** Read through Ephesians 5, 4:7 points to the gospel: Christ gave himself for his bride "that he might present the church to himself in splendor, without spot or wrinkle…holy and without blemish" (Eph 5:27). The believer is "all fair" now *in Christ*, through imputed righteousness, with no condemnation (Gill, Durham). The beauty is borrowed (Sibbes; Henry: "The spouse shines in her husband's rays"). This answers the accusing conscience: Spurgeon notes Christ speaks of it "as already done."
4. **Already and not yet (sanctification and glory).** "No flaw" is not a license (Durham's anti-Antinomian guardrail). Christ's *design* is to make his bride completely what he already declares her to be. Hudson Taylor: as we behold him we reflect his image, and "the bride becomes ever fairer." Assurance of being accepted fuels the pursuit of holiness. It doesn't replace it.
5. **The Levitical background of *mum*.** Under the law, no priest or sacrifice with a *mum* could come near God. In Christ, the spotless Lamb (1 Pet 1:19, *amōmou*), a people full of blemishes is declared to have "no blemish" and brought near. That is the logic of the gospel inside a single Hebrew word.
6. **Reading restraint as a spiritual discipline.** Henry's Concise warning ("great mistakes are made by fanciful guesses") can model for listeners how to read hard poetry humbly: let the plain sense stand, and let the New Testament (Eph 5) set the direction of any Christward application.
# Research: John 6:16-24 — Jesus Walks on the Sea ("It is I; do not be afraid")

## 1. Text (ESV)

Source: https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John+6%3A16-24&version=ESV (fetched)

> 16 When evening came, his disciples went down to the sea, 17 got into a boat, and started across the sea to Capernaum. It was now dark, and Jesus had not yet come to them. 18 The sea became rough because a strong wind was blowing. 19 When they had rowed about three or four miles, they saw Jesus walking on the sea and coming near the boat, and they were frightened. 20 But he said to them, "It is I; do not be afraid." 21 Then they were glad to take him into the boat, and immediately the boat was at the land to which they were going.
>
> 22 On the next day the crowd that remained on the other side of the sea saw that there had been only one boat there, and that Jesus had not entered the boat with his disciples, but that his disciples had gone away alone. 23 Other boats from Tiberias came near the place where they had eaten the bread after the Lord had given thanks. 24 So when the crowd saw that Jesus was not there, nor his disciples, they themselves got into the boats and went to Capernaum, seeking Jesus.

ESV footnote on 6:19: "Greek twenty-five or thirty stadia; a stadion was about 607 feet or 185 meters." ESV cross-reference on 6:20: Luke 24:38-39 (the risen Christ calming the disciples' fear that he was a spirit).

### Outline

1. **The disciples sent into the night (vv. 16-17).** Evening, the boat, set out for Capernaum; "It was now dark, and Jesus had not yet come to them."
2. **The storm (v. 18).** Rough sea, strong wind.
3. **The Lord on the waters (v. 19).** After 25-30 stadia, they see Jesus walking on the sea, drawing near. They are terrified.
4. **The word that ends fear (v. 20).** *Egō eimi; mē phobeisthe*: "It is I; do not be afraid."
5. **Received and arrived (v. 21).** Glad to take him in; "immediately the boat was at the land."
6. **The crowd's puzzle and pursuit (vv. 22-24).** One boat only; Jesus had not gone in it; boats from Tiberias; the crowd crosses to Capernaum "seeking Jesus". This sets up the Bread of Life discourse (6:25-59), which opens with Jesus exposing their motive (6:26).

### Background notes

- **Setting (6:14-15).** Right after the feeding of the 5,000 the crowd says "This is indeed the Prophet" and plans to "take him by force to make him king"; Jesus withdraws to the mountain alone. Matthew 14:22 and Mark 6:45 say he *made* (ESV "made"; KJV "constrained") the disciples get into the boat. Ryle (Notes on v. 16, fetched) suggests Jesus "probably saw that in their ignorance of the spiritual nature of His kingdom they were ready to fall in with the wishes of the multitude." So the storm-crossing is partly a rescue of the Twelve from messianic fever.
- **John vs. Matthew 14:22-33 / Mark 6:45-52:**
  - John has no Peter walking on the water (Matt 14:28-31 only) and no "Truly you are the Son of God" worship scene (Matt 14:33).
  - John doesn't mention the fourth watch (Matt 14:25; Mark 6:48, roughly 3-6 a.m.), Mark's "he meant to pass by them" (Mark 6:48, echoing the theophanies of Exod 33:19-22; 34:6; 1 Kgs 19:11), or Mark's "their hearts were hardened" because they "did not understand about the loaves" (Mark 6:52).
  - John doesn't say the wind stopped (Matt 14:32; Mark 6:51).
  - Only John has "they were glad to take him into the boat, and immediately the boat was at the land" (v. 21). Only John has the 25-30 stadia, the boats from Tiberias, and the crowd's reasoning about the one boat (vv. 22-24).
  - All three share the core words *egō eimi; mē phobeisthe* (Matt 14:27; Mark 6:50; John 6:20).
  - Chrysostom (Hom. 43, fetched) thought John's event "a different miracle from that found in Matthew xiv." Almost all commentators, including Calvin, Ryle and Ellicott (all fetched), treat it as the same event.
- **Distance.** 25-30 stadia is about 15,000-18,000 ft, roughly 2.9-3.5 miles (ESV "three or four miles"). Josephus (Wars 3.10.7) gives the lake as 40 furlongs wide and 140 long, per the editor's note in Calvin's commentary (fetched). Calvin cites Pliny at 6 miles by 16. Begg (fetched) gives 13 miles long and 8 at the widest. Ellicott and Ryle (fetched) both conclude they were roughly halfway across, 2-3 miles from shore. Ryle uses this against rationalist "he was walking on the shore" readings.
- **Route.** They crossed from the feeding site, on the NE shore in the district east of Bethsaida (Luke 9:10), toward Capernaum on the NW shore (Ryle notes, fetched). Matthew and Mark say they landed at Gennesaret, the plain just SW of Capernaum, which Ryle reconciles with John.
- **Winds.** The lake lies about 690 ft below sea level and is ringed by hills cut by ravines. Ryle quotes the traveller W. M. Thomson (*The Land and the Book*): the gorges "act like gigantic funnels to draw down the cold winds from the mountains," and he had seen the lake "quiver, and leap, and boil like a caldron, when driven by fierce winds." Four of the disciples were experienced fishermen, which says something about how bad this storm was (Ryle).
- **Tiberias (v. 23).** Built by Herod Antipas (c. AD 20) on the SW shore and named for the emperor. John alone calls the lake "the Sea of Tiberias" (6:1; 21:1). Boats from there, probably sheltering from the same storm or on business, gave the crowd passage.
- **"Jesus Boat" / Ancient Galilee Boat.** In 1986, during a drought, brothers Moshe and Yuval Lufan of Kibbutz Ginosar found it in the mud on the NW shore, about 1.5 km north of Migdal. Nautical archaeologist Shelley Wachsmann led the excavation. It measures about 8.3 m x 2.3 m x 1.3 m (27 x 7.5 x 4.3 ft) and could hold up to about 15 people. Radiocarbon dates put it between 120 BC and AD 40. It took about 11 years to conserve and is on display at the Yigal Allon Museum, Ginosar. Responsible framing: there is "no evidence to link this particular boat to Jesus or his disciples," but Wachsmann says it "is of the type that would have been used by Jesus and his disciples." (Source: https://www.seetheholyland.net/?p=313, fetched via summarizer, so treat the wording as close paraphrase and not verbatim.) Useful for picturing 12 men rowing a boat of that size in a squall.

### Old Testament background for "walking on the sea" and *egō eimi*

- **Job 9:8.** God "alone stretched out the heavens and trampled the waves of the sea" (ESV). The LXX reads roughly "walking on the sea as on dry ground." Chrysostom (fetched) quotes it directly: "the sea retaining its proper nature so bare its Lord upon its surface, thus testifying to the Scripture which says, Who walks upon the sea as upon a pavement. Job 9:8."
- **Psalm 77:19.** "Your way was through the sea, your path through the great waters; yet your footprints were unseen." This is an Exodus psalm, and John 6 is full of Exodus: Passover (6:4), manna (6:31), grumbling (6:41). Begg (fetched) makes this link.
- **Psalm 107:23-30.** Sailors at their wits' end cry to the LORD; "He made the storm be still... Then they were glad that the waters were quiet, and he brought them to their desired haven." Compare John 6:21, "they were glad... and immediately the boat was at the land." Ellicott (fetched) thinks the psalm "were present to the mind of St. John." Begg (fetched) reads it as the disciples' own Scripture memory.
- **Exodus 3:14.** "I AM WHO I AM... Say this to the people of Israel: I AM has sent me to you." LXX: *egō eimi ho ōn*.
- **Isaiah 43.** Verse 2: "When you pass through the waters, I will be with you." Verse 5: "Fear not, for I am with you." Verse 10: "that you may know and believe me and understand that I am he" (LXX *egō eimi*). Verse 16: "who makes a way in the sea, a path in the mighty waters." Verse 25 has the doubled *egō eimi egō eimi*. Isaiah 43 is probably the richest single background: *egō eimi*, "fear not", and a path through the sea all in one chapter.
- **Also:** Ps 29:3, 10; 93:3-4; Isa 51:10 ("made the depths of the sea a way"); Hab 3:15 ("You trampled the sea with your horses"); Exod 14 (Red Sea). Matthew Henry (fetched) contrasts this with Moses: "Christ walked upon the water as upon dry land, which was more than Moses's dividing the water and walking through the water."
- **In John:** *egō eimi* used without a predicate at 8:24, 28, 58; 13:19; and 18:5-8, where the arresting party "drew back and fell to the ground." Calvin (fetched) links 6:20 and 18:6. *Egō eimi* with a predicate (the "I am" sayings) starts at 6:35: "I am the bread of life."

## 2. Interpretive questions

### (a) Is *egō eimi* here a divine claim or simple identification ("It's me")?

- **Plain sense.** In context it is self-identification. They think he is a ghost (Matt 14:26; Mark 6:49), and he says, "It's me." Joseph uses the same words in Gen 45:3 LXX (*egō eimi Iōsēph*), as Begg (fetched) notes.
- **Ryle (fetched) says it is simple identification.** He calls the divine-name reading "a pious thought, but hardly in keeping with the context," and says the Greek words "are the only words that He could well have used." He is a useful conservative voice who doesn't over-read.
- **For a double meaning:**
  1. The setting is a theophany. Only God treads the sea (Job 9:8), makes a path through the waters (Ps 77:19; Isa 43:16), and stills the storm (Ps 107).
  2. Mark's "meant to pass by them" (Mark 6:48) uses theophany language (Exod 33-34; 1 Kgs 19).
  3. The words come paired with "do not be afraid," as in Isa 43:1-13.
  4. John tends to load ordinary words with deeper meaning (e.g., 3:3 "again/from above"; 11:50). He sets this *egō eimi* just before the predicate "I am" sayings (6:35, 41, 48, 51) and later uses the bare *egō eimi* (8:58; 18:6) in clearly divine senses.
  5. Matthew's version ends in worship, "Truly you are the Son of God" (Matt 14:33).
- **Begg (fetched) holds a cautious middle position.** It "may actually be very much involved in this, and it may not." If it means more than "Hey, guys, it's me," that is because of "Moses and manna and the exodus and the wilderness... swirling around in this experience."
- **Calvin (fetched)** is not interested in the lexical question. His focus is the *effect* of the words: the same "It is I" comforts disciples and fells the wicked (John 18:6).
- **Suggested podcast stance:** "It is I" is real self-identification, *and* the One identifying himself is doing what only YHWH does. The words don't need to carry the whole divine claim, because the act already makes it.

### (b) Why did Jesus send them out alone into the dark and the storm?

- **To protect them from the crowd's king-making zeal** (6:15; Matt 14:22 "made them"). Ryle (fetched) and Henry (fetched) both say this.
- **To train their faith and show his power.** Chrysostom (fetched): "By the first He teaches them how great (an evil) it is to be forsaken by Him, and makes their longing greater; by the second, again, He shows forth His power." Calvin (fetched): "the Lord often makes his people fall into alarming dangers, that they may more plainly and familiarly recognize him in their deliverance."
- **He was interceding for them meanwhile** (Matt 14:23; Mark 6:46). Henry: "Christ was now in the mount praying for them; and yet they were in this distress." This is a natural picture of Christ's heavenly intercession (Heb 7:25) while the church labors.
- **"It was now dark" (*skotia*)** may carry John's light/darkness theme (1:5; 8:12; 12:35). Augustine (fetched) reads it as spiritual: "Rightly he said dark, for the light had not come to them." Ryle notes the Greek word "is always rendered 'darkness' in other places." Begg is cautious: "Sometimes darkness in the Bible has a peculiar significance... we're left wondering."
- **Caution on allegory.** Augustine reads the boat as the Church, the waves as end-time iniquity, and the 25/30 furlongs as law (5×5) completed by gospel (5×6). Begg (fetched) warns against this kind of reading: "you can teach that from anywhere you want, but you can't teach it from this passage." Ryle and Henry allow a measured "church in the storm" application. This is a good tension to air on the podcast.

### (c) Is "immediately the boat was at the land" a second miracle?

- **Yes:**
  - Gill (fetched), following the 5th-century poet Nonnus: "another miracle was wrought; the ship was in an instant at the place whither they intended to go."
  - Chrysostom (fetched): "He not only gave them a safe passage, but also one with a fair wind."
  - Augustine (fetched) reads it as a sign of the end of the journey: "from the watery to the solid, from the agitated to the firm, from the way to the goal."
- **Not necessarily:**
  - Ryle (fetched): "There is, perhaps, no occasion to suppose any other miracle." The storm stopped (Matt 14:32), so the rest of the crossing was quick.
  - Ellicott (fetched) argues the Synoptics would hardly leave out such a miracle, and that "immediately" may mean the boat went "straight" after being tossed about.
- **Agnostic:** Begg (fetched): "You want a third miracle? That's fine... I don't think it adds anything to it, nor is anything lost by not considering it."
- **Grammar point.** *Ēthelon oun labein auton* literally means "they were willing/wanting to take him." Chrysostom reads it to mean he did *not* actually get in. Ellicott and Ryle say Matthew and Mark show he did, and that the phrase means "gladly received." The ESV rendering "were glad to take him" follows that view.
- **Ps 107:30** ("he brought them to their desired haven") is the likely background either way. The point is the arrival, not how fast it happened.

### (d) Why do the crowds search (vv. 22-26)?

- **John spells out their reasoning.** One boat; Jesus was not in it; yet he is gone. This is circumstantial evidence for the sea-walking that the crowd itself did not see. Calvin (fetched): "the multitude might conclude that Christ had gone across by divine power." Chrysostom (fetched) notes that even so, "they asked Him not how He crossed over... nor sought to understand so great a sign."
- **Their motive is exposed in 6:26:** "you are seeking me, not because you saw signs, but because you ate your fill of the loaves." Henry (fetched, on 6:15) quotes Augustine's line: "Vix quæritur Jesus propter Jesum, sed propter aliud — Jesus is usually sought after for something else, not for his own sake."
- **Henry's irony** (fetched): the "unsound hypocritical followers of Christ had a calm and pleasant passage, while his sincere disciples had a rough and stormy one."
- **The sequence matters.** The crowd seeks bread and an earthly king. The disciples receive the Lord himself on the sea. The chapter ends with many disciples leaving (6:66) and Peter's "Lord, to whom shall we go?" (6:68). Begg: "proximity to Jesus is no guarantee of real faith."

## 4. Applications (for a Reformed / PCA audience)

1. **Obedience can lead straight into the storm.** The disciples were in the boat because Jesus told them to be (Matt 14:22). Trouble does not prove you have left God's will (Henry; Ryle: "Christ knew it, and Christ appointed it, and it was working for their good"). WCF 5.5: God sometimes leaves his children "to manifold temptations" to humble them and make them more watchful.
2. **Christ is praying while we row.** He was on the mountain praying before he came on the water. Today he intercedes at the Father's right hand (Heb 7:25; Rom 8:34; WSC 25). His absence from sight is not absence of care.
3. **Our worst fears often come from misreading his approach.** They were more afraid of the "ghost" than of the storm. Henry: "most frightened when we are ready to be helped." Calvin: without his *word*, a bare display of power just breeds fear and idol-making. We come to know Christ through his spoken word, not through raw experience. This is a Reformed emphasis on Word over sight.
4. **"It is I" means both comfort and judgment.** The words that calm the disciples knock down Judas's band (John 18:6). How you hear Christ's self-revelation depends on whether you are his (Calvin). Evangelistic edge: will you hear "I am" as Savior or as Judge?
5. **The One who treads the sea is YHWH.** Job 9:8, Ps 77:19 and Isa 43 make this a Christological text. Jesus does what only God does, and that grounds the "I am" sayings that follow. Faith rests on who he is, not just on what he provides.
6. **Receive him and you are home.** "Glad to take him into the boat... immediately at the land." Whether or not that is a second miracle, his presence turns the voyage into arrival (Ps 107:30). Henry: "nearer to it than we think we are." This has an eschatological edge: the church's crossing ends when he comes (Ryle's note).
7. **Seek Jesus for Jesus' sake.** The crowd crossed the sea for bread and a political king (6:26). Augustine via Henry: "Jesus is usually sought after for something else, not for his own sake." Effort and closeness are not faith (Begg). This leads into 6:27-29 and 6:66-69.
8. **On preaching method.** Augustine's number allegory (25 = law, 30 = law plus gospel) and Begg's warning against allegory make a good discussion of the Reformed grammatical-historical approach. The text points to Christ first, and to "the church in the storm" only by careful application.

### Word-study notes worth using on air

- **"Imperishable" ties back to chapter 1.** Peter used the same family of words earlier: the inheritance is ἄφθαρτον, "imperishable" (1:4); believers were ransomed "not with perishable things [φθαρτοῖς] such as silver or gold" (1:18); they were born again "not of perishable seed but of imperishable [ἀφθάρτου]" (1:23). So the wife's beauty in 3:4 belongs with the imperishable things of the gospel. Gold, which shows up in 3:3, is exactly what 1:18 called perishable. (Precision note: the "precious" blood in 1:19 is τίμιος, not πολυτελής. The link to chapter 1 is the perishable/imperishable contrast, not the word "precious".)
- **"Very precious" is the word for luxury.** In 1 Tim 2:9, πολυτελής describes the "costly attire" women should not rely on (ἱματισμῷ πολυτελεῖ, SBLGNT). In Mark 14:3 it describes the "very costly" nard. Peter takes the vocabulary of expensive fashion and says what God counts as truly high-end: a gentle and quiet spirit.
- **"Gentle" (praus).** Jesus uses this word of himself ("I am gentle," Matt 11:29), and it appears in the beatitude ("Blessed are the meek," Matt 5:5). The meekness asked of the wife is the meekness of Christ, and 2:21-25 has just set him out as the pattern. Peter asks the same of every Christian in 3:15-16: "with gentleness [πραΰτητος] and respect."
- **"Quiet" (hēsychios).** The word means settled or tranquil, not silent. It is used in 1 Tim 2:2 ("a peaceful and quiet life"). The point is a calm, unflustered spirit, and v.6 ("do not fear") explains where that calm comes from: hope in God (v.5).
- **"Weaker vessel" (skeuos).** A "vessel" is any container or tool. The comparative ἀσθενεστέρῳ ("weaker") implies that the husband is a vessel too, only a stronger one. Calvin notes, "The word vessel, as it is well known, means in Scripture any sort of instrument." Compare 1 Thess 4:4 (ESV cross-ref) and 2 Tim 2:20-21.
- **"Understanding way" (kata gnōsin).** Literally "according to knowledge." This can mean knowledge of his wife, knowledge of God's will for marriage, or both. Gill takes it as both (see quote bank).
- **Proverbs 3:25.** The ESV cross-references Prov 3:25 ("Do not be afraid of sudden terror"). Commentators commonly note that the Greek Old Testament (LXX) of Prov 3:25 uses the same rare noun πτόησις ("terror") found in 1 Pet 3:6. *I did not fetch the LXX text to verify this; check it before stating it on air.* Proverbs 3 is the "trust in the LORD with all your heart" chapter (3:5), so the echo fits "women who hoped in God."

## 2. Place in the letter: outline and flow

**Big picture of 1 Peter.** The letter is written to "elect exiles of the dispersion" (1:1), Christians in Asia Minor who are slandered and pressured by their pagan neighbors. The theme sentence of 2:11-12 is: as "sojourners and exiles," "keep your conduct among the Gentiles honorable, so that when they speak against you as evildoers, they may see your good deeds and glorify God on the day of visitation." Note "see" and "conduct" (ἀναστροφή). The same pair returns in 3:1-2: unbelieving husbands "see" the wives' "conduct" (ἀναστροφή, twice).

**The submission section (household code), 2:13 to 3:7:**
1. **2:13-17** All believers: "Be subject for the Lord's sake to every human institution" (emperor, governors). The goal is that "by doing good you should put to silence the ignorance of foolish people" (2:15). Freedom is not a cover-up for evil; "Honor everyone... Fear God. Honor the emperor" (2:17).
2. **2:18-20** Household servants: "be subject to your masters with all respect, not only to the good and gentle but also to the unjust." Suffering for doing good is "a gracious thing in the sight of God."
3. **2:21-25** *The hinge: Christ's example.* "Christ also suffered for you, leaving you an example... When he was reviled, he did not revile in return; when he suffered, he did not threaten, but continued entrusting himself to him who judges justly" (2:21-23). Then he "bore our sins in his body on the tree" (2:24).
4. **3:1-6** Wives: "Likewise" (ὁμοίως, *homoiōs*). The word ties this paragraph back to the pattern of subjection in 2:13 and 2:18 and to Christ's example in 2:21-25. The wife who quietly entrusts herself to God, even with a hostile or unbelieving husband, is following the path of the Christ who "entrusted himself to him who judges justly."
5. **3:7** Husbands: "Likewise" (ὁμοίως) again. The husband is not told to "be subject", but he is placed under the same Christ-shaped logic of honoring others (2:17, "Honor everyone") and of self-giving.
6. **3:8-12** "Finally, all of you": unity, sympathy, brotherly love, a tender heart, humility, blessing instead of reviling (Ps 34). This means the household code ends with commands for *everyone*.

**Notable features:**
- Wives get six verses and husbands one. The likely reason is the pastoral situation: Christian wives of pagan husbands were the most exposed group. The wife's case is the hardest and most missionary one, much like the slave of the "unjust" master in 2:18.
- Unlike Eph 5 and Col 3, Peter does not address children or slaves' masters. He addresses only the subordinate party in each relationship, *except* husbands. That makes the address to husbands stand out all the more. In a believing marriage, Christian husbands are not exempt.
- 3:1 "even if some do not obey the word" (καὶ εἴ τινες) shows that Peter addresses all wives but has the mixed marriage especially in view.

**Roman background: a wife's religion followed her husband's.** Plutarch (c. AD 46-120), *Advice to Bride and Groom* (*Coniugalia Praecepta*) §19 (Moralia 140D), Loeb trans. F. C. Babbitt (1928), via LacusCurtius: https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Plutarch/Moralia/Coniugalia_praecepta*.html

> A wife ought not to make friends of her own, but to enjoy her husband's friends in common with him. The gods are the first and most important friends. Wherefore it is becoming for a wife to worship and to know only the gods that her husband believes in, and to shut the front door tight upon all queer rituals and outlandish superstitions. For with no god do stealthy and secret rites performed by a woman find any favour.

Significance: by Plutarch's standard, a Christian wife of a pagan husband had *already* done something shameful by refusing her husband's gods. Peter does not tell her to give up Christ. He tells her to show by her conduct that her faith makes her a better wife, not a rebellious one. Tertullian's *To His Wife* 2.4 (c. AD 200, quoted below) shows what this meant in practice: the pagan husband schedules the baths for fast days and blocks her from visiting prisoners and attending night services.

## 3. Verse-by-verse notes

**v.1 "be subject to your own husbands"**: "your own" (ἰδίοις) means not men in general. This is a word to wives about their own husbands. It is not a statement that women as such are subordinate to men as such.

**"won without a word"**: there is a play on words: they disobey "the word" (the gospel), so they will be won "without a word" (without nagging or preaching at them). This does not mean people are saved apart from the gospel. Calvin and Gill both insist "faith comes by hearing" (Rom 10:17). The wife's conduct softens the husband and makes him willing to hear (see Calvin and Gill in the quote bank). The husband already knows *what* she believes, and that is why he is hostile. What he needs is to *see* it.

**v.2 "respectful and pure conduct"**: literally "pure conduct in fear". This is most likely fear of God, as Gill allows, and it explains how she can submit without being servile: she does it for God's sake (cf. 2:13 "for the Lord's sake").

**vv.3-4 adornment**: the construction is "not this... but that." It is a contrast of priorities, not a list of banned items. Read woodenly as a ban, "the clothing you wear" would forbid wearing clothes at all. Calvin, Gill, Henry, and the Ligonier devotional all make this point. Still, Calvin denies that dress is morally "indifferent": excess comes from a corrupted mind. Some church fathers (Tertullian, Cyprian, Clement) read the verse more strictly as a ban on gold and hair-plaiting. That is useful for showing the range of views.

**v.4 "hidden person of the heart"**: the outward body is visible and perishing, the inner person is hidden and imperishable (cf. 2 Cor 4:16; Rom 2:29 in the ESV cross-refs). "In God's sight": what matters is God's evaluation, not men's eyes. Calvin makes this pointed: women adorn themselves to catch men's eyes, but Peter redirects them to God's eye.

**vv.5-6 holy women who hoped in God; Sarah**: their submission came from hope in God, not fear of their husbands. Sarah is the model, but Peter quotes Gen 18:12, where Sarah laughs *to herself* in unbelief: "After I am worn out, and my lord is old, shall I have pleasure?" (ESV). This is an odd text to pick (see the interpretive questions below). "You are her children": Gentile women become daughters of Sarah by faith and well-doing, as men become sons of Abraham by faith (Gal 3:7; Rom 4).

**v.6 "do not fear anything that is frightening"**: this is the key to the whole paragraph. The wife's submission is not cowardice. It is courage in the face of a possibly hostile husband and society. Compare 3:14-15: "Have no fear of them, nor be troubled, but in your hearts honor Christ the Lord as holy." Calvin reads it narrowly as not fearing that submission will make her worse off. The 19th-century editor's footnote in the Calvin translation (citing Macknight) and Gill and Henry read it as not being frightened by the husband *out of her religion*. Spurgeon takes it as Sarah's settled faith under trials.

**v.7 husbands**:
- "live with" (συνοικοῦντες) means sharing a household: the whole of married life, including the sexual relationship (Gill).
- "in an understanding way" (κατὰ γνῶσιν) means with knowledge of her, of God's word, and of his duty.
- "showing honor" (ἀπονέμοντες τιμήν): the word for honor is the same one used in 2:17, "Honor everyone... honor the emperor." The husband gives his wife the kind of honor due to the emperor.
- "as the weaker vessel": this is a reason *for* honor, not contempt. Calvin explains it as 1 Cor 12:23-style honor given to weaker members. Henry says the weakness is "no just reason either for separation or contempt."
- "heirs with you of the grace of life": her spiritual equality with him. The textual variant ("since you are joint heirs") does not change the sense.
- "so that your prayers may not be hindered": the husband's treatment of his wife has a direct effect on his relationship with God. Compare Matt 5:23-24 and 1 Tim 2:8.

## 4. Interpretive questions

**Q1. Does submission require a wife to follow her husband into sin or into his religion?**
No. The passage itself assumes the opposite. Peter's original readers were women who had *already* declined their husbands' religion, against the cultural norm stated by Plutarch. Peter does not tell them to go back. He tells them to "obey" God's word while their husbands "do not obey the word." Submission is "for the Lord's sake" (2:13) and sits under the controlling principle "We must obey God rather than men" (Acts 5:29). Henry: wives should not, "through fear of your husbands, either quit the truth you profess or neglect your duty to them." Gill: wives obey "their lawful commands." The Macknight paraphrase in the Calvin translation footnote reads v.6 as "not being frightened to actions contrary to your religion through fear of displeasing them." Tertullian's *To His Wife* shows the real-life clash points (fasts, worship, the Supper, visiting prisoners, hospitality). Sarah is not a model in the Gen 12 and 20 episodes, where Abraham's request was sinful. Peter cites Gen 18, and Gill's mention of Egypt and Gerar praises her courage, not the deception. *Pastoral caution: submission never requires a wife to stay under abuse or to cover up crime. The civil authority of 2:13-14 exists "to punish those who do evil."*

**Q2. What does "weaker vessel" mean?**
Most likely physical strength (and with it social vulnerability), not moral, intellectual, or spiritual inferiority. The same verse rules out spiritual inferiority: she is a co-heir "of the grace of life." The comparative implies that both are "vessels" (fragile clay jars, 2 Cor 4:7), so the husband is only *relatively* stronger. Ligonier gives three possible senses: physical strength; the low dignity society assigned to women (which Peter does not endorse); and lesser authority in marriage. Calvin and Gill reflect their own era in extending it to "endowments of mind" (Gill) or women being "suspicious and timid" (Calvin on v.6). It is worth saying honestly on air that the text does not require those views. In every source the weakness is grounds for *more* honor and gentleness, never for contempt.

**Q3. Is the adornment verse a prohibition?**
No, it is a contrast. The parallel phrase "or the clothing you wear" proves the point: Peter is not banning clothes. Calvin calls it "immoderate strictness" to forbid "neatness and elegance in clothing," while still condemning vanity and excess. Gill cites Rebekah's gold jewelry (Gen 24). Henry says apparel "is not absolutely forbidden." The Ligonier devotional says that a literal ban would mean "forbidding the wearing of any clothing at all." The church fathers (Tertullian, Cyprian, Clement) were stricter, and that is a useful example of exegesis drifting into asceticism. The principle applies to men too (Ligonier: "This passage has a message for men and women"), since men also have status displays: trucks, watches, gear, physiques.

**Q4. Sarah calling Abraham "lord" (Gen 18:12): how do commentators handle the fact that it was said to herself, in a moment of doubt and laughter?**
- The phrase in Gen 18:12 is private and comes in the same breath as her unbelieving laugh. Peter picks it anyway.
- Calvin answers that the word itself is not the virtue. Peter means "Sarah usually spoke thus." In other words, an offhand, private remark shows her habitual attitude. What she called him when talking to herself, with no one listening, is what she really thought of him.
- Henry sees grace in the choice: God "covers a multitude of failings; Sara's infidelity and derision are overlooked, when her virtues are celebrated."
- Spurgeon admits that "she laughed once" but calls it "but a slip for the moment," and points to Heb 11:11, "by faith Sarah herself received power to conceive."
- Gill places her obedience in the larger pattern of her life: following Abraham out of Ur, to Canaan, to Egypt.
- Podcast angle: this is gospel encouragement. God's verdict on Sarah is written in Hebrews 11 and 1 Peter 3, not by the laugh in Gen 18. Wives (and husbands) who fail are still counted as "her children" by faith.

**Q5. "That your prayers may not be hindered."**
- Whose prayers? "Your" (ὑμῶν) is plural. It could mean the husband's prayers, or the couple's prayers together. Calvin takes it of both together, and from it draws the general doctrine that "no one ought to come to God except he is united to his brethren." Henry: "their prayers one with another and one for another will be hindered." Gill draws a case for family prayer.
- What hinders them? (a) Strife at home makes it impossible to pray together with one heart (Calvin, Henry, Gill). (b) More seriously, God himself does not listen to a man who mistreats his wife (cf. Ps 66:18; Mal 2:13-16; Matt 5:23-24). The verb ἐγκόπτω means "to cut in, obstruct."
- Calvin rejects a reading based on 1 Cor 7:5, which takes it as meaning too much marital intimacy distracts from prayer.
- For the male listener, this is the sharpest edge in the passage: how you treat your wife governs your access to God.

## 6. Applications (listener is a man)

**For husbands (lead with this; it's the listener's verse):**
1. **Study your wife.** "According to knowledge" is a call to learn her, in the way a man studies his trade. Ask: what weighs on her, what frightens her, what helps her flourish? Henry: "not according to lust, as brutes; nor according to passion, as devils."
2. **Honor her the way you'd honor a king.** Peter uses the same "honor" (τιμή) as 2:17. Chrysostom's model speech gives a practical script: tell her you value her "above all gold" and would rather be home with her than "in the market-place."
3. **Strength is for protection, not leverage.** "Weaker vessel" is a reason for gentleness. Never use your physical strength, volume, money, or authority against her. Gill: "hide [her infirmities] as much as he can, and not expose them."
4. **She is your fellow heir.** You will stand beside her in glory as an equal. Lead her now in a way you won't be ashamed of then. Pray *with* her.
5. **Your prayer life is tied to your marriage.** If prayer feels like a closed door, look first at how you are treating your wife (Calvin: "Among strifes and contentions there is no place for prayer"; cf. Matt 5:23-24).
6. **Learn from 3:1-6 as well.** Men are also called to the "gentle and quiet spirit" (Matt 11:29; 1 Pet 3:15). Calm faith instead of anxious control is Christlike in a husband too (Spurgeon: "Christian men also").

**For wives (and for husbands to understand what is being asked of their wives):**
1. Submission is free, voluntary, "for the Lord's sake," and grounded in hope in God, not fear of man. It is never submission to sin.
2. With a hard or unbelieving husband, a godly life speaks louder than arguments (Monica). Patience, and timing what she says (Augustine: "so soon as he was grown calm"), can be a mission field.
3. True beauty is something God values highly. The world's beauty industry measures what is perishable.
4. "Do not fear": a wife's submission is an act of courage and trust, as Sarah "hoped in God."

**For both:**
- The whole section (2:13-3:7) is about Christian witness in a hostile world. The home is where the gospel is either made attractive or slandered.
- Christ is the pattern for both (2:21-25): the wife entrusts herself to God, and the husband lays down his strength for her.
- Sarah's laugh in Gen 18 shows grace. God remembers her faith, not her failures. Couples who have failed each other can still be "her children."
- Mixed marriages (believer and unbeliever): 1 Cor 7:12-16 alongside 1 Pet 3:1. Stay, live faithfully, pray, and wait.
