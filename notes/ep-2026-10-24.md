# Lamentations 1: research

## Setting and background
- Book: five poems. Chapters 1, 2 and 4 are 22-verse acrostics, one letter per stanza. Chapter 3 is a triple acrostic of 66 verses. Chapter 5 has 22 lines but no acrostic. Chapter 1 stanzas are mostly three lines, except 1:7, which has four (JFB). Chapter 1 keeps the normal ayin-before-pe order. Chapters 2-4 put pe before ayin, and Henry passes on Lightfoot's conjecture that the ayin (numeral 70) was displaced to recall the seventy years. The poems use the qinah, or "limping," dirge rhythm, a long line followed by a shorter one (general knowledge, not quoted).
- Title: eikah, "How!", the dirge opener (Isaiah 1:21; also 2:1 and 4:1). The Jewish title is Qinot, "lamentations"; the LXX has Threnoi (Calvin, Henry, Cambridge). It is read on Tisha B'Av, the ninth of Av (JFB).
- Author: Jeremiah by tradition. The LXX prefatory sentence says Jeremiah sat weeping after the captivity (Benson quotes it). Henry calls him "Jeremiah the prophet, who is here Jeremiah the poet." Ligonier: there are "few good reasons" to doubt it, though the book nowhere names its author. Henry and Calvin reject the rabbinic and Jerome view that this is the Josiah elegy of 2 Chronicles 35:25. Calvin: "Grossly mistaken was Jerome." (Josephus and Jerome thought the Josiah elegies were in this collection, per JFB.) In the Hebrew canon the book sits in the Writings. In the Greek and English order it follows Jeremiah, and Origen's canon list (Eusebius 6.25) counts "Jeremiah, with Lamentations and the epistle in one."
- Date: after the burning of the temple in Av 586 B.C. (2 Kings 25:8-9; Jeremiah 52:12-13). Calvin argues it was written after the event, not as prophecy. Henry dates the section 588 B.C.
- Bridge from Jeremiah 52: Clarke's old manuscript Bible goes straight from 52:34 into the LXX-style preface and then, "in red letters: Here beginneth the Lamentation of Jeremye ... Aleph: How sitteth aloon the city" (Clarke on Jeremiah 52:34, https://biblehub.com/commentaries/clarke/jeremiah/52.htm).
- Archaeology: in 2019 the Mount Zion excavation (Gibson, UNC Charlotte) found an ash layer with Scythian-type arrowheads, lamps, and a gold-and-silver earring or tassel with a grape cluster. Gibson: "Nobody abandons golden jewelry and nobody has arrowheads in their domestic refuse." He speculated it was one of the great houses of 2 Kings 25:9. https://inside.charlotte.edu/news-features/2019-08-12/evidence-587586-bce-babylonian-conquest-jerusalem-found-mount-zion/
- Later echo: the Judaea Capta coins of Vespasian and Titus show a woman sitting mourning under a palm (Barnes, Benson, Gill, JFB, Ellicott).
- Fathers check: Origen wrote five volumes on Lamentations at Alexandria (Eusebius, Church History 6.24.2: "those on the first twenty-five Psalms and on Lamentations. Of these last five volumes have reached us." https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250106.htm). They survive only in fragments, with no public-domain English found. Theodoret's commentary exists in a copyrighted translation. No usable patristic quotation on chapter 1 was found. On air, mention only that Origen wrote on it.

## Structure and outline
- 1:1-11: the narrator (prophet) describes Zion in the third person. Two breaks into her own voice: 1:9c "O Lord, behold my affliction" and 1:11c "Look, O Lord, and see, for I am despised."
- 1:12-22: Zion speaks in the first person ("Is it nothing to you"), with one narrator interruption at 1:17. Keil and Delitzsch call it a pause, "as if the voice of the weeping one were choked with tears."
- Refrain "none to comfort" (menachem, comforter): 1:2, 1:9, 1:16, 1:17, 1:21. That is five times. (Hebrew root n-ch-m, comfort.)
- Key verses: 1:1 widow and princess turned slave; 1:5 "the Lord has afflicted her for the multitude of her transgressions"; 1:8 "Jerusalem sinned grievously"; 1:10 nations entering the sanctuary "whom you forbade" (Deuteronomy 23:3, Ammonite and Moabite; compare 2 Kings 24:2, where Moab and Ammon raided Judah, and the oracles of Jeremiah 48-49); 1:12; 1:14 the yoke of transgressions (recalls Jeremiah 27-28); 1:15 winepress; 1:16 "my eyes flow with tears" (compare Jeremiah 9:1; 13:17; 14:17); 1:18 "The Lord is in the right" (tsaddiq hu Yahweh; compare Jeremiah 12:1); 1:19 lovers deceived (Jeremiah 30:14); 1:21-22 let them be as I am.
- Echoes of Deuteronomy 28: "head" and "tail" (1:5; Deuteronomy 28:13, 44, cited by Calvin); "she finds no resting place" (1:3; Deuteronomy 28:65 "no resting place for the sole of your foot").
- Answer later in the canon: Isaiah 40:1 "Comfort, comfort my people"; 49:14 "But Zion said, The Lord has forsaken me"; 51:12 "I, I am he who comforts you"; 51:19 "who will comfort you?"; Matthew 5:4 "Blessed are those who mourn, for they shall be comforted."

## Interpretive questions
1. Is 1:12 about Christ?
   - Calvin reads it of Jerusalem only. He discusses whether it is a question or a wish, and notes that Jerome rendered "gleaned."
   - Henry (Concise): "far more may be learned from the sufferings of Christ." JFB: "She here prefigures Christ."
   - Spurgeon preached two sermons on the verse applied to the cross, and in No. 1620 he says explicitly that it is "not as directly meaning what we shall say upon it, but as suggesting an adaptation." In No. 3360 he says that if the words are put "into the mouth of Jesus ... then there is no hyperbole."
   - The New Testament never quotes it.
   - Host: read it first as Jerusalem's own cry. It is an adaptation by analogy, which is legitimate if we know we are doing it. This is NT move 1.
2. How can grief and confession go together? Is this a pagan dirge? Calvin contrasts Virgil's Troy and "cruel Jupiter" with 1:18. The faithful "confess that God is just" and still pray. Ligonier: "godly grief over the fate of a people even when that fate is deserved."
3. Is the prayer for vengeance in 1:21-22 legitimate? Calvin gives his qualifications: the enemies must be God's enemies, the cause must not be our own, and there must be zeal for God's glory. Ligonier connects it to Leviticus 26:44-45. Do not repeat yesterday's vengeance discussion from Jeremiah 51:35 at length; touch it lightly.
4. Who is speaking? The prophet and Zion personified; the change of voice.

## 1. Setting and structure

- **Where we are.** Chapter 6 carries on the dialogue that began in 5:9 ("What is your beloved more than another beloved?"). It closes the night-seeking episode of 5:2-8: she was slow to open the door, he withdrew, she searched, the watchmen beat her, and she gave her great praise of him (the wasf of 5:10-16). Now the daughters, won over by that praise, ask to seek him *with* her (6:1).
- **Three speakers, three parts.** Durham divides the chapter by speaker: the daughters ask (v.1), the bride answers (vv.2-3), and the bridegroom "comes in" (v.4 onward). Henry's chapter summary has the same outline.
- **The answer is surprising.** He has not gone far away. "My beloved has gone down to his garden" (6:2). In 4:12-5:1 the garden is the bride herself, so the reader learns he was never really lost to her. Barnes: "the loss and seeking are not to be taken too seriously" (song-bh-6-3.txt).
- **His praise comes back (6:4-10).** He opens with two cities (Tirzah and Jerusalem) and an army image, then gives a shortened repeat of the wasf of 4:1-3 (hair, teeth, temples, 6:5b-7). Next comes a uniqueness formula set against the harem (6:8-9) and the women's acclamation (6:10). The phrase "awesome as an army with banners" brackets 6:4 and 6:10.
- **Coda (6:11-13).** The nut orchard (the speaker is disputed), the very obscure 6:12, and the call "Return, O Shulammite" with the "dance before two armies" (Mahanaim). In Hebrew Bibles 6:13 is numbered 7:1, so it opens the next wasf (7:1-9).

## 2. Outline

1. **Seeking together (6:1).** The daughters move from "what is he?" (5:9) to "where is he? we'll seek him with you." Sibbes: "Grace begets grace." Durham: "they fay not, where is our Beloved", meaning beginners admire Christ before they dare claim him.
2. **Not far off (6:2-3).** He is in his garden, grazing, gathering lilies. Then comes the covenant formula, with its order reversed from 2:16.
3. **Praise renewed (6:4-7).** Tirzah and Jerusalem: one city whose name means "pleasing," and the city of the great King. "Awesome as an army with banners." "Turn away your eyes, for they overwhelm me." Then the 4:1-3 lines repeated word for word.
4. **The only one (6:8-9).** Sixty queens, eighty concubines, maidens without number, and "my dove, my perfect one, is the only one." The women themselves bless her.
5. **Dawn, moon, sun, bannered host (6:10).** The women's praise. The poetic words for sun and moon are used here.
6. **The orchard, the chariots, the dance (6:11-13).** A notorious crux (6:12). Shulammite. Mahanaim.

## 3. Interpretive questions, with voices

### Q1. Why does 6:3 reverse the order of 2:16?
2:16 says "My beloved is mine, and I am his." 6:3 says "I am my beloved's and my beloved is mine." 7:10 then has only "I am my beloved's, and his desire is for me." Many readers see a progression here, from her claim on him to his claim on her.
- **Hudson Taylor** states the progression most clearly: "Then her first thought of Christ was of her claim upon Him: His claim upon her was secondary. Now she thinks first of His claim; and only afterwards mentions her own." He then points ahead to 7:10.
- **Durham** reads the reversal as a lesson in assurance. She argues *from* her adherence to him *to* his being hers: "and from her betaking her felf to him, and adhering to the bargain, fhe concludes he alfo is hers." He adds that this way of reasoning holds "even tho' fenfe would fay the contrary."
- **Matthew Henry** (full commentary): "now she inverts the order." And: "the covenant never breaks on his side."
- **JFB** reads it the other way round: after the darkness she now "grounds her convictions on His love towards her, more than on hers towards Him."
- **Sibbes** does not dwell on the order. He stresses mutuality ("mutual propriety ... mutual love ... mutual familiarity ...") and grounds it all in union: "Union is the foundation of communion."
- **Plain sense.** Married love says both "you belong to me" and "I belong to you." After a quarrel, starting with "I am yours" is the humbler and more mature thing to say. The shift fits a marriage that has come through a failure.

### Q2. Why does he repeat 4:1-3 word for word (6:5b-7)?
The commentators agree that the repetition is deliberate. After her failure in 5:2-6 he does not withdraw his praise. He says the same words again.
- **Durham:** she might think that, after her "foul flip" (foul slip), his promises no longer applied to her, so "he will not only commend her, but in the fame very words, to fhew that fhe was the fame to him, and that his refpett was not diminifhed to her."
- **Henry:** "not because he could not have described it in other words ... but to show that he had still the same esteem for her since her unkindness to him."
- **Poole:** the repetition is "an effect and testimony of vehement affection," and it shows "the church's miscarriages ... had not made him change his opinion of her."
- **Keil-Delitzsch (plain sense):** "Shulamith appears to Solomon yet more beautiful than on the day when she was brought to him as his bride. His love is still the same, unchanged."
- **Sibbes:** "He will have the last word." **Taylor:** "with no upbraiding word, but in tenderest love."
- **Application.** In marriage, renewed praise after a rupture is reconciliation spoken out loud. For Christ and the church, his estimate of his bride does not swing with her lapses (Eph 5:25-27).

### Q3. "Only one" amid sixty queens and eighty concubines (6:8-9): what about Solomon's polygamy?
- **Plain sense.** The verse sets one beloved against a harem and praises the one above the many. **Ellicott:** "It is impossible not to see in this a eulogy on monogamy." He adds that the queens and concubines themselves give "an unconscious testimony to the superiority of monogamy." **Barnes:** "This passage is invaluable as a divine witness to the principle of monogamy under the Old Testament and in the luxurious age of Solomon."
- **The historical problem.** 1 Kings 11:3 lists 700 wives and 300 concubines. Keil-Delitzsch (holding Solomonic authorship) infer that the lower numbers point to "the commencement of his reign." Cambridge and Pulpit make similar suggestions. JFB says the one bride "is contrasted with the many wives whom Eastern kings had in violation of the marriage law (1Ki 11:1-3)."
- **Christ and the church, with restraint.** The church is one bride (Eph 4:4-5). Gregory of Nyssa reads "one is my dove" through John 17: "all are united to one another in being joined to the life of the one Good." Henry: "in that one I have more than they have in all theirs."
- **Caution.** Do not allegorize the queens and concubines into specific groups. Gill and Poole try to (as nations or false churches, for example). The Pulpit Commentary is wiser here: "There is no necessity to seek for any exact interpretation of the queens and concubines."

### Other points to have in hand
- **Tirzah.** Northern capital before Omri built Samaria (1 Kgs 15:21, 33; 16:6-23). The name means "pleasing/delight." The LXX renders it *eudokia* ("good pleasure"; Ellicott: "The LXX. translate Tirzah by εὐδοκία , Vulg. suavis"). Gregory of Nyssa follows the LXX ("like good pleasure") and links it to Luke 2:14, "goodwill toward men." Pairing Tirzah with Jerusalem sets the two great royal cities side by side. Ellicott: "both were capitals, the one of the northern, the other of the southern kingdom."
- **Nidgalot ("bannered").** This picks up *dagul* in 5:10 ("distinguished/bannered among ten thousand"). Cambridge glosses it as "‘beflagged things,’" a niphal from *degel*, "banner." Note: the Cambridge text on biblehub prints "Cp. dâghûl , ch. Song of Solomon 6:10" where 5:10 is meant. As she raised his banner above ten thousand, so he now calls her bannered (JFB on 6:9: "As she exalted Him above all others (So 5:10), so He now her"). Spurgeon: "I believe the great banner of the Christian church to be the uplifted Savior."
- **"They overwhelm me" (6:5).** Hiphil of *rahab*. Durham reads her eyes as her faith: "The believer hath great weight with Chrift ... as faith is the only weapon." Compare Jacob in Genesis 32 and the Canaanite woman in Matthew 15 (Sibbes cites both on 6:3).
- **6:10, dawn, moon, sun.** The words are *shachar*, *lebanah* ("white one") and *chammah* ("heat"), poetic terms also paired in Isaiah 24:23 and 30:26 (Cambridge). The fathers and the Reformed tradition read the verse of the church. Henry (Concise) and JFB use moon = sanctification (borrowed light) and sun = justification. Poole: "the church, like the moon, may have her eclipse." Plain sense: Ellicott compares Romeo and Juliet ("It is the East, and Juliet is the sun!").
- **6:12.** Cambridge: "probably the most difficult verse in the whole book to interpret satisfactorily." The options: (a) Ammi-nadib as a proper name for a famous charioteer (KJV, Poole's first option); (b) "chariots of my willing/noble people" (Poole, Durham, Taylor; Psalm 110:3); (c) the text may be corrupt (Cambridge). Say on air that it is uncertain.
- **Shulammite.** This is its only occurrence, and it carries the article, so it is a gentilic, not a personal name (Delitzsch, via Pulpit: "It cannot be a pure proper name, says Delitzsch, because the article is attached to it"). Two derivations: (a) from Shunem/Shulem in Issachar (Barnes, Cambridge, Pulpit, Keil-Delitzsch; compare Abishag the Shunammite, 1 Kings 1:3); (b) the feminine of Shelomoh, "the Solomon-ess" (JFB: "Feminine of Solomon, Prince of Peace"; Taylor; Henry gives both).
- **Mahanaim.** "Two camps" (Genesis 32:1-2), where Jacob met God's angels. Either a known dance (Barnes, Cambridge) or an allusion to the angelic hosts (Durham: "fhe is an angelick fight"; Henry; JFB).

## 1. Setting

**Where it sits.** John 6 has a clear arc: the sign (feeding of the 5,000, 6:1-15), the walking on the sea (6:16-21), the crowd's pursuit (6:22-25), and the Bread of Life discourse (6:26-58), which John locates in "the synagogue, as he taught at Capernaum" (6:59). Verses 60-71 are the aftermath: the discourse sifts its hearers. The grumbling that began with "the Jews" (6:41, 52) now spreads to "many of his disciples" (6:60-61). The chapter that opened with a crowd of thousands wanting to make him king (6:15) ends with Twelve, and one of those a devil.

**Turning point.** In John's story this is the high-water mark and then the ebb of the Galilean ministry. After ch. 6 Jesus "would not go about in Judea, because the Jews were seeking to kill him" (7:1), and the action moves toward Jerusalem and the cross. Here John puts in story form his prologue's theme: "He came to his own, and his own people did not receive him. But to all who did receive him... who were born, not of blood nor of the will of the flesh... but of God" (1:11-13). Verse 63 ("the flesh is no help at all") and 6:65 ("granted him by the Father") echo 1:13 and 3:6 ("that which is born of the flesh is flesh"). Peter's confession here is John's counterpart to the Synoptic Caesarea Philippi scene (Matt 16:16; Mark 8:29), and as there, it is followed straight away by a dark word (Matt 16:23 "Get behind me, Satan" // John 6:70 "one of you is a devil").

**Greek notes.**
- **6:60 *sklēros* ("hard")** means harsh, rough, or offensive, not "hard to understand." (Cf. Matt 25:24, the "hard man"; Jude 15, "harsh things"; Acts 26:14, "it is hard for you to kick against the goads.") The problem is not that the saying is obscure. It is unacceptable. "Who can listen to it (*akouein*)?" means "who can stomach / obey it?" Chrysostom: "difficult to be received, transcending their infirmity." Gill points out that the Hebrew/Aramaic idiom "a hard saying" was used for something that seems absurd or objectionable.
- **6:61 *skandalizei*** asks "Does this cause you to stumble / take offense?" This is the language of the "stone of stumbling" (Isa 8:14; 1 Pet 2:8). Calvin applies Isa 8:14 directly to 6:66.
- **6:61 *gongyzousin* ("grumbling")** repeats 6:41, 43 and recalls Israel murmuring over manna in the wilderness (Exod 16; Num 11), the backdrop of the whole chapter.
- **6:62** is an unfinished conditional (aposiopesis): "Then what if you were to see the Son of Man ascending (*anabainonta*) to where he was before...?" with no apodosis. It can be read two ways. (a) It will be even more offensive: the lowly man returns to glory, and the cross is part of that "lifting up" in John. (b) It will resolve the offense: once his body ascends whole into heaven, they will see he was not talking about cannibalism (Augustine, Gill).
- **6:63 "the flesh (*sarx*) is no help at all (*ouk ōphelei ouden*)."** The contrast is Spirit/flesh as in 3:6. Note *ta rhēmata* ("the words") "are spirit and life." Life comes through Christ's words received by the Spirit, not through eating as such.
- **6:64 *ex archēs*** ("from the beginning"): Jesus' foreknowledge of unbelief and of the betrayer.
- **6:65 *dedomenon*** ("granted / given"), a perfect passive participle in a periphrastic construction, picks up 6:37, 39 ("all that the Father gives me") and 6:44 ("unless the Father... draws him"). Calvin notes that "give" here replaces "draw."
- **6:66 *ek toutou*** can be temporal ("from that time," KJV) or causal ("because of this," i.e., as a result of the saying; ESV "After this"; NASB "As a result of this"). Both are probably in view. *apēlthon eis ta opisō* means "went away back" (cf. Augustine's "went back... after Satan," citing 1 Tim 5:15) and "no longer walked (*periepatoun*) with him."
- **6:67** begins with *mē*: "You do not want to go away too, do you?" The form expects a "no" answer, a gentle question and not an accusation (Chrysostom).
- **6:68** "Lord, to whom shall we go? You have *rhēmata zōēs aiōniou*." These are the same "words" as 6:63. Peter has heard 6:63 rightly.
- **6:69 Text variant.** The oldest manuscripts (P75, Aleph, B, C*, D, etc.; NA28/ESV) read ***ho hagios tou theou*, "the Holy One of God."** The Byzantine/Textus Receptus (KJV) reads "that Christ, the Son of the living God" (*ho Christos ho huios tou theou tou zōntos*). That reading is widely regarded as scribal harmonization to Matt 16:16. Calvin, Chrysostom, Gill, Ryle and Spurgeon all comment on the TR form. Only demons use "Holy One of God" elsewhere in the Gospels (Mark 1:24; Luke 4:34), and Isaiah's "Holy One of Israel" lies behind it (MacArthur draws this connection). Perfect tenses: *pepisteukamen kai egnōkamen*, "we have believed and have come to know." Augustine stresses the order, believing before knowing.
- **6:70 *diabolos*** means "slanderer, adversary, devil." John alone among the evangelists calls Judas *diabolos*. Compare 13:2 ("the devil had already put it into the heart of Judas") and 13:27 ("Satan entered into him"). "Did I not choose (*exelexamēn*) you, the Twelve?" Calvin and Gill: this is choice to the apostolic office, not the eternal election of 6:37-44.
- **6:71 "Judas the son of Simon Iscariot."** In the best text "Iscariot" goes with Simon (the father) as well (cf. 13:26), which suggests a place name. The most common derivation is Hebrew *ish Qeriyyot*, "man of Kerioth" (a town in southern Judea, Josh 15:25). If so, Judas was likely the only non-Galilean among the Twelve. Other proposals ("assassin" from *sicarius*; "false one" from Aramaic *sheqar*) are less likely. "He was going to betray (*emellen paradidonai*) him" uses *paradidōmi*, the same verb used for the Father "giving up" the Son (Rom 8:32).

## 2. Outline

**I. The Offense of the Hard Saying (6:60-61a)**
- "This is a hard saying; who can listen to it?" (6:60). Grumbling spreads from "the Jews" to "disciples."

**II. Jesus' Answer to the Offended (6:61b-65)**
- A. The offense will grow or be resolved at the ascension (6:61b-62): "What if you were to see the Son of Man ascending to where he was before?"
- B. The Spirit, not the flesh, gives life (6:63): "It is the Spirit who gives life; the flesh is no help at all. The words that I have spoken to you are spirit and life."
- C. The root problem is unbelief, known to Jesus from the beginning (6:64).
- D. The deeper cause is sovereign grace (6:65): "no one can come to me unless it is granted him by the Father."

**III. The Great Defection (6:66)**
- "After this many of his disciples turned back and no longer walked with him."

**IV. The Question and the Confession (6:67-69)**
- A. Jesus' searching question: "Do you want to go away as well?" (6:67)
- B. Peter's answer, nowhere else to go: "Lord, to whom shall we go?" (6:68a)
- C. The reason, Christ's words: "You have the words of eternal life" (6:68b)
- D. The confession: "we have believed, and have come to know, that you are the Holy One of God" (6:69)

**V. The Devil Among the Twelve (6:70-71)**
- "Did I not choose you, the Twelve? And yet one of you is a devil." John's note: Judas, son of Simon Iscariot, "one of the Twelve, was going to betray him."

## 3. Interpretive Questions

### Q1. What does "the flesh is no help at all" (6:63) mean? Does it rule out a carnal or physical eating of Christ's flesh?

The verse seems to contradict 6:53-56 ("unless you eat the flesh..."). There are three main answers in the sources:

- **Chrysostom:** "flesh" means *carnal hearing*, not Christ's own flesh: "He speaks not of His own flesh, (God forbid!) but of those who received His words in a carnal manner."
- **Augustine:** "flesh" is Christ's flesh *taken alone, without the Spirit* (as "knowledge puffs up" means knowledge without love): "the flesh profits nothing, only when alone. Let the Spirit be added to the flesh... and it profits very much." The Capernaites pictured flesh "sold in the shambles." Christ's grace "is not consumed by tooth-biting."
- **Cyril of Alexandria:** close to Augustine but more Christological. Flesh by its own nature cannot give life, but *this* flesh is united to the life-giving Word: "as to Christ alone it holdeth not."
- **Calvin** expressly rejects Chrysostom's reading ("improperly, in my opinion") and sides with Augustine's "alone, and by itself." Christ's flesh is real food, but "if it be estimated by its origin and nature" it does not give life. Its power to quicken is from the Spirit, and we receive it by "the spiritual mouth of faith." This matches the Reformed (WCF 29.7) view of the Supper: Christ is truly but spiritually received, by faith. Calvin also stresses that 6:63b makes the "words" the vehicle: the word "calls us upwards to seek Christ in his heavenly glory."
- **Ryle** applies it bluntly against ritualism: "neither His flesh nor any other flesh, literally eaten, can do good to the soul. Spiritual benefit is not to be had through the mouth, but through the heart."
- **Har Reeder (PCA)** reads "flesh" anthropologically as fallen human nature, Rom 3:10-18 ("We are dead in our sins").

*For the podcast:* All agree that physical eating as such gives no life (against the Capernaites' crude literalism and against any *ex opere operato* sacramentalism). They differ on whether "flesh" means (a) a carnal way of hearing, (b) Christ's flesh apart from the Spirit, or (c) fallen human nature. The Reformed tradition (Calvin) combines (b) with the stress on words and faith.

### Q2. What does the unfinished question about the Son of Man "ascending" (6:62) imply?

- **Augustine:** it *removes* the offense. If they see him ascend "whole" into heaven, "certainly then, at least, you will understand that His grace is not consumed by tooth-biting." He also uses it to teach the unity of Christ's person (the Son of Man "was" in heaven by communication of properties).
- **Chrysostom:** not "difficulties to difficulties" but a way to lead them away from thinking of him as Joseph's son: "one that was persuaded that He had come down from heaven, and would ascend there, might more easily give heed to His words."
- **Calvin:** the ascension is "the completion of that glory." The two natures form one person, so "it is not an unusual way of speaking to transfer to one nature what is peculiar to the other."
- **Gill** combines both readings. The ascension "would prove that he came down from heaven... and that his flesh and blood were not to be eaten in a corporeal sense," yet a lowly man ascending would also seem "much more" incredible to them.

### Q3. Sovereignty and responsibility (6:64-65) and why Jesus chose Judas (6:70-71)

- **6:64-65.** Jesus names both unbelief (responsibility, 6:64) and the Father's gift (sovereignty, 6:65), side by side.
  - Augustine: "Hence to believe is also given to us... What do you have that you did not receive?"
  - Calvin: "faith proceeds only from the secret revelation of the Spirit". At the same time 6:64 "imputes blame to them."
  - Chrysostom is the outlier: "imagine not merely an arbitrary distribution, but that if any has rendered himself worthy to receive the gift, he has received it." This is a good spot to contrast Greek patristic synergism with Augustine and Calvin (WCF 10.2, effectual calling "not from anything at all foreseen in man").
  - MacArthur: Jesus "rests in the fact that divine sovereignty is the final decider," yet 6:67 is "a brokenhearted comment" in which Jesus "grieved over unbelief, even in the face of divine sovereignty."
- **Why choose Judas?**
  - Augustine: God "makes a good use of the evil works of wicked men... All this evil was Judas's; but the Lord employed his evil for good. He endured to be betrayed, to redeem us." (WCF 5.4: God's providence over sin, yet He is not its author.)
  - Calvin: "chosen" means to the *apostolic office*, not eternal election: "it is impossible that any one of those who have been predestinated to life shall fall away." Gill agrees.
  - Chrysostom: Jesus warned Judas "to check the wickedness of the traitor, knowing that He should avail nothing, yet doing His own part." He did not expose him by name but would not let him be hidden either.
  - Ryle: the lesson is that "the possession of religious privileges alone is not enough to save our souls."
- **6:66 and perseverance.**
  - Ryle: apostasy is from "counterfeit grace," since "The true grace of God no doubt is an everlasting possession."
  - Calvin: "we cannot go back a foot breadth, without being immediately in danger."
  - Spurgeon holds perseverance and self-examination together: perseverance "is a result of grace" and also "one of the most certain signs of it" (WCF 17).

## 1. Setting

**The argument of the letter.** Peter writes to "elect exiles of the dispersion" in five Roman provinces of Asia Minor: Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia, and Bithynia (1:1). The churches were mostly Gentile converts. 4:3 says they "spent" past time doing "what the Gentiles want to do," and that only makes sense for former pagans. Calvin assumed Jewish readers and has to explain 4:3 away. The letter's two themes run together throughout: (a) holy, distinct living as God's people and "sojourners" (1:13-2:12), and (b) patient, innocent suffering on the pattern of Christ (2:18-25; 3:13-4:19). Leighton calls these "the main of a Christian's duty," which is "patience in suffering, and avoidance of sin" and says the apostle "slides" from one into the other.

**Date and pressure.** The traditional setting is Rome ("Babylon," 5:13) in the early 60s, near Nero's persecution (AD 64) and before Peter's martyrdom. In 4:1-11 the suffering is mostly social: estrangement, slander, and being "maligned" (4:4) by former friends. It is not yet clearly state persecution. The "fiery trial" of 4:12 is just ahead.

**4:1 resumes 3:18.** "Since therefore (*oun*) Christ suffered in the flesh" picks up 3:18 ("Christ also suffered once for sins... being put to death in the flesh"). 3:19-22 (the spirits in prison, Noah, baptism) is an excursus that ends with Christ's exaltation. Calvin's editor (n. 44) describes 3:19 as a digression after which "the Apostle returns here to his former subject." Leighton: "that of patience, ch. iii. v. 18, that of holiness, here." Christ's suffering is first the ransom ("it was to us more than an example ; it was our ransom," Leighton) and then the pattern.

**Structure of the unit.** 4:1-6 is the call to break with the old pagan life, set against the coming judgment of "the living and the dead." 4:7-11 tells the church how to live because "the end of all things is at hand": prayer, love, hospitality, and gifts. It ends in a doxology (4:11), which closes the main body of the letter. 4:12 starts again with "Beloved."

**Greek notes.**
- **4:1 *hoplisasthe*** ("arm yourselves") is an aorist middle imperative of *hoplizō* (cf. *hopla*, "weapons," Rom 6:13; 13:12). It is military language: put on the mindset as armour. Calvin: we "are really and effectually supplied with invincible weapons to subdue the flesh." Leighton: "There is still fighting, and sin will be molesting you".
- **4:1 *ennoia*** ("way of thinking," KJV "mind") means an intention, resolve, or settled insight. In the NT it occurs only here and Heb 4:12. The weapon is a conviction: Christ's suffering in the flesh means a break with sin.
- **4:1 *pepautai hamartias*** ("has ceased from sin") is a perfect middle/passive of *pauō*. The genitive is one of separation: he has been made to stop and is now done with sin. On the meaning, see Q1.
- **4:2 *epithymiais anthrōpōn* vs. *thelēmati theou***, "human passions" against "the will of God." This contrast governs the "rest of the time in the flesh."
- **4:3** gives a vice list: *aselgeiai* (sensuality), *epithymiai* (passions), *oinophlygiai* (drunkenness, literally "wine-bubblings"), *kōmoi* (orgies or revels, the drunken processions of pagan festivals), *potoi* (drinking parties), and *athemitoi eidōlolatriai* ("lawless idolatries"). The list describes pagan civic and guild life, where religion, banqueting, and sex went together.
- **4:4 *xenizontai*** ("they are surprised," literally "they find it strange or foreign"). It is the same root as *xenos* ("stranger"). The world finds the Christian "foreign," but Christians are already "sojourners and exiles" (2:11). The verb returns in 4:12 ("do not be surprised at the fiery trial").
- **4:4 *syntrechontōn*** means "running together with them," like a crowd stampeding.
- **4:4 *anachysis*** is a hapax in the NT. It means a pouring out or overflow, a place where water spreads out, a flood. ESV renders it "flood of debauchery," and KJV has "excess of riot." *Asōtia* (debauchery, dissipation) is the word of the prodigal's "reckless living" (Luke 15:13).
- **4:4 *blasphēmountes*** ("maligning you") means slandering, and also blaspheming. Henry notes they "speak evil of their persons, of their way, their religion, and their God."
- **4:5 *tō hetoimōs echonti krinai*** means "to him who is ready to judge." "The living and the dead" is creedal language (Acts 10:42; 2 Tim 4:1; the Apostles' Creed).
- **4:6 *nekrois euēngelisthē*** means "to the dead also the good news was preached." There is no verb "are" in the Greek (Poole). It is an aorist passive. See Q2.
- **4:7 *ēngiken*** ("is at hand," a perfect of *engizō*) is the verb of Jesus' "the kingdom of God is at hand" (Mark 1:15). *Telos* means the goal and consummation, not just termination.
- **4:7 *sōphronēsate*** ("be self-controlled," literally "be of sound mind") is the *sōphrosynē* word group: sanity, balanced judgment, the opposite of the Gentiles' frenzy in 4:3-4 (cf. Mark 5:15, the demoniac "in his right mind").
- **4:7 *nēpsate*** ("be sober-minded," literally "be sober, not drunk") appears also at 1:13 and 5:8 ("Be sober-minded; be watchful"). It is the opposite of *oinophlygiai* in 4:3. Both aorist imperatives have *eis proseuchas*, "for the sake of [your] prayers." Calvin and Leighton both read sobriety as chiefly of the mind. Leighton: "spiritual temperance of a Christian mind in all earthly things."
- **4:8 *pro pantōn*** ("above all").
- **4:8 *ektenē*** ("earnest," KJV "fervent") comes from *ekteinō*, "to stretch out." Love is to be at full stretch, sustained, unflagging. Compare 1:22 (*ektenōs*, "love one another earnestly from a pure heart"), Luke 22:44 (Jesus praying "more earnestly"), and Acts 12:5 (the church's "earnest" prayer). Ellicott: Peter "assumes that the charity is there, but insists on its not being suffered to flag." Barnes: "means properly extended; then intent, earnest, fervent." Leighton: "a high bent, or strain of it, that which acts strongly and carries far."
- **4:8 *agapē kalyptei plēthos hamartiōn*** ("love covers a multitude of sins") follows the Hebrew of Prov 10:12 (*ve'al kol-peshaim tekhasseh ahavah*). The LXX of that verse is quite different. James 5:20 has the same phrase, so it was probably a current proverb (Ellicott, Cambridge, Meyer). See Q3.
- **4:9 *philoxenoi*** ("hospitable") is literally "lovers of strangers" (*philos* + *xenos*). It plays on 4:4: the world finds them *xenoi* (strange), but they are to love *xenoi*. "Without grumbling" (*aneu gongysmou*) uses the murmuring word of Israel in the wilderness.
- **4:10 *charisma*** is a gift of grace (*charis*). Peter uses it of the whole range of ministries, both "speaking" and "serving" (4:11), and not only of the miraculous. "As each has received" means every believer has one.
- **4:10 *oikonomoi*** ("stewards") are household managers, usually slaves, who administer the master's goods (Luke 12:42; 16:1-8; 1 Cor 4:1-2). Calvin: "we do not give our own, but only dispense what God has committed to us."
- **4:10 *poikilēs charitos theou*** is "the varied (manifold, many-colored) grace of God." *Poikilos* means multicolored or variegated, like embroidery or a mottled coat. It is the same word as 1:6, "various trials" (*poikilois peirasmois*): God's grace is as many-colored as the trials. (The 1:6 link is a standard observation, not quoted from a raw file here.) Ellicott: "the beautiful word rendered “manifold” brings out the subtle and picturesque variety with which God arranges and distributes His bounty."
- **4:11 *hōs logia theou*** means "as oracles of God." *Logia* means divine utterances. The term is used of the OT Scriptures in Rom 3:2 ("entrusted with the oracles of God"), Acts 7:38, and Heb 5:12. There is no article (Ellicott). The speaker is to speak as one delivering God's own words, not his own (Calvin: "as from hand to hand, the doctrine received from God").
- **4:11 *ex ischyos hēs chorēgei ho theos*** means "by the strength that God supplies." *Chorēgeō* first meant to fund a chorus, that is, to pay for a public performance lavishly, and then to supply or furnish generously (cf. 2 Cor 9:10; 2 Pet 1:5, 11 *epichorēgeō*). God is the patron who underwrites the whole production.
- **4:11 doxology**: *hina en pasin doxazētai ho theos dia Iēsou Christou, hō estin hē doxa kai to kratos...*. Commentators differ over whether "to whom" refers to God (Calvin: "the context requires that it should be rather applied to God") or to Jesus Christ, the nearer antecedent (Henry: the doxology "prove[s] that Jesus Christ is the most high God").

## 3. Interpretive Questions

### Q1. What does "whoever has suffered in the flesh has ceased from sin" (4:1) mean?

The main readings in the sources:

1. **The believer's death to sin by union with Christ (Rom 6:7).** This is the majority Reformed reading. Calvin takes the clause as "an indefinite sentence, which generally extends to all the godly." He links it to "He who is dead is justified or freed from sin" in Rom 6:7, and says "flesh" shifts meaning: in Christ's case it is his human nature, in ours "the corruption, and the sinfulness of our nature; and thus suffering in the flesh signifies the denying of ourselves." Leighton puts the stress on the ongoing struggle: "it is plainly this, ceasing from sin... It is the suffering and the dying of our corruption, the taking away of the life of sin by the death of Christ." He insists the death of sin is real but gradual: "this figurative death, this mortification of the flesh in a Christian, is gradual." Henry holds the same view ("the beginning of all true mortification lies in the mind"), and so does Spurgeon in "Luminous Words": "every believer ought to regard Himself as having been put to death on account of sin—as having undergone, in the person of His great Substitute, the capital sentence."
2. **Christ himself is the one who "ceased from sin."** Erasmus applied the clause to Christ, who by dying was finished with sin's assault (or with the sin-bearing). Calvin rejects this explicitly: Erasmus "has incorrectly, as I think, rendered the word" so as to apply it "to Christ."
3. **Suffering as a well-doer proves one has broken with sin.** Calvin's translator, John Owen, adds a note (n. 44) taking the clause as a parenthesis about Christ's innocence: "For he who suffered ceased from sin," that is, had no sin, but was just. He applies it to believers who suffer "not... for their sins, but for righteousness' sake." Many moderns (e.g., Grudem, Schreiner; not quoted here) read it as: the person who chooses to suffer for Christ rather than sin has decisively renounced sin as a way of life.

**Podcast angle:** All three agree that this is not sinless perfection. Leighton, against perfectionism: "Be not desperate when you lose, nor secure when you gain them." Spurgeon: "in the sight of the all-seeing JEHOVAH, he has his infirmities, his failures, and his sins, but still, as far as his heart is concerned, he has done with it."

### Q2. Who are "the dead" to whom "the gospel was preached" (4:6)?

The prior episode followed Augustine on 3:19: Christ preached "in the spirit" through Noah to his contemporaries, who are now "in prison." So 4:6 needs careful handling. Four options appear in the sources:

1. **The spiritually dead (unbelievers), who heard the gospel and were converted.** Augustine, Letter 164 to Evodius, applies the same method he uses on 3:19. He says there is "no necessity" to take the dead as the physically departed. The gospel was preached "in this life to the dead, that is, to the unbelieving wicked," so that believing they might be judged in the flesh "by means of various afflictions and by the death of the body itself" and live to God in the spirit. Clement of Alexandria (*Adumbrations*) is earlier and reads it the same way: "to us, namely, who were at one time unbelievers." Whitby, Doddridge, and Macknight followed this view (Calvin's editor, n. 45; Barnes). *Weakness:* "dead" in 4:5 ("the living and the dead") is almost certainly literal, and 4:6 picks it up with *kai nekrois*. JFB: the word "must be taken in the same literal sense as in 1Pe 4:5, which refutes the explanation" of "dead" as dead in sins. Gill agrees.
2. **Believers who heard the gospel in life and have since died.** This is the majority Protestant reading: Leighton, Barnes, Gill, Henry (first option), Poole (second option), the Geneva notes, and most moderns (Grudem, Schreiner, Davids). Leighton: "such as had heard and believed the Gospel, when it came to them, and now were dead." The pastoral point is that the earlier Christians who died, some perhaps martyred and all "judged in the flesh the way people are" by dying, have not lost out. They "live in the spirit the way God does." Barnes: "the most natural and obvious interpretation is to refer it to those who were then dead, to whom the gospel had been preached when living." This reading fits the aorist ("was preached," at a past time) and the literal "dead" of 4:5. It also fits Augustine's 3:19 well, because neither text then requires a preaching in Hades.
3. **Calvin's position.** He reads 4:6 as a comfort for the godly, "that death itself brings no loss to their salvation... for his power extends to the dead." He takes "judged in the flesh" as a concession: the dead seem condemned in the outward man, "yet they cease not to live with God." In effect this is close to view 2. He frames it, though, as "the grace of Christ once penetrated to the dead," and he links it back to his reading of 3:19.
4. **Christ's descent preaching to the dead in Hades** (a second-chance or post-mortem proclamation). Ellicott: "No one with an un-preoccupied mind could doubt... that the persons to whom this preaching was made were dead at the time of being preached to," and he ties it to "the purpose of Christ’s descent into hell." Meyer also holds a version of this. Gill names Origen and rejects this reading: "not the dead, whose souls are in hell, for to them, there, the Gospel never was, nor never will be preached." WCF 25.6/LC 86 and the Reformed denial of post-mortem conversion (Heb 9:27; Luke 16:26) rule out a second chance. (A Reformed host can still note that a proclamation of *victory* in 3:19 is not the same as gospel offer in 4:6.)

**Suggested line for the episode:** Present view 2 as the best reading of 4:6, with Augustine's view 1 as an honorable patristic alternative that matches last week's handling of 3:19. Either one keeps 4:6 clear of a descent to preach. Leighton's application is strong: "It is therefore a wise prevention, to have sin judged and put to death in us before we die."

### Q3. Does "love covers a multitude of sins" (4:8) mean love covers *others'* sins, or that love atones for *one's own* sins?

1. **Love covers the sins of others: it forbears, forgives, and does not publicize them.** This is the Reformation and Protestant reading, and it follows Prov 10:12, where "hatred stirs up strife" stands opposite "love covers all offenses." Calvin: "men who love one another, kindly and courteously forgive one another; hence it comes that, willingly burying each other's vices, one seeks to preserve the honor of another." Calvin then turns it into polemic: "It hence appears how absurd are the Papists, who seek to elicit from this passage their own satisfactions." Leighton: love "delights not in the undue disclosing of brethren's failings." He notes that this "bars not the judicial trial of scandalous offences," so church discipline still stands. Henry, Henry's Concise (which says love does not "make amends for the sins of those who exercise it"), Gill ("not a man's own sins, but the sins of others; and not from the sight of God"), Barnes, Poole, the Geneva notes, and Meyer (who lists Estius, Luther, Calvin, Beza) all take this line. So does the Ligonier devotional: "Peter is not telling us that our love for one another provides atonement for sin." JFB argues from grammar, citing Luther, that the "own sins" reading fails, "for then the Greek middle voice would be used".
2. **Love (or almsgiving) covers one's *own* sins before God.** This reading has old roots. *2 Clement* 16 (c. AD 150) puts the proverb in a list of merits: "Good, then, is alms as repentance from sin ; better is fasting than prayer , and alms than both; charity covers a multitude of sins ," Augustine (Homily 1 on 1 John) applies the verse to the believer's own daily sins: "Before all, then, confession; then, love... Charity covers a multitude of sins . ... charity extinguishes sins ." Meyer reports that Oecumenius and "many Catholic expositors (Salmeron, Cornelius a Lapide, Lorinus, etc.)" took the verse "of the blessing which love brings to him who puts it into practice." This was the exegetical basis for the medieval doctrine of works of satisfaction, and it is what Calvin is answering.
3. **Mediating readings.** Some read it like James 5:20: love wins the sinner back, and so God covers that sinner's sins (Grotius, noted by Meyer; JFB allows it as a secondary idea). Others take Matt 6:14-15 as a parallel: the forgiving are forgiven. Henry allows that love "prepares for mercy at the hand of God, who hath promised to forgive those that forgive others," but he never says love *merits* that mercy. Benson (on the bh4-8 page) goes slightly further: "will entitle us, through divine mercy to the expectation of forgiveness."

**Note for a Reformed treatment:** 1 Clement 49 (c. AD 96) quotes the line in a hymn to love ("Love unites us to God . Love covers a multitude of sins") without any merit theology. Augustine's reading is pastoral, about daily sins of believers who already confess, and not a theory of satisfaction. Only the blood of Christ covers sin before God (WCF 11.3; 16.5: we cannot merit pardon of sin by our best works). Love covers sin before *people*: it refuses to expose, retaliate, or keep a ledger. Leighton's sharpest line: "Such never uncover their brother's sores, but to cure them."
