## 1. Background and structure

**Zophar the Naamathite.** He is the third and bluntest friend, and this is his last speech. In the third cycle he does not speak at all. Ellicott (20:27): "We here take leave of Zophar, who does not reply again". F. B. Meyer: "Zophar is the man who least of all understood Job."

**What provokes him.** Job had just ended ch. 19 with a warning to the friends: "be afraid of the sword... that you may know there is a judgment" (19:29).
- Pulpit (20:1-2) says Zophar "has heard the check of his reproach", which is Job's closing warning. "He cannot allow Job to shift the onus of guilt and the menace of punishment on his friends."
- Meyer says the same: "The rebuke which Job had just administered, Job_19:28-29, has vexed him."
- Gregory reads it more kindly. Zophar is stirred "on hearing from his lips the terribleness of the Judgment to come".

**Tone.**
- Pulpit: "even more harsh than his first (ch. 11.). He adds coarseness and rudeness".
- Ellicott: he "retorts with yet greater vehemence than before, and assumes a more ornate and elaborate style".
- Cambridge (20:7): "Zophar is not the most refined of the three".

**Structure** (this matches the outline the brief proposed; the commentators divide the chapter in much the same way):
- **20:2-3, preface.** His thoughts "answer" him; he is stung by Job's rebuke. Keil & Delitzsch read חוּשִׁי as "my feeling" within, not "haste". Pulpit takes it as "because I am of a hasty and impetuous temperament."
- **20:4-11, the wicked man's joy is short.** It is an appeal to tradition "since man was placed on earth" (20:4). Barnes and Poole note that "short" in 20:5 is Hebrew "from near" (מִקָּרוֹב), meaning soon over. "Triumphing" is רְנָנָה (Barnes: shouting after a victory or at harvest).
  - 20:7, "like his own dung" (גֶּלֶל). Keil & Delitzsch defend the literal sense against scholars who emend it to "majesty". They link it to 1 Kings 14:10 and to dung-cakes burned as fuel until they are completely gone.
  - JFB: the term "expresses disgust and the lowest degradation".
  - 20:8, he vanishes like a dream. 20:11, "his bones are full of youthful vigour" (Ellicott and K&D read "youth" or "vigour" rather than "sin of his youth").
- **20:12-23, sweet evil turns to venom.** Cambridge: "12–22. His sin changes into his punishment."
  - The wicked man is pictured as an epicure who rolls a morsel under his tongue. Renan, quoted in K&D: "comme un bonbon qu'on laisse fondre dans la bouche".
  - 20:14, "gall of asps": מְרוֹרַת פְּתָנִים. Barnes says merorah means "bitterness, acridness", and so bile or gall. K&D: "The bitter and poisonous are synonymous in the ancient languages; hence we find the meanings poison and gall (Job 20:25) in מררה."
  - Barnes, Poole, Pulpit and JFB note the ancient belief that a snake's venom sat in its gall. JFB: "Scripture uses popular language, where no moral truth is thereby endangered."
  - 20:15, he swallows riches and vomits them up, and "God shall cast them out". Pulpit: "Whatever is the immediate motive of the restitution: it will really be God's doing."
  - 20:19, the charge of oppressing the poor. Ellicott: "For these insinuations there was not a vestige of ground, but Job formally rebuts them in Job 31".
  - 20:23, God "rains" wrath on him while he eats.
- **20:24-29, the weapons and the heritage.** He flees the iron weapon and the bronze bow strikes him (Ellicott: "if he escaped one mischance, another should overtake him"). There is fire "not fanned", and heaven and earth testify against him (20:27).
  - 20:29: "This is the wicked man's portion from God, the heritage decreed for him by God."
  - Barnes, Poole, Benson and Gill note that "heritage of his word" (נַחֲלַת אִמְרוֹ) means God's decree. Poole and Gill also mention the minority reading "heritage of his [the wicked man's] words", a dig at Job's speeches.

**Zophar ignores, or twists, Job's 19:25 confession.**
- Gill (20:5): Zophar may be aiming at "Job's triumph of faith, Job 19:25". He hints that Job's joy in a living Redeemer "would be soon over".
- Gill (20:1): Zophar answers "notwithstanding the noble confession of his faith he had made".
- Expositor's Bible (Watson): "When Job affirmed his sublime confidence in a Divine Vindicator, Zophar caught only at the idea of an avenger."
- Poole (20:5): "the joy of the hypocrite" is a "reflection upon Job".

**How Job 21 answers.**
- Pulpit (20:5) notes that brief triumph for the wicked was "the traditional belief of the time". Job's experience says otherwise: the wicked "live, become old, and remain mighty in power" (21:7) and die quietly (21:13).
- Pulpit adds that Job modifies this later, in 27:13-23.
- Cambridge (20:29): Zophar's closing "from God" "forces Job into the arena". In ch. 21 Job "shews that that which comes 'from God' (ch. Job 21:22) is something very different".
- Gill (20:5): the friends first said the wicked never prosper. "being beat out of that", they now grant brief prosperity.

## 2. Hard interpretive questions

**Q1. Is Zophar's doctrine true? If so, what is wrong with the speech?**
The near-unanimous Reformed and patristic answer is that the doctrine is true, but Zophar applied it wrongly.
- **Henry:** "Never was any doctrine better explained, or worse applied".
- **Calvin, Sermon 73:** the friends "haue taken a grounde that it is true in it selfe, but they applyed it amisse to the persone of Iob". Later: "he taketh a generall sentence, and applyeth it amisse".
- **Gregory:** we should set aside what he says untrue against Job and "consider how true are the things he speaks, if he were speaking them against the ungodly". Gregory then reads the whole chapter morally, as a picture of the *hypocrite*.
- **Ellicott (20:25):** "perfectly true in a sense, yea, even a truism, but... utterly false in its application to Job".
- **Barnes (20:29)** puts the error in the time horizon: "the fault here was in taking in only a small part of existence - this short life".
- **Benson and Meyer** agree that he was wrong to think temporal judgment always falls in this life, though there is a real link between sin and punishment.
- **Pulpit (20:5)** asks whether the triumph of the wicked is really "short": "Dionysius the elder, Sylla, Marius, Tiberius, Louis XIV., Napoleon".
- **The Expositor's Bible (Watson) is harshest.** Applied to Job, the speech is "flat heresy, a foul slander charged on the providence of God". Even as a general doctrine it covers only the rare spectacular tyrant: "We cannot argue from what sometimes is to what must be."

**Q2. "Sweet in his mouth... gall of asps" (20:12-16): what is the sweet thing, and how does it turn bitter?**
- **Sin in general, pleasant now and deadly later:** Barnes, Benson, Geneva, Meyer ("The 'pleasures of sin' are but for a season").
- **Covetousness and ill-gotten wealth in particular:** Gill and Ellicott, in context with 20:15 and 20:19. Ellicott suspects Zophar "extracts" the charge "from the bare fact of his having been a wealthy man".
- **Conscience, with restitution forced rather than given freely:** Henry (Zaccheus vs. Judas), Benson, Pulpit ("disgust and nausea arrive in course of time"; compare Butler on conscience waking up).
- **Calvin** pictures the poisoner who sweetens his dose with honey and sugar. He then turns the image toward Christ: "seeke true and liuely sweetenesse in his grace".
- **Gregory** takes the "bread" as Holy Scripture in the hypocrite's mouth. Studied for show, "he converts the draught of life into a cup of poison to himself".

**Q3. Why does Zophar not answer "I know that my Redeemer lives"? What does that teach?**
- **Gill:** he may be mocking the "triumph of faith" as the hypocrite's short joy.
- **Expositor's (Watson):** Zophar heard "Goel" as "avenger" only. He is "the bigot in the presence of genius". Watson compares him to the scribes who rejected Christ.

## 1. Background and structure notes

**Upper Room / "five chapters" (13-17).** Sproul: "chapter 13 begins five chapters of gospel narrative that focus our attention on the events that took place in the upper room, right before the death of Christ." Ryle: "For five consecutive chapters we find the Evangelist recording matters which are not mentioned by Matthew, Mark, and Luke." Ferguson's Ligonier series page calls John 13-17 "the Upper Room or Farewell Discourse." (The scholarly label "Book of Glory" for chs. 13-20/21 is standard (from Raymond Brown and C. H. Dodd) but it is **not** in any saved source, so attribute it generally if you use it.)

**Passover timing.** John says "before the feast of the Passover" (13:1). Sproul calls the John/Synoptic Holy Week chronology "one of the most ferocious problems we have in biblical studies," says "We do not know for sure," and gives his own view: "I do not think that it was. I think it was an evening meal before the actual Passover meal." Ellicott refers readers to his "Excursus F: The Day of the Crucifixion of our Lord" (not fetched). Gill goes further: he puts this supper at Bethany two days before the Passover (a minority view). Augustine connects "pascha" with *transitus*, "passing over" (Jesus "pass[es] out of this world to the Father"; we "pass over from the devil to Christ"). Suggested handling: one sentence. John times the scene "before the feast" and the meal is in progress (Vincent: the better reading of v.2 means "*during* supper," since Jesus reclines again in v.12 and gives the morsel in v.26; Augustine makes the same point, that the supper "was still going on").

**Foot-washing as a slave's task.**
- Sproul (verified): "in antiquity, the washing of a person’s feet by another was considered such a low, degrading, menial task that it was reserved for slaves. Even within Israel, if a Jewish person had a Jewish slave, the slave owner was not permitted to require his Jewish slave to engage in such an action as the washing of his master’s feet. Only a gentile slave could be required to do such a menial task." (This is the rabbinic tradition usually cited from the Mekilta on Exod 21:2. I could not fetch a primary or Carson text to confirm it, so **attribute it to Sproul**.)
- Ligonier devotional: "most Jews saw this task as demeaning, so it was given only to the most menial of servants."
- Pulpit Commentary cites Westcott citing a rabbinic gloss on Ezek 16:9: "Among men, the slave washes his master, but with God it is not so."
- EGT (Dods): ordinarily the youngest or the disciples in turn would do it, "but this evening the disciples had been disputing which of them was the greatest, Luke 22:24, and consequently no one could stoop to do this menial office." Ellicott makes a similar link to Luke 22:24-27 ("I am among you as he that serveth").
- Ellicott: the outer garment was laid aside, "leaving the tunic, which was the ordinary dress of a servant"; girding with a towel "was itself a mark of the servant’s position."

**"eis telos" (13:1), "to the end" or "to the uttermost."**
- Ellicott: the sense "unto the end" is "very rare"; the usual meaning is "in the fullest degree," "up to the limit." He suggests a Hebrew idiom: "Loving, he loved them with fulness of love."
- Vincent: interpretations differ. He lists "to the uttermost" (Westcott, Godet) and "at last" (Meyer, Thayer), and prefers "at last, or finally He loved them," meaning a final *manifestation* of his love.
- EGT: Chrysostom, Euthymius and Cyril took it as "in the highest degree," but "Godet is wrong in saying that εἰς τέλος never means “unto the end”" (Matt 10:22).
- Chrysostom: "He continued loving them unceasingly." Augustine takes the "end" as Christ himself ("For what mean these words, 'to the end,' but just to Christ?", citing Rom 10:4). He also accepts "it was His very love that carried Him on to death," and denies that the love ended at death.
- Calvin: the love "was firm and lasting; ... death itself would not quench this love."
- Takeaway for the episode: both senses work. His love reached the end *and* went to the uttermost.

**"tithēmi": laying aside the garments (13:4) / laying down life (10:11, 17-18).**
- EGT (verified): "τίθημι is similarly used in τίθημι τὴν ψυχήν, John 10:11, etc." Vincent notes the present tense, "layeth aside," and that the garments were the upper garments. EGT's note on v.12 cross-references "τίθησι τὰ ἱμάτια of John 13:4". My own inference (not stated by EGT): v.12 "taken his garments" echoes 10:17-18, "lay it down ... take it up again".
- Augustine draws the theological parallel without the Greek: "[Why should we wonder that He] laid aside His garments, who, being in the form of God, made Himself of no reputation ... When about to be crucified, He was indeed stripped of His garments." Sproul and Ellicott both connect the act to Philippians 2.

**louō vs niptō (13:10).** Vincent: the A.V. "obliterates the distinction between λούω, to bathe, to apply water to the whole body, and νίπτω, to wash a part of the body." EGT: "He that has been in the bath has no need to wash save his feet"; at a feast, guests would have bathed beforehand and only their feet, dusty from the road, were washed at the house (citing Wetstein). Sproul makes the same everyday point. Ellicott: "Better, He who has bathed."

**Majesty before humility (13:3).** Augustine: "when about to speak of the pre-eminent humility of the Lord, it was his [John's] desire first to commend His majesty." Chrysostom: John speaks "even wondering, that one so great ... did this thing." Calvin reads v.3 as the source of Christ's "well-regulated composure of mind." Ellicott: "*because* He was conscious of all this, He left the disciples an example."

## 2. Hard interpretive questions

### Q1. What does the washing signify: an example of humility, or a sign of cleansing?
- **Mainly an example of humility (with cleansing secondary):** Chrysostom says Jesus did it so "That we may learn to be modest". He reads "clean" in v.10 as "clean through the word" (15:3) rather than forgiven, and his application turns to good works. Ellicott: "By the act of washing their feet, He, their Lord, taught the spirit of self-sacrifice and love in opposition to the spirit of self-seeking and pride" Peter must learn to "accept the love of Christ’s humiliation."
- **Mainly a sign of cleansing that points to the cross:** Sproul: "if we stop there and only focus on that dimension [humility], we will miss the central point". The rite "is looking ahead" to the cross and is tied to "the significance of baptism". Spurgeon (Sine Qua Non): Christ was thinking "not so much to the actual foot-washing, as to the spiritual washing." Peter already understood the literal act, so "What I do thou knowest not now" must refer to its inner meaning.
- **Both:** Augustine (the basin of water points to "His blood upon the earth to wash away the filth of their sins", and humility is to be imitated); Matthew Henry (it teaches us "to think nothing below us" and also signifies "the value of spiritual washing"); Ryle (a "plain practical lesson" of humility and love "on its surface" and "deep spiritual lessons" beneath it); the Ligonier devotionals (vv.1-11 = cleansing, vv.12-17 = example).
- **Calvin's angle:** Peter's refusal is false humility. "pride will always lurk under the garb of humility" until we stop judging God's works. True humility is obedience. Henry agrees: "it is not humility, but unbelief, to put away the offers of the gospel."

### Q2. "The one who has bathed ... except for his feet": what are the bath and the feet? (Baptism? Justification vs. daily sanctification or daily pardon?)
This is where the voices disagree most.
- **Augustine:** the bath = baptism ("in holy baptism a man has all of him washed"). The feet = the daily sins we pick up walking through the world, washed by Christ's intercession and by confession (the Lord's Prayer, 1 John 1:8-9).
- **Calvin:** "washed" includes both pardon and renewal, but he thinks the context points to "the grace of regeneration". The daily foot-washing is explicitly "not the forgiveness of sins, but the renewal". It is ongoing *sanctification* against the "remains of the flesh". The "feet" are "all the passions and cares by which we are brought into contact with the world."
- **Ryle:** the opposite emphasis from Calvin. The cleansed "need a daily application to the blood of Christ for daily pardon."
- **Spurgeon (Sine Qua Non):** joins the two. First comes the once-for-all "washing in the precious blood, by which all sin is once and for ever put away". Then "daily pardon for sin" ("our Lord is the morning and evening Lamb for daily guilt"). Third, "continual sanctification ... by the power of the Holy Spirit." His image is the priest bathed once at consecration and washing hands and feet at the laver before each sacrifice.
- **Ligonier devotional (a classic Reformed formulation):** the full bath = conversion/justification, permanent ("Postconversion sins do not affect our legal relationship to God"). Foot-washing = confession that restores *fellowship*.
- **Matthew Henry:** the justified must daily "wash their feet; to cleanse themselves from daily guilt."
- **Sproul:** links the act to baptism (as a sign of cleansing) and the cross. His paradox: "When I wash your feet, I make you clean all over".
- **Chrysostom:** the disciples were "clean" not as forgiven (the sacrifice had not yet been offered) but "through the word" (15:3), freed from error.
- **Ellicott:** the bath = the moral change that made them children of God. The feet = defilement from everyday life (that night, their pride). Failure should not make us doubt "the reality of the moral change."

### Q3. "Not all of you are clean": were Judas' feet washed, and in what order?
- **Chrysostom:** yes, and *first*: "He seems to me to have washed the traitor first, then to have come to Peter". He takes "began" and "then he came to Simon Peter" as implying someone was washed before Peter. He says Jesus washed Judas to win him "not by compulsion, but by choice."
- **Augustine:** disagrees on the order. Jesus "began with him [Peter]," the first of the apostles. But Judas *was* washed: "He did not esteem it beneath His dignity to wash also the feet of one whose hands He already foresaw to be steeped in wickedness."
- **Calvin:** Judas was washed, which shows "the wonderful patience of Christ." Christ does not name him "that he may not shut against him the gate of repentance". Calvin also says "he began with Peter."
- **Spurgeon:** "the feet of Judas were washed". The outward rite did not bring union with Christ.
- **Sproul:** "presumably even washed the feet of Judas."
- **Ryle:** a sobering lesson: "Baptism and Churchmanship are no proof that we are right in the sight of God."
- So the outward washing (or sacrament) is not the same as being clean, and Christ's patience reached even the betrayer.

## 1. Background and structure (with sources)

**Author, place and date.**
- The writer calls himself "the elder" (ho presbyteros). Eusebius (HE 3.25, `3john_eusebius3.txt`) lists 2 and 3 John among the *disputed* books "whether they belong to the evangelist or to another person of the same name." Jerome (De Viris Illustribus 9, `3john_jerome_vir.txt`) records that some assigned them to "John the presbyter." Papias's "presbyter John" appears at HE 3.39.
- Ligonier (`3john_lig_third.txt`) holds that the apostle John wrote it. It says the letter may be the earliest of the three, and "in any case it would not be much earlier than the last decade of the first century."
- The Catholic Encyclopedia article on John (`3john_ce_john.txt`) gives the tradition that John "lived in Asia Minor in the last decades of the first century and from Ephesus had guided the Churches of that province."
- Expositor's Greek Testament (in `3john_bh_1-7.clean.txt`) says the missionaries "went out … from Ephesus, the seat of the Apostle."
- Benson (in `3john_bh_1-9.clean.txt`) argues that the writer acts as Diotrephes's superior, which makes the apostle more likely than "John the presbyter."
- Ligonier notes that 3 John is the shortest book in the NT.

**Gaius.**
- There are several men named Gaius in the NT: Acts 19:29; 20:4; Rom 16:23; 1 Cor 1:14. Ligonier says none can be identified with certainty as this Gaius.
- Whedon (`3john_whedon.txt`) says this Gaius lived "some thirty-five or forty years later, and in Asia Minor," so he is probably not Paul's Gaius.
- Ligonier calls him "likely wealthy and held a position of influence."
- Barnes and Gill (v4, `3john_bh_1-4.clean.txt`) infer from "my children" that John converted him.
- The *Apostolic Constitutions* 7.46 (`3john_apconst7.txt`, a 4th-century compilation of uncertain reliability) lists among the bishops ordained by the apostles: "Of Pergamus, Gaius." It also lists "Of Philadelphia, Demetrius, by me." (The "me" is the voice of John in the text.) Treat this as late legend, but it is colourful.

**Diotrephes, v9.**
- The name means "Zeus-nourished" (Vincent; Gill says it marks a Gentile).
- *philoprōteuōn* ("loving to be first") occurs only here in the NT (Barnes, Cambridge, Vincent, Meyer).
- Barnes: it "means simply, 'who loves to be first' - meaning that he loved to be at the head of all things."
- Cambridge links the word to Matt 20 ("whosoever willeth to be first among you"). It suggests Diotrephes wanted to make his church independent of John at Ephesus.
- Catholic Encyclopedia, "Hierarchy of the Early Church" (`3john_ce_hierarchy.txt`): the word "does not warrant the conclusion that Diotrephes had usurped his position of authority." In other words, he misused a real office.
- The letter John says he wrote "to the church" is lost. Pulpit, Ligonier and MacArthur say Diotrephes may have suppressed or destroyed it.
- v10 gives four charges: (1) he prates against us with evil words; (2) he refuses to receive the brothers himself; (3) he stops those who want to; (4) he puts them out of the church. MacArthur's four counts are worded a little differently.

**"In a manner worthy of God" (axiōs tou theou), v6.**
- Expositor's gives two readings: "treat them as you would treat God," since they are His representatives (John 13:20), or "treat them as God would treat them."
- Pulpit: "No higher standard could well be set." It links the phrase to John 13:20 and Matt 25 ("I was a stranger, and ye took me in").
- Bengel: "He, who honours such as are described in the following verse, honours God."
- Gill and Expositor's: *propempō* ("send forward") means escorting and supplying travellers for the road (cf. Titus 3:13).

**"For the sake of the Name," v7.**
- The best text reads "the Name," not "his name" (Cambridge, Pulpit, Vincent). "The Name" means Jesus Christ (Acts 5:41; Jas 2:7). Pulpit: to a Jew "the Name" meant Jehovah, and to a Christian it means Jesus Christ. The Apostolic Fathers use the phrase the same way (Ignatius).
- "Taking nothing from the Gentiles" means taking nothing from pagans. Pulpit says they did this to avoid looking like "quack priests and philosophers." Expositor's says they waived their right, as Paul did (1 Cor 9).

**Didache context** (`3john_didache.txt`, chs. 11-13).
- An itinerant apostle is to be "received as the Lord," but he "shall not remain except one day; but if there be need, also the next; but if he remain three days, he is a false prophet."
- "if he ask money, he is a false prophet."
- In ch. 12 a traveller who will not work is a "Christ-monger."
- In ch. 13 a true prophet or teacher who settles is "worthy of his support."
- This shows the same world as 3 John: wandering preachers and the house-churches that hosted them, which needed both generosity and discernment.

**Mirror of 2 John.**
- Pulpit on v8: as John warned the elect lady that welcoming false teachers makes you share their evil works, here welcoming true teachers makes you share their good works. Matt 10:41: "a prophet's reward."
- Ligonier (`3john_lig_third.txt`): "Having seen in the apostle John's second epistle that believers are not to sanction or provide for false teachers, we will now read how the church is to treat those who teach true, apostolic doctrine."

**Structure.**
- 1-4: greeting, the prayer for health (v2), joy that "my children walk in truth."
- 5-8: Gaius's hospitality to travelling "brothers, strangers," and the call to be "fellow workers for the truth."
- 9-10: Diotrephes.
- 11: the moral, "imitate good." Bengel: the evil is in Diotrephes, the good in Demetrius.
- 12: Demetrius, well spoken of "by everyone, and by the truth itself." He was perhaps the bearer of the letter.
- 13-15: "face to face," peace, "greet the friends by name."

**Church fathers: check before using.**
- Clement of Alexandria's *Adumbrations* (`3john_clement.txt`, newadvent 0211) cover only 1 Peter, Jude, 1 John and 2 John. **There is nothing on 3 John.** Do not cite Clement for 3 John.
- The Venerable Bede did comment on 3 John (in his commentary on the Catholic Epistles). No free English text could be found. His view of Diotrephes survives only as quoted in Whedon's commentary, so it is second-hand.
- Eusebius and Jerome speak only about the authorship and canonical standing of 3 John, not its content.
- No Augustine, Jerome or Chrysostom comment on Diotrephes was found.

## 2. Hard interpretive questions

### Q1. What was Diotrephes's problem: doctrine or ambition?
- **Ambition, not heresy:**
  - Barnes: "the real ground, the apostle says, was his desire to rule."
  - Meyer: John "only accuses him of one thing, namely, the φιλοπρωτεύειν." He calls the theories that Diotrephes was a Gnostic or a Judaizer "unfounded conjectures."
  - Alford (quoted in Whedon): "an ambitious man."
  - Ligonier: "Diotrephes' problem was not necessarily doctrinal … Rather, his problem was moral in that he denied his need to serve others." Ligonier adds that doctrine and conduct "cannot be separated."
  - Matthew Henry: "The temper and spirit of Diotrephes was full of pride and ambition."
- **A heretic:**
  - Bede (via Whedon) calls him "a heresiarch of that time," who used novelties "to usurp the primacy of knowledge."
  - Whedon himself says he "would not reject the apostle but upon some doctrinal ground."
  - Barnes reports that "Erasmus and Bede supposed that he was the author of a new sect" and rejects the idea for lack of evidence.
- **Synthesis:**
  - MacArthur (Ligonier article) turns the question around. Pride is itself the root of heresy: "self-love is the mother of all heresies." By suppressing apostolic teaching, Diotrephes did the work of a heretic.
  - Cambridge sees a parallel. The antichrists of 2 John wanted to be first in ideas, and Diotrephes wanted to be first in power.
- **Episode angle:** 2 John's danger was false doctrine. 3 John's is a self-exalting spirit in someone who may be orthodox. Both shut out the truth.

### Q2. Does v2 promise health and prosperity, and who has authority?
- **v2:**
  - Ligonier: the greeting was standard in letters of the day. Using it to promise perfect health is ruled out "because secular letters of the time include such greetings." Full health and wealth come only with the new creation (Rev 21).
  - Pulpit and Barnes: *peri pantōn* means "in all respects," not "above all things." Pulpit: "St. John would surely never have said that."
  - Matthew Henry turns the verse round: soul prosperity is the standard. "How many professors there are, about whom the apostle's words must be reversed."
  - Barnes: it is still right to wish friends earthly well-being.
- **Authority:**
  - Barnes and Whedon: Diotrephes may have claimed his congregation's independence ("a high congregationalist," Whedon).
  - Gill: a pastor does have a God-given preeminence, but this man "coveted more than was his due." Gill goes on to compare papal claims.
  - The Catholic Encyclopedia says he held a real office, which shows that office does not guarantee faithfulness.
  - Bengel: "If even then, during the life of the apostle, Diotrephes exalted himself, what was not the case afterwards?"
  - Reformed angle: apostolic, written authority (the letter) stands over local leaders. That is *sola Scriptura* in miniature.
