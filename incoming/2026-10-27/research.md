## 1. Setting and background

**Date and events.**
- Chapter 4 looks back on the end of the siege of Jerusalem by Nebuchadnezzar's army in 587/586 BC.
- The famine, from the ESV of Jer 52:6: "On the ninth day of the fourth month the famine was so severe in the city that there was no food for the people of the land." (src/lam/esv/esv_jeremiah_52.txt)
- Zedekiah's flight, from Jer 39:4-5: "they fled, going out of the city at night by way of the king's garden through the gate between the two walls; and they went toward the Arabah. But the army of the Chaldeans pursued them and overtook Zedekiah in the plains of Jericho." The same account is in Jer 52:7-8 and 2 Kgs 25:4-5 (src/lam/esv/).
- This is the scene behind 4:19-20: "they chased us on the mountains; they lay in wait for us in the wilderness. The breath of our nostrils, the LORD's anointed, was captured in their pits."
- Keil-Delitzsch connects "in the wilderness" to the capture: "'In the wilderness' alludes to the capture of Zedekiah; cf. Jeremiah 39:5" (bh_lam4-20.txt).
- He was blinded at Riblah and taken to Babylon (Jer 39:6-7; Calvin on 4:6 mentions it).

**Chapter 5's vantage point.** Chapter 5 is set after the fall: Judah under occupation.
- "Our inheritance has been turned over to strangers" (5:2).
- "We must pay for the water we drink" (5:4).
- "Slaves rule over us" (5:8).
- On 5:21 the Pulpit Commentary takes it as "not a speech of the exiles, but of the Jews left behind, at least for the present, in Judea" (bh_lam5-21.txt).
- On 5:1 Gill reads it of "the Jews in Babylon" (bh_lam5-1.txt).
- The Cambridge Bible leaves the date to its introduction (bh_lam5-1.txt).
- Safe on air: chapter 5 sounds like some time after 586, with people living under occupation.
- Gill and Henry both report that the Vulgate (and Syriac and Arabic) title chapter 5 "The Prayer of Jeremiah". Gill: "This chapter is called, in some Greek copies, and in the Vulgate Latin, Syriac, and Arabic versions, 'the prayer of Jeremiah'" (bh_lam5-1.txt). Henry: "Some ancient versions call this chapter, 'The Prayer of Jeremiah.'" (henry_lam5.txt)

**5:8, "Slaves rule over us."**
- Ellicott says the Chaldeans sent "as rulers those who had filled menial offices in the courts of their kings" (bh_lam5-8.txt).
- JFB cites Neh 5:15 and Gen 9:25: "Israel, once a 'kingdom of priests' (Ex 19:6), is become like Canaan, 'a servant of servants'" (bh_lam5-8.txt).
- Henry cites Prov 30:22: "nothing is more vexatious than a servant when he reigns" (henry_lam5.txt).

**Structure: closing the series threads**

- **Eikah ("How").** Chapter 4 opens with אֵיכָה *'êkāh*, "How" (Strong's 349), as in 1:1 and 2:1 (interlinear_lam4-1.txt).
  - Calvin's translator's note: "This chapter, like the two first chapters, begins with the word 'ykh, 'How this!'" (calvin_lam4_5.txt, note 207).
- **Acrostic.**
  - Chapter 4 is a single acrostic of 22 two-line verses. Calvin's editor: "Here, as in the two first chapters, the verses only begin alphabetically, but instead of having three or six lines, they have only two or four." (calvin_lam4_5.txt, note 206)
  - Cambridge on 5:1: "Like ch. 4, as against ch. 3, each v. is made up of two, not three, members." (bh_lam5-1.txt)
- **Pe before ayin is confirmed in chapter 4.**
  - The Hebrew of 4:16 begins פְּנֵי *pənê*, "the face [of the LORD]", which is pe.
  - 4:17 begins עוֹדֵינָה *'ōwdênāh*, "still", which is ayin (interlinear_lam4-16.txt, interlinear_lam4-17.txt).
  - JFB: "Ain and Pe are here transposed (La 4:16, 17), as in La 2:16, 17; 3:46-51." (jfb_lam4.txt)
  - A nice detail: Brenton's Septuagint labels v.16 "AIN" and v.17 "PHE", so the Greek tradition smooths the order back to the normal one (lxx_brenton_lam4.txt). The Hebrew keeps pe first, as in chapters 2-3.
- **Chapter 5: 22 lines, no acrostic.**
  - Henry: "This chapter, though it has the same number of verses with the 1st, 2nd, and 4th, is not alphabetical, as they were" (henry_lam5.txt).
  - Cambridge (quoting Dummelow): "Rhyme takes the place of the alphabetical structure, the poem having not less than 45 words ending in the sound u." These are the -*nû* "us/our" endings: "our inheritance... our homes... our mothers" (bh_lam5-1.txt).
  - Ellicott's guess that chapter 5 is an acrostic left "in a half-finished state" is a speculation. Use it only to set up a contrast, or not at all.
- **The "like" thread.**
  - In chapter 4 the ESV has "like the ostriches in the wilderness" (4:3) and "like sapphire" (4:7). The other comparisons are "than" comparisons: "greater than the punishment of Sodom" (4:6), "purer than snow, whiter than milk... more ruddy than coral" (4:7), "blacker than soot" (4:8), "as dry as wood" (4:8), "Happier were the victims of the sword than the victims of hunger" (4:9), "swifter than the eagles" (4:19).
  - The thread closes in chapter 5. 5:3, "our mothers are like widows", echoes 1:1, "How like a widow has she become."
  - The book ends with a city of widows. The one "like" left that points upward is 5:21: "Renew our days **as of old**" (*kəqedem*).
- **The comfort thread.**
  - The word "comfort" never appears in chapters 4-5 (ESV).
  - But 4:22 is the book's first flat declarative of comfort: "The punishment of your iniquity, O daughter of Zion, is accomplished."
  - Ellicott: "The mourner shares in the Messianic hopes of Isaiah 40:2, and expresses it nearly in the same words." (bh_lam4-22.txt)
  - Isa 40:1-2 opens "Comfort, comfort my people... Speak tenderly to Jerusalem" (esv_isaiah_40.txt). The "no comforter" of chapter 1 is answered with a sentence from the prophets that will become Isaiah's word of comfort.
  - Henry on 4:21-22: "David's psalms of lamentation commonly conclude with some word of comfort... so does this lamentation here in this chapter." (henry_lam4.txt)

**Key Hebrew**

- **4:20.**
  - *rûaḥ 'appênû* = "breath of our nostrils".
  - *məšîaḥ YHWH* = "anointed of YHWH".
  - *nilkad bišḥîtôtām* = "was caught in their pits".
  - *bəṣillô niḥyeh baggôyim* = "under his shadow we shall live among the nations" (interlinear_lam4-20.txt).
  - LXX (Brenton): "The breath of our nostrils, our anointed Lord, was taken in their destructive snares" (lxx_brenton_lam4.txt).
  - The Greek reads "Christos Kyrios" (Lord Christ), which is why the fathers quote it as "Christ the Lord." Gill's footnote: Sept. "Christus Dominus" (bh_lam4-20.txt).
- **4:10.** *biššəlû*, "have boiled", is Piel of בָּשַׁל *bāšal* (Strong's 1310). It is the same verb as 2 Kgs 6:29, *wannəbaššêl*, "So we boiled my son" (interlinear_lam4-10.txt, interlinear_2ki6-29.txt). "Compassionate" is *raḥămānîyôt*, from the *reḥem*/compassion root.
- **5:19.** "You, O YHWH, forever *têšêb*" means "sit / are enthroned". It is word for word the same as Ps 102:12, "But you, O LORD, are enthroned forever" (interlinear_lam5-19.txt, interlinear_ps102-12.txt).
  - Cambridge: "mg. sittest as king. Cp. Psalm 102:12."
  - Pulpit: "Remainest; better, art enthroned." (bh_lam5-19.txt)
- **5:21, the *shuv* wordplay.** הֲשִׁיבֵנוּ ... וְנָשׁוּבָה, *hăšîbênû* ... *wənāšûbāh*: "Return us (Hiphil, causative) ... and we will return (Qal cohortative)" (interlinear_lam5-21.txt).
  - Jer 31:18 has the identical construction in the singular, from Ephraim: *hăšîbênî wə'āšûbāh*, "turn me back, and I will return" (interlinear_jer31-18.txt).
  - Young's Literal: "Turn us back, O Jehovah, unto Thee, And we turn back".
- **5:22, *kî 'im*.** The interlinear gives "Unless"; YLT turns it into a question: "For hast Thou utterly rejected us?" The options:
  1. "But" (KJV, Vulgate *sed*).
  2. "Unless" (ESV, NASB; Calvin's *nisi*).
  3. "Although / even if" (Targum, per Calvin's editor).
  4. "For surely" (Septuagint/Syriac "for"; Blayney).
  5. As a question (Junius-Tremellius, YLT; Henry's margin: "For wilt thou utterly reject us?").
  - Calvin's editor, note 240: "They are rendered here, 'for,' by the Sept., Syr., and Arab; 'but,' by the Vulg., and 'although,' by the Targ."
  - *mā'ōs mə'astānû* = "rejecting you have rejected us". The doubled form gives the ESV's "utterly" (interlinear_lam5-22.txt).
- **Hebrew/Jewish custom of repeating 5:21 after 5:22.**
  - Witnessed by Calvin in the 1560s (calvin_lam4_5.txt), by Gill citing Rashi ("as Jarchi observes", bh_lam5-22.txt), by Keil-Delitzsch ("In many Hebrew MSS Lam 5:21 is found repeated after Lamentations 5:22"), and by Barnes, Ellicott, Cambridge and Henry.
  - All list the same group of books: Isaiah, Malachi, Ecclesiastes (and Lamentations).
  - Calvin is the best on-air source because he explains the reason (see quote bank).
  - Direct Jewish sources (Sefaria, Chabad) returned 403.

## 2. Outline with key verses

**Chapter 4: The gold grown dim (acrostic; "How" opens it)**
1. **4:1-2. The precious made cheap.** Gold dimmed, holy stones in the streets, "precious sons of Zion... regarded as earthen pots" (an echo of Jeremiah's potter, Jer 18-19, as Calvin notes).
2. **4:3-10. The famine.**
   - Jackals nurse but Zion is "cruel, like the ostriches" (4:3).
   - Infants' tongues stick (4:4).
   - The purple-raised "embrace ash heaps" (4:5).
   - Worse than Sodom (4:6).
   - Princes unrecognizable (4:7-8).
   - The sword is better than hunger (4:9).
   - "Compassionate women have boiled their own children" (4:10).
3. **4:11-12. The LORD did it.** "The LORD gave full vent to his wrath." The nations thought the city could not be taken.
4. **4:13-16. Why: prophets and priests.** They shed "the blood of the righteous" and became like lepers ("Away! Unclean!"). "The face of the LORD" scattered them (4:16; the **pe** verse).
5. **4:17-20. False hopes collapse.**
   - The watch for Egypt (4:17; the **ayin** verse).
   - Hunted in the streets, "our end had come" (4:18).
   - Chased on the mountains and in the wilderness (4:19).
   - The king, "the breath of our nostrils", captured (4:20).
6. **4:21-22. The turn.** Edom's cup is coming. "The punishment of your iniquity, O daughter of Zion, is accomplished; he will keep you in exile no longer."

**Chapter 5: "Remember, O LORD" (22 lines, no acrostic; a communal prayer)**
1. **5:1.** Remember, look, see our disgrace.
2. **5:2-18. The litany.**
   - Land lost; orphans; mothers "like widows" (5:3).
   - Water and wood bought; yoked; begging bread from Egypt and Assyria.
   - "Our fathers sinned... we bear their iniquities" (5:7).
   - "Slaves rule over us" (5:8).
   - Bread at the risk of life; the skin hot "as an oven".
   - Women raped, princes hung up, young men at the mill.
   - Music and dancing stopped.
   - "The crown has fallen from our head; woe to us, for we have sinned!" (5:16).
   - Zion desolate, "jackals prowl over it" (5:18, closing the frame with 4:3's jackals).
3. **5:19. The pivot.** "But you, O LORD, reign forever."
4. **5:20.** "Why do you forget us forever?"
5. **5:21-22. The petition and the open ending.** "Restore us to yourself, O LORD, that we may be restored! Renew our days as of old, unless you have utterly rejected us..."

## 3. Interpretive questions (the hardest)

### Q1. Who is "the breath of our nostrils, the LORD's anointed" (4:20)? Zedekiah, Josiah, or Christ?

- **Josiah.** This is the view of the Targum and Rashi ("Jarchi"), the Geneva note, and Vatablus.
  - Poole and Gill both dismantle it: Josiah was slain, not taken, by Egypt, not Babylon, twenty-three years before the fall.
  - JFB tries to have both, original reference Josiah and applied to Zedekiah.
- **Zedekiah.** Calvin, Henry, Poole, Gill, Barnes, Cambridge and Carson hold this. Keil-Delitzsch broadens it to "the theocratic king" as bearer of 2 Sam 7.
  - Calvin's key move: Zedekiah is called this "however unworthy he was," because of the office and not the man. The titles "properly belong to Christ only".
  - Barnes and Cambridge soften Zedekiah: "weak rather than vicious".
- **Christ.** The fathers read the LXX "Christ the Lord" directly of Christ:
  - Justin (First Apology 55) uses it for the cross-shape of the face.
  - Irenaeus (Demonstration 71) reads "shadow" as Christ's body, "trodden upon" in his sufferings.
  - Tertullian uses it in Against Praxeas 14 for the Son as the Father's "face", and in Against Marcion III.6.
  - Origen (De Principiis II.6.7) reads the "shadow" as Christ's human soul.
  - Gill: "particularly Theodoret takes it to be a direct prophecy."
  - The 19th-century Baptist James Smith: "more applicable to Jesus, than to Josiah, or Zedekiah."
- **Tension worth airing.**
  - Calvin holds the line on the history (Zedekiah, and "absurd" to apply it to Josiah) but still lands in Christ by typology: "we must come to Christ, who is really the spirit or the breath of our nostrils."
  - Henry draws the opposite application: making any creature our "breath" is idolatry. "It is just with God to stop that breath."
  - The fathers jump straight to Christ from a Greek text that already reads "Christ."
  - Recommended on-air line: Zedekiah in its own setting (Jer 39 / 52), with the typology through the Davidic office (2 Sam 7; Ps 89). That counts as one of the two NT moves.

### Q2. "Our fathers sinned... we bear their iniquities" (5:7) vs. "we have sinned" (5:16), Ezekiel 18 and Jer 31:29

- **Calvin.** It "seems" like the sour-grapes blasphemy, but 5:16's confession rules that out. The aim is "to turn God to mercy". Both things are true: God visits fathers' sins, and the children "deserved a hundred deaths." He quotes Horace and Virgil as the pagan version of blaming ancestors.
- **Keil-Delitzsch.** "God does not punish the sins of the fathers in innocent children, but in children who continue the sins of the fathers." Their cross-reference is Jer 16:11-12, "ye do still worse than your fathers."
- **Henry.** It is "not here a peevish complaint... But it is a penitent confession of the sins of their ancestors". He adds a pastoral line: "If we be penitent and patient... he who punishes will pity."
- **Poole.** We "must not understand this in the same sense as Ezekiel 18:2".
- **Pulpit.** It lays out both strands of Scripture and says they "are reconcilable".
- **Ellicott and Cambridge.** The fathers escaped the penalty that fell on the children, so "a double penalty".
- **Summary.** The voices agree. Verse 7 is a plea for pity, verse 16 a confession. Ezekiel 18 forbids the excuse, not the lament.

### Q3. How does the book end (5:21-22)? "Unless you have utterly rejected us"

- **Calvin.**
  - He rejects "but if" as forced.
  - He grants the common reading "although" with candor: if it is right, "the Prophet here exceeded due limits... some heat bubbles up."
  - His own proposal is "Unless". He says, a little proudly, "it is singular that no one has perceived this". It works as an argument from the impossible: "But it cannot be that God will reject his people."
  - His English editor thinks this "too refined" and prefers "For surely... thou hast rejected us." On that reading the verse gives the reason for the petition.
- **Followers of "unless" as an implied impossibility.** Barnes, Ellicott, Cambridge, Pulpit (citing Isa 54:6), JFB, and Ligonier (explicitly following Calvin) all take this line.
- **Gill and Henry.** They prefer the question: "wilt thou utterly reject us? ... surely thou wilt not."
- **Keil-Delitzsch.** They do not need the softening repetition: "v. 22, rightly understood, did not require this repetition." Hope "glimmers from afar, like the morning star."
- **The synagogue custom.**
  - Calvin calls it "superstition" (avoiding a "sad omen").
  - Henry treats it gently: "that the sun may not set under a cloud".
  - Pulpit notes that "ancient doctors of the synagogue thought otherwise" than the hopeful "unless".
- **Q3b. Is 5:21 a prayer for conversion or for restoration?** This is a real Reformed disagreement.
  - Calvin: "he does not speak here of repentance". It is God's outward turning to favor; JFB follows him.
  - Henry, Gill, Geneva, Cambridge and Pulpit read it as converting grace: "Turn us or we shall not be turned."
  - Augustine (on the parallel Psalm prayers) makes the anti-Pelagian point: the God who says "Turn ye and live" is the one to whom we pray "Turn us."
  - Jer 31:18's identical construction (Ephraim's repentance) supports Henry and Gill. Poole holds both senses.

## 1. Setting and background

- **Where we are in the book.** The great climax is 8:6-7 ("love is strong as death... Many waters cannot quench love... If a man offered for love all the wealth of his house, he would be utterly despised"). Verses 8-14 read like a coda or epilogue. Ellicott notes that "Commentators are almost all at one in the feeling that the poem properly ends with Song of Solomon 8:7" (ellicott_8.txt). Keil-Delitzsch places the scene back in "Shulamith's parental home" (kd_8.txt), so the book ends where her story began, among her brothers and vineyards (1:6).
- **Who speaks in 8:8-9.** The ESV heading is "Others". Keil-Delitzsch argues that 8:9 must be the brothers: "it is her brothers alone, as Hoelemann rightly remarks, who utter these words, as might have been expected from them in view of Sol 1:6". KD gives 8:8 to Shulamith asking about a real younger sister. Cambridge (Harper) disagrees: "the little sister is the Shulammite herself", and she is "recalling what she had heard her brothers (Song of Solomon 1:6) say of her in the past". Ellicott takes the same view ("The recollection is carried back to the childhood of the bride"). KD adds the cultural point that "The brothers are the nearest guardians and counsellors of the sister, and, particularly in the matter of marriage, have the precedence even of the father and mother, Genesis 24:50... Genesis 34:6-8" (compare Laban, and Dinah's brothers). The phrase "spoken for" (*dabar be-*) is the idiom for asking a woman in marriage, as when David "spoke for" Abigail in 1 Sam 25:39 (Cambridge, KD, Durham).
- **Hebrew words** (Bible Hub Strong's, saved as strongs_*.txt):
  - *chomah* (H2346), "a wall" (https://biblehub.com/hebrew/2346.htm). KD derives it from a root meaning "to preserve, protect".
  - *deleth* (H1817), "a door" (https://biblehub.com/hebrew/1817.htm). Cambridge: "The Heb. deleth means always strictly a door, never a doorway, which is pethach". KD: the door is "moveable; and though it be for the present closed... it is so formed that it can be opened again."
  - *shalom* (H7965), "completeness, soundness, welfare, peace" (https://biblehub.com/hebrew/7965.htm). In 8:10, "one who finds peace" (*kemotse'et shalom*) is a variation on the common phrase "find favor (*chen*) in the eyes of". KD and Pulpit both see a deliberate play: *Shulammite / shalom / Shelomoh* (Solomon), and 1 Chron 22:9 calls Solomon "a man of peace". The ESV margin "brings out" reflects a different vowel reading (interlinear: https://biblehub.com/interlinear/songs/8-10.htm).
  - *Baal-hamon* (H1174), "possessor of abundance", a place in Palestine, used only here (https://biblehub.com/hebrew/1174.htm). Literally "lord/owner of a multitude". Ellicott: "Baal, as lord with us, often means husband, and Baal-hamon has a covert allusion to the polygamy of the king." The LXX has *Beelamon*. KD, Cambridge and Pulpit tie it to Balamon near Dothan (Judith 8:3), north of Samaria near Shunem.
  - *kerem* (H3754), "a vineyard" (https://biblehub.com/hebrew/3754.htm). Note the echo of 1:6: "mine own vineyard have I not kept" becomes "My vineyard, my very own" (*karmi shelli*).
  - *barach* (H1272), "to go through, flee" (https://biblehub.com/hebrew/1272.htm). This is the verb behind "Make haste" in 8:14. KD renders it "Flee, my beloved". Durham says the word "signifieth 'flee away,' importing the greatest haste and speed that maybe".
- **Money.** "A thousand pieces of silver" means 1,000 shekels. Isaiah 7:23 speaks of "a thousand vines, worth a thousand shekels of silver". Ellicott says Isaiah shows "it was customary to portion off vineyards into sections containing a certain number of vines". Cambridge says Isa 7:23 "is not parallel": there the figure is the vineyard's price, here its yield. The 200 for the keepers is a 20% share. KD calls it "the rendering of thanks... as מעשׂר (a tenth part)" times two brothers. The verbal link back to 8:7 ("all the wealth of his house... utterly despised") is the point Cambridge stresses: "Following up the same train of thought, that love could not be bought".
- **Last verse and 2:17.** Song 2:17 reads "Turn, my beloved, be like a gazelle or a young stag on cleft mountains" (KJV "mountains of Bether", i.e., separation). Song 8:14 changes this to "mountains of spices". Pulpit: "what were before 'mountains of Berber [sic, Bether],' that is, of 'separation,' are now 'mountains of Besamin' - balsam mountains." Hudson Taylor and Spurgeon make the same contrast.
- **Revelation 22:17, 20.** The NT does not quote Song 8:14, so the connection is drawn by interpreters. Durham ("Rev. 22:17, 'The Spirit and the Bride say, come.'"), Spurgeon (sermon 2360, reading Rev 22:20), Henry ("Even so, come, Lord Jesus, come quickly"), Gill ("as John does the Revelation"), JFB (Re 22:20) and Hudson Taylor (ends his book quoting Rev 22:17, 20) all draw it. Keil-Delitzsch explicitly rejects any eschatological "hasten hither" sense in the verb.

## 2. Outline with key verses

1. **8:8-9: The brothers and the little sister.** "We have a little sister... What shall we do for our sister on the day when she is spoken for? If she is a wall, we will build on her a battlement of silver, but if she is a door, we will enclose her with boards of cedar." The wall stands for chastity and steadfastness, rewarded with ornament. The door stands for openness, met with protective restraint.
2. **8:10: Her answer.** "I was a wall, and my breasts were like towers; then I was in his eyes as one who finds peace." Her purity is proved, and so she wins the beloved's favor (*shalom* / Solomon wordplay).
3. **8:11-12: Two vineyards.** Solomon's huge vineyard at Baal-hamon ("lord of a multitude") is let out to keepers for 1,000 silver pieces. Her answer: "My vineyard, my very own, is before me; you, O Solomon, may have the thousand." What is rented and bought is set against what is self-given (echoing 1:6 and 8:7).
4. **8:13: He asks for her voice.** "O you who dwell in the gardens, with companions listening for your voice; let me hear it." "You who dwell" is feminine in Hebrew, so he is addressing her.
5. **8:14: Her last word.** "Make haste, my beloved, and be like a gazelle or a young stag on the mountains of spices." The book ends on longing and motion, not on stillness. Compare 2:17.

## 3. Interpretive questions

### Q1. Who are the speakers in 8:8-10, and what do "wall" and "door" mean?
- **Literal sense (KD, Cambridge, Ellicott).** These are the brothers of 1:6, the guardians of a young girl's honour before marriage. The wall means a girl who resists seduction, and the door means one who might open. KD: "A maiden inaccessible to seduction is like a wall, and one accessible to it is like a door." Ellicott calls it "one of the strongest arguments for the theory that chaste wedded love is the theme of this book". Her reply in 8:10 is a vindication. Cambridge: "The bride here proudly claims that she has proved herself the sufficient guardian of her own honour." KD reads it as gratitude to the brothers: "the praise she takes to herself she takes to the honour of her brothers." Cambridge, by contrast, says "All her brothers' anxieties were idle." That is a real tension, and it is useful for a talk on raising daughters. Did the brothers' strict care help, or was it her own resolve? KD says both. Practical wisdom: guardians protect and reward, but the girl's own "I was a wall" is decisive.
- **Patristic application to raising a daughter.** Jerome (Letter 107 to Laeta, AD 403, on how to raise little Paula) quotes 8:10 directly: "when one knocks at her door... let her say: I am a wall and my breasts like towers."
- **Allegorical sense (Henry, Gill, Poole, Durham, JFB).** The little sister is the Gentile world, or the unconverted elect. "Wall" and "door" become faith and openness to Christ. Durham reads the door *positively*, as weak but open to Christ ("if she be a door, and give entrance to Christ... we will not only adorn her, but also fix and strengthen her more"). This is the reverse of the literal commentators, for whom the door is a moral danger. Poole is in between: the door is "weaker than a wall... yet we will not therefore reject and forsake her". Pulpit (Clarke/Jones) rejects the seduction reading as "far fetched" and treats wall and door as different functions ("The wall is for defence; the door is for admission"). So the voices answer differently.
- **Who speaks 8:8?** KD says Shulamith speaks 8:8 and the brothers 8:9. Hitzig (cited in KD) suggests two brothers in dialogue. Pulpit says the bride asks and Solomon answers with a royal "we". Cambridge and Ellicott say the brothers in the past, recalled by her. Henry, Poole and Durham give 8:8 to the bride (church) and 8:9 to Christ, with Durham taking the "we" as "the Father, Son, and Spirit". The ESV heading "Others" sensibly leaves it open.

### Q2. What is the point of Solomon's vineyard at Baal-hamon (8:11-12), and who speaks 8:12?
- **Literal.** Cambridge (on the shepherd-hypothesis view): "her vineyard, the only wealth she has, viz. her person and her love, are in her own power, and Solomon will have to be content with the material riches he possesses." Ellicott (one-wife reading): the harem is like a rented-out vineyard "valued only for the return it yields", but "the one wedded wife is a vineyard tended by the owner, loved for its own sake". KD (Solomon is the husband, not a rival): "This vineyard has been given over with free and joyful cordiality into Solomon's possession", and the 200 is her thanks to the brothers "the true keepers and protectors of her innocence".
- **Tension.** Cambridge reads 8:11-12 as "scorn for Solomon and his wealth" (shepherd hypothesis). Pulpit and KD insist "this language of the bride is entirely against the shepherd theory" (Pulpit on 8:10). This is the classic split in literal readings of the Song: Solomon as the true lover or Solomon as the rival. For the PCA host: the traditional reading takes Solomon (the type of the "greater than Solomon") as the bridegroom.
- **Who speaks 8:12?** Most give it to the bride: Cambridge, KD, Henry ("They are rather the words of the spouse"), Gill (the second clause "certain[ly]"), and Durham, who argues it at length ("It cannot therefore be the Bridegroom that here speaks, but the Bride"). Poole splits the verse: first clause Christ, second clause the bride. Henry mentions that "Some take these for Christ's words".
- **Allegorical.** Henry: the church is the vineyard, compare Mt 21:33. "Our hearts are our vineyards, which we must keep with all diligence." Poole: "purchased not by his money, but by his blood". Hudson Taylor: "what she was (by grace) was more important than what she did". Durham turns 8:11-12 into a practical syllogism of assurance.

### Q3. Why does the book end on longing and separation ("Make haste... be like a gazelle") rather than on consummation?
- **Literal.** KD reads *barach* as "Flee" and the verse as a playful song she sings as they leave together. It insists "there is not a syllable of that thought" (an eschatological return) "in the text". Ellicott: the last two verses "compress into them all that has been over and over again related under different figures". Pulpit: "The bridegroom and the bride are seen disappearing together over the flowery hills". It also notes the shift from mountains of separation to mountains of spices.
- **Allegorical and eschatological.** Durham, Henry, Gill, Poole, JFB, Spurgeon and Taylor all read 8:13-14 as Christ's departure (ascension) and the church's prayer for his return. Spurgeon: "The Song of Love and the Book of Love end in almost the same way". JFB: "The abrupt, unsatisfied close with the yearning prayer for His visible coming shows that the marriage is future". Durham: "uninterrupted communion is reserved for heaven". Henry: "Why tarry the wheels of his chariots?"
- **Who draws Rev 22?** Durham explicitly cites Rev 22:17. Spurgeon cites Rev 22:20 and expounded Rev 22 in the same service. Henry and Gill echo "Even so, come, Lord Jesus". Taylor ends his book with Rev 22:17, 20. KD dissents.
- **Tension inside Spurgeon on 8:13.** In his 1883 sermon he says the Hebrew is feminine, so Christ addresses the church ("we are bound to regard it as the word of the Bridegroom to His bride"). In *Morning and Evening* (Oct 30, evening) he reads the same verse as Christ dwelling in the garden of his church. Gill notes that the Septuagint (masculine) and Ambrose read it as the church speaking to Christ.

## 1. Setting and background

**Where we are.** This is still the Feast of Tabernacles (Booths), in the middle of the week (7:14, "about the middle of the feast"). Last episode covered 7:14-24: the teaching at mid-feast, "my teaching is not mine," and the circumcision argument. The next episode covers 7:37-44, "the last day, the great day of the feast" and the water-pouring. Our passage sits between the two and has two scenes:
- **7:25-31**, still in the temple (7:28): Jerusalemites debate whether the rulers have secretly concluded he is the Messiah. Jesus "cried out," they try to seize him and fail, and many believe.
- **7:32-36**: the Sanhedrin sends officers. Jesus speaks of going away, and they misunderstand.

Westcott treats 25-31 as "the second scene" and 32-36 as "the third scene in the controversy" (westcott_john_1892_djvu.txt).

**Who are "some of the people of Jerusalem" (7:25)?** They are not the pilgrim crowd of 7:20 ("Who is seeking to kill you?"). The Jerusalem locals know about the plot. Ellicott: "These Jerusalemites are distinct from the multitude of John 7:20, and are acquainted with the intention which seemed so impossible to the latter." MacArthur and Calvin say the same. Westcott notes that the Greek *Hierosolymitai* occurs elsewhere in the NT only in Mark 1:5.

**"Speaking openly" (7:26)** translates *parrēsia* (boldness, plainness of speech; Strong's 3954, strongs_3954.txt). It answers 7:4 ("no one works in secret") and 7:13 ("no one spoke openly of him for fear of the Jews").

**"Can it be that the authorities really know..." (7:26).** The Greek question (*mēpote*) expects "no." Ellicott: "The word 'indeed' shows that the questioners think it impossible." Westcott renders it "Can it be that the rulers indeed know...?" MacArthur: "It's a kind of question that carries with it its own denial."

**The hidden-Messiah tradition (7:27).** These sources were downloaded and checked:
- **Justin Martyr, *Dialogue with Trypho* 8** (c. AD 155-160). Trypho the Jew says: "But Christ — if He has indeed been born, and exists anywhere — is unknown, and does not even know Himself, and has no power until Elias come to anoint Him, and make Him manifest to all." (justin_dial_1-9.txt; https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/01281.htm)
- **Justin, *Dialogue* 49**, Trypho again: "For we all expect that Christ will be a man [born] of men, and that Elijah when he comes will anoint him." (justin_dial_48-54.txt; https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/01284.htm)
- **Justin, *Dialogue* 110**, Justin reporting the rabbis: "they maintain He has not yet come; or if they say that He has come, they assert that it is not known who He is; but when He shall become manifest and glorious, then it shall be known who He is." (justin_dial_109-124.txt; https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/01288.htm)
- **1 Enoch 48:6** (R. H. Charles translation): "And for this reason hath he been chosen and hidden before Him, / Before the creation of the world and for evermore." (enoch_charles_pseudepigrapha.txt; http://www.pseudepigrapha.com/pseudepigrapha/enoch.htm)
- **4 Ezra (2 Esdras) 13:25-26, 52** (RSV). The man rising "from the heart of the sea... is he whom the Most High has been keeping for many ages." Then: "Just as no one can explore or know what is in the depths of the sea, so no one on earth can see my Son or those who are with him, except in the time of his day." (esdras2_13_rsv.txt; https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=2+Esdras+13&version=RSV). 4 Ezra is usually dated after AD 70. Present it as a later witness to the same strand of thought, not as a source the crowd heard.
- **Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 97a**, quoted second-hand by Westcott and the Pulpit Commentary: "three things come wholly unexpected, Messiah, a god-send and a scorpion." (westcott_john_1892_djvu.txt; bh_7-27.txt)
- The proposed OT roots that commentators name are Malachi 3:1 ("the Lord... will suddenly come to his temple"), Daniel 7:13 (coming with the clouds), Isaiah 53:8 ("who shall declare his generation?"), and Micah 5:2's "whose goings forth have been from of old." Ellicott, Westcott, Ryle, MacArthur, Calvin, Augustine, Cyril and Spurgeon each name one or more of these.
- The **Cambridge Bible (Plummer)** adds a caution: "This text is the strongest, if not the only evidence that we have of the belief that the immediate parents of the Messiah would be unknown." (bh_7-27.txt)

**John's irony against 7:42.** Five verses later, other people object from the opposite side: "Has not the Scripture said that the Christ comes from the offspring of David, and comes from Bethlehem?" The crowd holds both beliefs, that no one will know where the Messiah comes from and that he must come from Bethlehem. They get his origin wrong twice: his earthly birthplace was Bethlehem, not Nazareth, and his true origin is the Father. Chrysostom presses this contradiction hard (quote bank).

**"Cried out" (7:28).** The Greek is *ekraxen*, from *krazō* (Strong's 2896, "to cry out, to shout"; strongs_2896.txt; interlinear_7-28.txt). In John's Gospel, Jesus is said to "cry out" in 7:28, 7:37 and 12:44, and John the Baptist in 1:15. Westcott: "The original word (κράζω) occurs only in these places in the Gospel." 7:37 belongs to the next episode, so this word is a natural bridge.
- *Caution:* MacArthur says "Four times in the New Testament it says that Jesus did this" and that *anaboaō* is "only used one time of Jesus... on the cross." Ryle's note gives a different list. Do not repeat the count on air.

**"He who sent me is true" (7:28).** The word is *alēthinos* (genuine, real), not *alēthēs* (truthful) (interlinear_7-28.txt). Westcott: "God is described as true not merely in so far as He gave a true message, but as one who really sent a messenger; a real Father, as it were, sending a real Son."

**"His hour had not yet come" (7:30).** *Hōra* (Strong's 5610). The hour theme runs through the Gospel:
- 2:4: "My hour has not yet come."
- 7:6 and 7:8: "my time" (*kairos*).
- 7:30.
- 8:20: "no one arrested him, because his hour had not yet come."
- 12:23: "The hour has come for the Son of Man to be glorified."
- 13:1: "Jesus knew that his hour had come."
- 17:1: "Father, the hour has come."

Ellicott links 7:30 back to 2:4 and 7:6. Westcott refers the reader to his note on 13:1.

**"Officers" (7:32, again in 7:45-46).** The word is *hypēretai* (Strong's 5257). Etymologically it means "under-rower," a crewman on a lower deck. In use it means a subordinate who carries out official orders, here the temple police of the Sanhedrin (strongs_5257.txt: "from 5259 /hypó, 'under' and ēressō, 'to row' – properly, a rower..."). Westcott: officers "clothed with legal authority and obeying the instructions of the Council." "The chief priests and Pharisees" probably names the Sanhedrin by its two main parties; Westcott calls the chief priests "rather a political faction than a definite office." The officers come back in 7:45-46 with "No one ever spoke like this man!" (two episodes ahead).

**"A little longer" (7:33).** Tabernacles falls in autumn (Tishri), and the last Passover is about six months later. Westcott: "It was about six months to the Last Passover." Gill: "no longer than till the next passover, which was but about half a year at most."

**"The Dispersion among the Greeks" (7:35).** The phrase is *tēn diasporan tōn Hellēnōn* (interlinear_7-35.txt). *Diaspora* (Strong's 1290) occurs in the NT only here, in James 1:1 and in 1 Peter 1:1. Ellicott traces the Babylonian, Egyptian and Syrian dispersions. Westcott contrasts the two terms for Jews outside the land: "Captivity" marks their relation to the land they lost, "Dispersion" their relation to the lands where they now live. The irony is that the apostles did exactly what is mocked here. They began in diaspora synagogues and went on to the Gentiles, and in John 12:20-23 "some Greeks" come asking for Jesus, which triggers "The hour has come."

**Later echoes.**
- 8:21: "I am going away, and you will seek me, and you will die in your sin. Where I am going, you cannot come."
- 13:33, to the disciples: "Little children, yet a little while I am with you. You will seek me, and just as I said to the Jews, so now I also say to you, 'Where I am going you cannot come.'" Then 13:36: "you will follow afterward."
- OT background for 7:34: Proverbs 1:28 ("Then they will call upon me, but I will not answer; they will seek me diligently but will not find me") and Isaiah 55:6 ("Seek the LORD while he may be found"). MacArthur closes his sermon with Isaiah 55:6. Ryle quotes Proverbs 1:28.

## 2. Outline with key verses

1. **The Jerusalemites' puzzle (7:25-27).** "Is not this the man whom they seek to kill?... Can it be that the authorities really know?... But we know where this man comes from." Their unbelief has its own theology.
2. **Jesus cries out (7:28-29).** "You know me, and you know where I come from" (ironic concession). "But I have not come of my own accord. He who sent me is true, and him you do not know. I know him, for I come from him, and he sent me." His real origin is the Father.
3. **The failed arrest (7:30).** "No one laid a hand on him, because his hour had not yet come." The plotters are bound by God's timetable.
4. **Fragile faith in the crowd (7:31).** "When the Christ appears, will he do more signs than this man has done?"
5. **The warrant (7:32).** The Pharisees hear the crowd "muttering" (*gongyzō*, the wilderness verb of 6:41, 6:61). Chief priests and Pharisees send officers.
6. **Jesus answers the warrant (7:33-34).** "I will be with you a little longer, and then I am going to him who sent me. You will seek me and you will not find me. Where I am you cannot come." The departure is his own act, and there is a "too late."
7. **The unwitting prophecy (7:35-36).** "Does he intend to go to the Dispersion among the Greeks and teach the Greeks?" They mock what the book of Acts will record as fact.

## 3. Interpretive questions (the hardest)

### Q1. How can they say both "we know where he comes from" and "no one will know where the Christ comes from," and how does Jesus answer?
- **Augustine** (Tr. 31.2-3) says both statements are half-true. The Scriptures foretold where the Messiah would come from as man (Nazareth, Bethlehem). As God, his origin "was hidden from the ungodly." So Jesus answers: "you both know me, and you know me not."
- **Chrysostom** (Hom. 50) calls their reasoning "the words of drunken men" and "the decision of madmen." He sets 7:27 against 7:42 and 9:29. He reads "you know whence I am" as a different kind of knowing: "It is therefore possible, knowing, not to know."
- **Calvin** finds the root of their error in reading only half the Scripture. Micah 5:2 names Bethlehem and also an eternal "going forth." To take one without the other is to "mangle the Scriptures, and even Christ himself." He calls Jesus' reply "ironical language."
- **Cyril** (Book 5) reads the crowd's maxim through Isaiah 53:8, "His generation who shall declare?", and applies it to the Son's eternal begetting: "what tongue shall declare the unspeakable Birth of the Son from the Father?" His Jesus in effect says: "I agree to your words herein." The Messiah's origin really is unknown, because it is the Father.
- **Henry** shows the syllogism is a fallacy because its terms shift between Christ's divine and human natures.
- **Ryle** admits it is "rather difficult" and offers that these speakers were "singularly ignorant Jews." He quotes the Scottish Covenanter George Hutcheson, who blames the error on "not comparing of Scripture with Scripture."
- **Plummer** (Cambridge) and **Westcott** think "whence" means his parentage and home, not his birthplace.
- **Possible tension:** Ryle and MacArthur say the crowd could easily have checked the Bethlehem records ("Their ignorance was without excuse"). Augustine and Cyril are more interested in the mystery of the eternal generation. Both points stand, and a good segment can use both.

### Q2. "His hour had not yet come" (7:30): fate, providence, or Jesus' own will?
- **Augustine and Cyril** both warn against reading this as fate or astrology. Augustine: "The Lord was not born under fate." Cyril writes several pages against those who "suppose that the affairs of man are subject to hours and days and seasons." Both put the hour in Christ's own will. Augustine: "He departed by power, because He came not by necessity." Cyril: "not by the violence of the Jews, but of His own Will did He come to the Cross."
- **Chrysostom** offers a striking (and arguable) reason why John wrote "his hour had not yet come" instead of saying Jesus restrained them by power: "The Evangelist was minded to speak more humanly and in a lowlier strain, so that Christ might be deemed to be also Man."
- **Calvin** stresses the Father's decree, so the cross is "not through the caprice of men." He then makes a pastoral turn: "the time of every man's death has been fixed by God." He adds a guardrail against presumption.
- **Ellicott** separates the immediate cause (the crowd was divided, so no one dared to act) from the primary cause (God's eternal counsel).
- **Henry, Ryle, Spurgeon and MacArthur** all draw the same pastoral line: Christ's servants are "immortal till their work is done."
- **Real tension:** Augustine and Cyril say "his hour" means his will. Calvin and Henry say the Father's decree. They are complementary, but the difference is worth naming on air (WCF 3.1; 5.1-2 on providence and second causes fits Ellicott's distinction).

### Q3. "You will seek me and not find me... Where I am you cannot come" (7:34). What seeking? Final, or not? And who are "the Dispersion" (7:35)?
- **What seeking?**
  - Augustine reads it hopefully: after the resurrection many sought him "pricked in the heart" at Pentecost. They "were not able" *then*, "for at that time they were such as were not able." He links this to 13:33, 36: "you shall follow me hereafter."
  - Chrysostom thinks of the women mourning and of those who remembered Christ "when the city was taken."
  - Calvin explains it as "the groans of distress uttered by the wicked," Esau-like seeking that never repents.
  - Westcott: "not in penitence nor yet in anger, but simply in distress."
  - Gill reads it as Israel's long and fruitless search for a Messiah.
  - Henry, Ryle, Cyril and MacArthur stress the "too late" warning. MacArthur reads it as hell ("Hell is not where Christ is forgotten; it is where He is unavailable"). Henry: "You cannot come, because you will not."
  - Ellicott rejects any special reference to AD 70 and makes the words general.
  - So the voices range from Augustine's hopeful reading to MacArthur's final one. The 13:33 parallel shows that "you cannot come" is not by itself a sentence of damnation; it depends on the "yet" (13:36).
- **The Dispersion.**
  - Chrysostom (followed by Theophylact and Hengstenberg, per Ryle) takes it as the scattered Gentiles.
  - Calvin, Ryle, Westcott and Gill take it as Jews scattered among Gentile nations. Westcott says the usage is "quite decisive against the interpretation 'the dispersed Greeks'."
  - Augustine and Cyril both say the mockers unknowingly *prophesied* (compare Caiaphas in 11:51). Spurgeon: "They appear to have had some intimation of that glorious love of Christ."
  - Ryle notes this is "precisely what the Apostle Paul and his companions afterwards did."

## 1. Setting and background

**Where we are in the letter.** Last episode covered 5:1-7: elders shepherd the flock, everyone is "clothed with humility," "humble yourselves under the mighty hand of God," "casting all your anxieties on him." Verses 8-11 are the last exhortation and the closing prayer and doxology. Verses 12-14 are the postscript: the courier or secretary, the greetings, and the kiss. The doxology in 5:11 ("To him be the dominion forever and ever") echoes 4:11. With 5:11, Peter's own argument is finished.

**The link between v.7 and v.8 is deliberate.** You cast your care on God, *and* you stay awake. Leighton, Henry, Bengel (via JFB) and Barnes all comment on the move from "cast all your anxieties" to "be sober-minded; be watchful." JFB, citing Bengel: "God provides, therefore do not be anxious. The devil seeks, therefore watch" (ch5_jfb / bh_5-8). Leighton (Quote 1 below) works this out at length.

**Echoes inside the letter:**
- *nēphō* ("be sober-minded"), now the third time: 1:13 ("preparing your minds for action, and being sober-minded"), 4:7 ("be self-controlled and sober-minded for the sake of your prayers"), 5:8. Gill: "The apostle had exhorted to each of these before... but thought fit to repeat them." Leighton says Peter chose "profitable iterations, than unprofitable variety"
- "a little while" (*oligon*) at 5:10 matches 1:6 ("though now for a little while, if necessary, you have been grieved by various trials"). MacArthur and the Ligonier devotional both point this out.
- "called you to his eternal glory in Christ" (5:10) picks up "a partaker in the glory that is going to be revealed" (5:1) and "the unfading crown of glory" (5:4). It also completes the opening doxology (1:3-5) and the calling language in 2:9, 2:21 and 3:9.
- "the God of all grace" (5:10), "the true grace of God" (5:12), "the varied grace of God" (4:10). Calvin's editor links 4:10 to 5:10 in a footnote.
- "Resist him, firm in your faith" (5:9) is the same verb as James 4:7 ("Resist the devil, and he will flee from you"), and Ephesians 6:11-13 ("withstand" = *antistēnai*). MacArthur, Brooks and Ligonier each connect these.
- The brotherhood (*adelphotēs*, 5:9) also appears at 2:17 ("Love the brotherhood"). Gill notes the link.

**The lion contrast with Lamentations.** Lamentations 3:10 has God himself as "a bear lying in wait... a lion in hiding" against Jerusalem. In 1 Peter 5:8 the lion is the devil. Augustine (on Psalm 50) and Thomas Brooks both handle the "two lions" problem: the devil is called a lion for savagery, and Christ is called the Lion of Judah for might (Rev 5:5). Cambridge Bible (5:8) adds that the image may start from Isaiah 38:13, "where, however, it is used of God as visiting men with pain and sickness," or from Psalm 22:21 (ch5_cambridge / bh_5-8). That gives the host a source for the Lamentations contrast: the lion who wounds his people in Lamentations is God, who disciplines, while the lion in 1 Peter is the devil, who devours. Under the sovereign Lion, the roaring lion "may worry, but he cannot rend" (Spurgeon).

**Peter's own story behind the words (question 3 below).**
- "Be watchful" (*grēgoreō*) is the Gethsemane word: "Simon, are you asleep? Could you not watch one hour? Watch and pray that you may not enter into temptation" (Mark 14:37-38; Matt 26:40-41). Vincent says it outright: "A reminiscence of the scene in Gethsemane." JFB: "Peter has in mind Christ's warning to himself to watch against Satan, from forgetting which he fell." Benson: "How deeply had Peter himself suffered for want of the wakeful vigilance which he here recommends!"
- Satan "demanded to have you, that he might sift you like wheat... and when you have turned again, strengthen [*stērixon*] your brothers" (Luke 22:31-32). In 5:10 Peter uses the same verb, *stērixei*, "confirm/establish." Pulpit, JFB, Vincent and Expositor's Greek Testament all make this connection.
- *katartisei* ("restore") is the word for James and John "mending [*katartizontas*] the nets" when Jesus called them (Mark 1:19 / Matt 4:21). Pulpit and Expositor's Greek Testament both note it, and Vincent adds that it is used "of restoring an erring brother (Galatians 6:1)."

**Greek words (Bible Hub Strong's; files strongs_NNNN.txt):**
- *nēphō* (G3525), https://biblehub.com/greek/3525.htm: "to abstain from wine (keep sober), i.e. (figuratively) be discreet." Aorist imperatives here; Ellicott: "Single words in the Greek, and in the tense which bespeaks immediate attention." Cambridge: "Rouse yourselves to sobriety and watchfulness."
- *grēgoreō* (G1127), https://biblehub.com/greek/1127.htm: "to keep awake, i.e. Watch," formed from *egeirō* (rise/wake).
- *antidikos* (G476), https://biblehub.com/greek/476.htm: "an opponent (in a lawsuit); specially, Satan." Vincent: "Only here, in New Testament, of Satan." Cambridge: "a plaintiff or accuser in a trial before a judge" (Matt 5:25; cf. Zech 3:1; Rev 12:10).
- *diabolos* (G1228), https://biblehub.com/greek/1228.htm: "false accuser, devil, slanderer," from *diaballō*. The LXX uses it to translate Hebrew *satan*.
- *ōruomai* (G5612), https://biblehub.com/greek/5612.htm: "roar." Vincent: "Only here in New Testament... It denotes especially the howl of a beast in fierce hunger." Ellicott: "the roaring implies hunger and determination."
- *katapinō* (G2666), https://biblehub.com/greek/2666.htm: "to drink down, i.e. Gulp entire": devour, swallow up, drown. Meyer: "denotes complete destruction."
- *anthistēmi* (G436), https://biblehub.com/greek/436.htm: "to stand against, i.e. Oppose." The same verb appears in James 4:7 and Eph 6:13.
- 5:10 has four future-indicative verbs in the best texts. Pulpit and Vincent say this turns a wish into a promise, and both stress the emphatic *autos*, "he himself":
  - *katartizō* (G2675), https://biblehub.com/greek/2675.htm: "fit, frame, mend, restore... to complete thoroughly, i.e. Repair." Used of mending nets (Mark 1:19).
  - *stērizō* (G4741), https://biblehub.com/greek/4741.htm: "to set fast... (figuratively) to confirm." Used in Luke 22:32.
  - *sthenoō* (G4599), https://biblehub.com/greek/4599.htm: "to strengthen." Pulpit: "The word occurs only here."
  - *themelioō* (G2311), https://biblehub.com/greek/2311.htm: "lay the foundation, ground, settle." Some ancient manuscripts omit it (JFB, Expositor's Bible).

**Textual note on 5:10.** Calvin worked from a text with an optative (a prayer). Most later commentators (Pulpit, Vincent) prefer the future indicative, which makes it a promise. The ESV has "will himself restore." Gill: "the sense is the same; for if it is a prayer, it is a prayer in faith, for what shall be done."

**Silvanus (5:12).** He is Silas: Acts 15:22, 27, 32 (a prophet, chosen to carry the Jerusalem Council letter, which was also "by their hand," *dia cheiros*, Acts 15:23); Paul's companion on the second missionary journey (Acts 15:40-18:5); co-sender of 1 and 2 Thessalonians; named in 2 Cor 1:19. Pulpit: “The preposition "by" διά has the same sense as διὰ χειρός in Acts 15:23. Silvanus was the bearer of the Epistle; he may have been the amanuensis also.” MacArthur thinks he was probably the secretary and perhaps the courier as well. Henry and Ellicott point out what it means for Peter to vouch for Paul's coworker. "As I regard him" (*logizomai*) expresses settled conviction, not doubt (Barnes, Pulpit, Ellicott).

**"She who is at Babylon" (5:13).** The Greek is just "the co-elect [feminine] in Babylon." Most commentators take this as a church; a minority read it as Peter's wife (Cambridge, Pulpit). For the location, see question 1. Background for the host: Babylon is the oppressor of God's people and the type of the world power in Jeremiah 50-51 ("Flee from the midst of Babylon," Jer 51:6, 45), and Revelation 14:8, 17-18 take that up. Ellicott: "on the mention of the name, all the prophecies of the vengeance to be taken on the city which had desolated the Holy Land would rush with consolation into the mind of the readers." This ties the letter's opening, "elect exiles of the dispersion" (1:1), to its close. The church is an exile community, and its home city in this age is "Babylon."

**Mark, "my son" (5:13).** John Mark: his mother's house in Jerusalem is where Peter went after his release from prison (Acts 12:12). He deserted Paul at Perga (Acts 13:13) and caused the split between Paul and Barnabas (Acts 15:37-39). Later he was restored: with Paul in Rome (Col 4:10; Philem 24), and "he is very useful to me for ministry" (2 Tim 4:11). Papias, as reported by Eusebius, says Mark was "the interpreter of Peter" and wrote down Peter's preaching (Quote 14). The two great restorations in the background of this passage are Mark's and Peter's.

**"The kiss of love" (5:14).** Paul calls it the "holy kiss" (Rom 16:16; 1 Cor 16:20; 2 Cor 13:12; 1 Thess 5:26). Expositor's Bible: "We find from Justin Martyr that it had come to be used in his day as part of the ceremonial preceding the Holy Communion." MacArthur: it was men with men and women with women. Calvin: "so that the sincerity of the heart might correspond with the external act." Peter ends with "Peace," where Paul usually ends with "grace."

## 2. Outline with key verses

1. **The enemy and the stance (5:8-9)**
   - v.8, two staccato commands: "Be sober-minded; be watchful." The reason (the "because" is not in the best text): "Your adversary the devil prowls around like a roaring lion, seeking someone to devour."
   - v.9: "Resist him, firm in your faith." The comfort is solidarity: "the same kinds of suffering are being experienced by your brotherhood throughout the world."
2. **The God of all grace and the promise (5:10)**
   - "after you have suffered a little while" (cf. 1:6)
   - "the God of all grace, who has called you to his eternal glory in Christ" (cf. 5:1, 4)
   - "will himself restore, confirm, strengthen, and establish you": mending nets, Luke 22:32, strength, foundation
3. **Doxology (5:11):** "To him be the dominion forever and ever. Amen." The roaring lion prowls, but the dominion belongs to God.
4. **Postscript (5:12-14)**
   - v.12: Silvanus; "I have written briefly"; the purpose of the whole letter in one line: "exhorting and declaring that this is the true grace of God. Stand firm in it."
   - v.13: the co-elect church in "Babylon"; "Mark, my son."
   - v.14: "the kiss of love"; "Peace to all of you who are in Christ."

## 3. Interpretive questions

### (1) Who or what is "Babylon"?

There are three options:

- **Rome (figurative).** This is the ancient view. Eusebius (*Church History* 2.15.2) reports it right after citing Clement and Papias on Mark's Gospel. Peter wrote "in Rome itself... when he calls the city, by a figure, Babylon" (Quote 13). Ellicott defends it vigorously: "nothing but Protestant prejudice can stand against the historical evidence that St. Peter sojourned and died at Rome" (Quote 20b). MacArthur also reads it as Rome, possibly as a cryptic name for the sake of security (Quote 27b). Most modern Reformed and evangelical commentators hold this view.
- **Literal Babylon on the Euphrates.** This was the Reformation and Puritan view: Calvin, Poole, Matthew Henry, Gill. Calvin's reasons are worth hearing: (a) Peter was apostle to the circumcision, and Babylon had a large Jewish diaspora; (b) he doubts the tradition that Peter was at Rome. Calvin and Poole are openly polemical against Rome's use of this text for the papacy, and both make the same sharp joke: Rome would rather "set Rome in the infernal regions" or "go to hell to seek" Peter's chair than lose the claim (Quotes 12, 15). Gill adds that the Babylonian Jews were the "metropolis of the dispersion" and suggests Psalm 87:4 was fulfilled there. The Pulpit Commentary also leans literal, noting that the provinces in 1:1 are listed east to west. Cambridge mentions that order but leaves the question open.
- **Babylon in Egypt** (a Roman fort near Cairo). Ellicott: "No one now, however, maintains this view."

**Tension worth airing.** This is a good case where the old Reformed voices (Calvin, Poole, Henry, Gill) disagree with nearly every modern conservative (Ellicott, MacArthur) and with the fathers (Eusebius). The host can note that Calvin's reluctance came partly from anti-papal polemic. He can also note that the Rome reading gives Rome no papal claim; it only places Peter there. Henry's PCA-friendly point holds either way: "All the churches of Jesus Christ ought to have a most affectionate concern one for another."

Theologically, both readings support the "exile" frame. Peter wrote to "elect exiles" (1:1) and signs off from "Babylon." Ellicott makes the Jeremiah 50-51 connection explicit.

### (2) Is the devil's prowling a real threat to the elect? How do they resist?

- **The threat is real and aimed at believers.** Leighton: "this his enmity... is most enraged against the children of God... directs his attacks most against those who are most advanced in holiness." He goes after them hardest when they are "richly laden," as he did with Jesus right after his baptism (Quote 4). Ellicott says that in a season of persecution Satan is "eyeing all the Christians in turn to see which he has the best chance of." Calvin calls the believer's life "continual war with the devil." Spurgeon says Satan's "ultimate aim" is "the total destruction of a believer."
- **The final outcome is not in doubt.** Gill: the devil can do nothing "without a permission... never with respect to the souls of any of God's elect, which are safe in Christ's hands." Spurgeon: "he cannot rend... He may wound, but he cannot kill" (Quote 21a). Calvin: "whosoever comes to this contest, endued with faith, he declares that he will certainly be a conqueror." This holds together with real warnings (perseverance of the saints, WCF 17). The warning is one of the means God uses to keep them. Leighton puts both together: vigilance and casting care on God are "inseparable."
- **How to resist ("firm in your faith").**
  - Leighton: by faith, not by your own resolutions (Quote 5).
  - Calvin: "there is sufficient strength in faith."
  - MacArthur: with truth and obedience, not mystical "binding" (Quote 27a).
  - Brooks: resist at once, and do not debate: "It is safe to resist, it is dangerous to dispute. Eve disputes, and falls" (Quote 25b).
  - Spurgeon: some temptations call for flight, but "when Satan roars we most raise the shout and the war-cry" ("most" is the CCEL typo for "must"). He also gives the ship image (Quote 21b).
  - Henry: Satan's aim in persecution is apostasy, so "if your faith give way, you are gone."
  - Solidarity matters too. Calvin: "Satan dangerously tries us, when he separates us from the body of Christ."
- **Slight tension.** Spurgeon distinguishes flight (from seductive temptations) from standing (against roaring ones). Brooks and MacArthur stress standing and resisting. Leighton and Gill stress sobriety, meaning detachment from the world, as the precondition. They complement each other more than they disagree.

### (3) "Strengthen your brothers": Peter's commission fulfilled

The asleep-in-Gethsemane disciple now says "be watchful." The disciple Satan sifted now warns about Satan. The disciple told to "strengthen [*stērixon*] your brothers" (Luke 22:32) now promises that God "will confirm [*stērixei*] you." The net-mender's word (*katartizō*) becomes the promise of God's restoring work.
- Pulpit: "Peter remembers his Master's words."
- Bengel, quoted by Pulpit: "Digna Petro oratio, 'Confirmat fratres sues'" (Pulpit's typo for *suos*; meaning "A prayer worthy of Peter: he confirms his brothers").
- JFB: "His exhortation accords with his name Peter."
- Expositor's Bible: "The whole passage brings to mind Christ's injunction to the Apostle, 'When thou art converted, strengthen thy brethren'" (ch5_expositors.txt).

Mark, the deserter who was restored and is now "my son," sits next to Peter, the denier who was restored. Calvin: Peter calls him son "because he had begotten him in the faith." Henry: "his son in a spiritual sense."

Leighton on 5:10 makes the pastoral point: Peter ends teaching with prayer, because only God can do the strengthening. Calvin: "doctrine is in vain poured forth into the air, unless God works by his Spirit." The host can make this application: the "strengthener" does not claim the strengthening for himself. He points to "the God of all grace... himself."
