Restore us to yourself, O Lord, that we may be restored! Renew our days as of old, unless you have utterly rejected us, and you remain exceedingly angry with us.

That is the last line of Lamentations. Not a doxology. A question left hanging in the air.

And for centuries, when Jewish congregations read this book aloud, they would not let it end there. They read verse twenty-two, and then they went back and read verse twenty-one again. Restore us to yourself, O Lord. They could not bear to stop on the word angry.

John Calvin knew the custom and did not like it. But he thought he had found something in that last verse that no one before him had seen. By the end of this episode you can decide whether he was right.

This is Old Paths. Today is day twenty-five of the tenth month in the reading plan, day two hundred fifty of three hundred. The readings are Lamentations chapters four and five, the Song of Songs chapter eight, verses eight through fourteen, John chapter seven, verses twenty-five through thirty-six, and First Peter chapter five, verses eight through fourteen.

Three books end today. Lamentations, the Song of Songs, and First Peter all reach their final verse this morning.

Last time we sat with the man in the ruins of Lamentations three. Nothing outside him had changed, and yet he said, his mercies are new every morning. What changed was that he called something to mind. Today the poet stops speaking as one man and speaks for all of them. The I of chapter three becomes the we of chapter five. And the whole book ends on its knees.

We have been following two threads through this book, and I promised we would close them. The first is the little word like. Like a widow in chapter one. Like an enemy in chapter two. Like a bear and like a lion in chapter three. The second is comfort. Chapter one kept saying, there is none to comfort her, and chapter two could find no words to comfort her. Watch both threads today. They each get a last word.

Lamentations chapter four.

We are still in the years right after 586 B.C. Jerusalem has fallen to Nebuchadnezzar, the temple is burned, and the survivors are either in Babylon or scratching out a living in the ruins.

Chapter four opens with the same word as chapters one and two. Eikah. How. How the gold has grown dim, how the pure gold is changed! It is the third time the book has started a poem with that cry.

The shape has changed again, too. Chapter three was a triple acrostic, three lines for each letter. Chapter four goes back to a single alphabet with shorter stanzas than chapters one and two. You can almost hear the poet running out of breath.

And yes, the mouth comes before the eye one more time. Verse sixteen is the pe stanza, the letter whose name means mouth, and it begins with the Hebrew word for face, the face of the Lord. Verse seventeen is the ayin stanza, the letter whose name means eye, and what does it say? Our eyes failed, ever watching vainly for help. The poet has put the eye where the letter eye belongs.

Verse two. The precious sons of Zion, worth their weight in fine gold, are regarded as earthen pots. Remember Jeremiah's clay jar smashed in the valley in chapter nineteen. Gold has been treated like pottery, and pottery gets broken.

Verses three through ten are the famine. Even jackals nurse their young, but Zion's mothers have become like the ostrich, the proverbial careless mother. Matthew Henry explains why. They had no food, so they had no milk, and though their hearts were tender, they looked as heartless as the ostrich.

Then verse six, which is hard to hear. The chastisement of the daughter of my people has been greater than the punishment of Sodom, which was overthrown in a moment. Sodom died in one morning. Jerusalem died slowly, through a siege that dragged on month after month. Henry says she died by inches. D. A. Carson draws out the point underneath. There are degrees of guilt, and those who know the most have the least excuse. Sodom never had the temple or the prophets.

And verse ten. The hands of compassionate women have boiled their own children; they became their food. I will not dress that up. The verb for boiled is bashal, the same verb used in Second Kings six, during the siege of Samaria, when a woman tells the king, so we boiled my son. And Moses had warned of exactly this in Deuteronomy twenty-eight. The most tender and refined woman among you, he said, would eat her own children in the siege. Calvin notices the word compassionate. The poet does not call these women monsters. He calls them merciful, and that is the horror of it. That is what the curse of the covenant looks like when it finally falls.

Verse eleven names who did it. The Lord gave full vent to his wrath.

So why did it fall? Verse thirteen. This was for the sins of her prophets and the iniquities of her priests, who shed in the midst of her the blood of the righteous. Remember Jeremiah twenty-six, where priests and prophets demanded Jeremiah's death and the prophet Uriah was hunted down and killed.

Then the poet turns the leprosy law on them. In Leviticus thirteen, the leper had to cover his lip and cry out, unclean, unclean. Now the priests themselves stagger through the streets so defiled with blood that people shout at them, Away! Unclean! Do not touch! The men who were supposed to declare others clean have become the lepers.

Calvin calls the prophets and priests the two eyes of the church. Matthew Poole, the Puritan, takes the lesson straight to his own day, and to ours. "The ecclesiastical men were a great cause of the first and last destruction of Jerusalem, and so they are of most other places that come to ruin, through their neglect of their duty, or encouraging others in their wicked courses; which both showeth us how great a blessing to a people a godly, conscientious ministry is, and how great a plague and curse a ministry is which is otherwise."

Pray for your pastor. And if you are a man with any say in who teaches in your church, take that sentence seriously.

Now verses seventeen through twenty, and the false hopes. Our eyes failed, ever watching vainly for help; in our watching we watched for a nation which could not save. That is Egypt. In Jeremiah thirty-seven, Pharaoh's army marched out, the siege lifted for a moment, and then Pharaoh went home. Calvin says that whenever salvation is expected from others rather than from God alone, God is in a sense reduced to nothing.

Verse nineteen. They chased us on the mountains; they lay in wait for us in the wilderness. That is the night in Jeremiah thirty-nine when the wall was breached, and King Zedekiah slipped out through the king's garden toward the Arabah, and the Babylonians caught him on the plains of Jericho.

And verse twenty. The breath of our nostrils, the Lord's anointed, was captured in their pits, of whom we said, Under his shadow we shall live among the nations.

Here is our first hard question. Who is the breath of our nostrils?

Some Jewish interpreters said Josiah, the last good king. But Josiah was not captured. He was killed by the Egyptians, twenty-three years earlier. The man caught on the run is Zedekiah.

But that is strange, isn't it? Zedekiah broke his oath, ignored Jeremiah for eleven years, and led the city to ruin. How can anyone call him the breath of our nostrils? Calvin's answer is that the poet is honoring the office, not the man. Zedekiah was still the anointed son of David. When he was captured, the people felt the very breath go out of them.

The Greek translation of this verse, the one the early church read, came out as, the breath of our face, Christ the Lord. Christos Kyrios. So the fathers read it straight as a prophecy of Jesus. Irenaeus, in the second century, made something beautiful of the shadow. "For, as the shadow of bodies standing upright is upon the ground and is trodden upon, so also the body of Christ fell upon the ground by His sufferings and was trodden on indeed."

So who is right, Calvin or the fathers? I think Calvin honors both. In the history, this is Zedekiah, and you need to see him caught at Jericho. But the titles, the anointed, the breath of the people, the shadow they would live under, were promises God made to the house of David, and Zedekiah could not carry them. Calvin says those high terms properly belong to Christ only. And then he says this. "If, then, we desire to live before God, we must come to Christ, who is really the spirit or the breath of our nostrils; for as man that is dead does no longer breathe, so also we are said to be dead when separated from Christ."

That is our first move toward the New Testament, and it runs through the Davidic office, not around the text. Zedekiah failed his people. The Son of David who was trodden down did not.

Then the last two verses of chapter four. Rejoice and be glad, O daughter of Edom, you who dwell in the land of Uz; but to you also the cup shall pass. Edom was Esau's family, Judah's brother, and Psalm one hundred thirty-seven tells us Edom cheered when Jerusalem burned. Now Edom's turn with the cup is coming. Keep that land of Uz in your mind. You will meet it again tomorrow.

And then verse twenty-two. The punishment of your iniquity, O daughter of Zion, is accomplished; he will keep you in exile no longer.

This is the first time in the whole book that anyone has said a plain word of comfort to Zion. Accomplished is tam, finished, complete. Ellicott points out how close this is to Isaiah forty. Comfort, comfort my people, says your God. Speak tenderly to Jerusalem, and cry to her that her warfare is ended, that her iniquity is pardoned. There is the answer to the comfort thread. No one comforted her in chapter one. Here someone finally says, it is finished.

Henry is careful to say what that means. Not that Zion had paid all her sin deserved, but that she had borne all that God had designed for her. The trouble lasts exactly as long as God means it to.

Spurgeon preached on this verse in November 1862, and he heard more in it. "Christ has for His people borne all the punishment which they deserved. And now every soul for whom Christ died may read with exultation, The punishment of thine iniquity is accomplished. God is satisfied, and asks no more." That is our second and last New Testament move, and I make it on purpose. Zion's exile ended because God set a limit. Your guilt ends because Christ bore it.

Now chapter five, and the poem changes shape one last time. It still has twenty-two lines, the number of the alphabet, but the alphabet is gone. Some old versions title this chapter the prayer of Jeremiah. And listen to the sound of it in Hebrew. Line after line ends in nu, which means us, or our. Our inheritance, our homes, our mothers, our necks. Dozens of times. The whole chapter rhymes on us.

It is a communal prayer. Remember, O Lord, what has befallen us. Then the list. Our inheritance turned over to strangers. We pay for the water we drink. Slaves rule over us. Princes hung up by their hands, young men grinding at the mill like animals. The old men have left the city gate, the young men their music.

And verse three. We have become orphans, fatherless; our mothers are like widows.

There is the end of our like thread. The book began with Zion like a widow, sitting alone. Then God like an enemy, like a bear and a lion. And it ends with a city full of women like widows. The figure of speech from chapter one has become the plain fact of chapter five.

Then a puzzle. Verse seven. Our fathers sinned, and are no more; and we bear their iniquities. But verse sixteen says, The crown has fallen from our head; woe to us, for we have sinned! Which is it? Are they suffering for their fathers or for themselves?

Both, and they know it. Keil and Delitzsch put it well. God does not punish innocent children for their fathers' sins. He punishes children who carry on their fathers' sins. Verse seven is not an excuse. It is a plea for pity. Verse sixteen is the confession. And it is the only time in the chapter they say it. Woe to us, for we have sinned.

And then, verse nineteen, everything turns on one word. But. But you, O Lord, reign forever; your throne endures to all generations. In Hebrew that is word for word the line from Psalm one hundred two, verse twelve. You are enthroned forever. The crown has fallen from our head, but his has not. Calvin says that if the world were to change and perish a hundred times, it could not touch the unchangeableness of God.

Then verse twenty, why do you forget us forever? And the final two lines, which bring us to the second hard question. How does this book end?

Restore us to yourself, O Lord, that we may be restored! Renew our days as of old, unless you have utterly rejected us, and you remain exceedingly angry with us.

The trouble is two little Hebrew words, ki im. They can mean but, or although, or unless. The King James has, but thou hast utterly rejected us. Read that way, the book ends in despair.

Calvin admitted that on that reading the prophet had overstepped, as believers sometimes do in prayer. Then he offered his own reading. Unless. And Calvin could not resist adding, "it is singular that no one has perceived this." Read that way, the last line is an argument from the impossible. And Calvin's answer to that unless is flat. It cannot be. God will not be so angry with his people that he is never reconciled to them.

His own English editor thought this too refined, but our English Standard Version reads it his way.

Calvin called the synagogue custom of repeating verse twenty-one a superstition, a way of avoiding a bad omen. Matthew Henry was gentler. They did it, he said, so the sun might not set under a cloud. And Keil and Delitzsch quote an old commentator who said the hope at the end of this book is like the morning star seen through clouds. It does not drive away the night. It tells you the sun is coming.

I think the book ends exactly where it should. Not with a resolution, but with a prayer that has not been answered yet. You need to know who you are talking to.

And look closely at what they ask for. Restore us to yourself, O Lord, that we may be restored. In Hebrew it is a play on one verb, shuv, to turn or return. Hashivenu, turn us back. Venashuvah, and we shall turn back. It is the very same construction Ephraim prays in Jeremiah thirty-one. Bring me back that I may be restored.

Here the voices part ways. Calvin says this is not a prayer about repentance at all. It is God turning back to them in favor. Henry hears it the other way. They had complained that God had forsaken them, and yet they do not pray, turn to us, but turn us to you, admitting the distance was on their side. And Henry says, "So necessary is that grace that we may truly say, Turn us or we shall not be turned, but shall wander endlessly; and so powerful and effectual is that grace that we may as truly say, Turn us, and we shall be turned."

I side with Henry here, and so does Jeremiah thirty-one. Augustine, writing against the Pelagians, made the same point from the matching prayer in Psalm eighty, turn us again, O God. If turning to God were not God's own gift, he says, no one would ask him for it. Our Confession calls this effectual calling. God does not wait for us to come back. He brings us back, and then we come.

So here is the application, and it is the whole book in one sentence. Ask first to be restored to God himself. They had lost the land, the temple, the king, the walls, the music. And the first thing they asked for was not the land. It was him. Restore us to yourself.

What are you asking God to give back to you this week? Your health, your job, your marriage? Ask. But ask for him first. Renew our days comes second. Restore us to yourself comes first.

The Song of Songs, chapter eight, verses eight through fourteen, the end of the book.

Yesterday we stood at the summit, love strong as death. Now the poem comes down the mountain and back to her family home.

Verse eight. We have a little sister, and she has no breasts. What shall we do for our sister on the day when she is spoken for? These are almost certainly the brothers from chapter one, the ones who were angry with her and made her keep the vineyards. In her world, brothers guarded a sister's honor and had a strong voice in her marriage.

Verse nine is their plan. If she is a wall, we will build on her a battlement of silver, but if she is a door, we will enclose her with boards of cedar. A wall stands firm. A door can be opened. Keil and Delitzsch say it plainly. A girl who will not be seduced is like a wall. One who might be is like a door. If she proves a wall, they will crown her with silver. If she proves a door, they will board it shut.

The Scottish Presbyterian James Durham, reading the Song of Christ and the church, takes the door more kindly, as a weak heart that at least opens to Christ.

Verse ten is her answer. I was a wall, and my breasts were like towers; then I was in his eyes as one who finds peace. She kept herself. And notice the word peace, shalom. One verse later comes the name Solomon, Shelomoh, the man of peace. She found shalom in the eyes of Shelomoh.

The commentators argue over whether the brothers deserve the credit. Keil and Delitzsch think their stern care helped. The Cambridge Bible says their anxieties were idle, because she guarded herself. Fathers, you need both. Build the walls you can around your daughters. Jerome quoted this verse to a Roman mother about raising her little girl. But pray for the day she can say for herself, I was a wall.

Then verses eleven and twelve. Solomon had a vineyard at Baal-hamon. The name means lord of a multitude, and it is hard not to hear Solomon's harem in it. He let it out to keepers, and each one paid a thousand pieces of silver. She answers, My vineyard, my very own, is before me; you, O Solomon, may have the thousand. In chapter one she had not kept her own vineyard. Now it is hers, and she gives it freely. Love cannot be bought, we heard yesterday. Solomon's vineyard is rented. Hers is given.

Verse thirteen, and the speaker is him. O you who dwell in the gardens, with companions listening for your voice; let me hear it. Everyone else gets to hear her voice. He wants it too. Spurgeon preached on this in 1883 and turned it on us. "Believer, will you let the Lord Jesus, as it were, with tears in His eyes, say to you, You talk to everybody but to Me! You lay yourself out to please everybody but Me! You are a charming companion to everybody but to Me?"

And then the last verse of the book. Make haste, my beloved, and be like a gazelle or a young stag on the mountains of spices. The verb is barach, which usually means flee. Go swiftly. Back in chapter two she sent him away to the mountains of Bether, which may mean the mountains of separation. Now they are the mountains of spices. The book ends with motion, not stillness.

Why end there, on longing rather than arrival? Durham answers for the whole church. "That the most lively and longest continued enjoyments of Christ, that believers have here upon earth, may, and will have their interruptions and off breakings for a time: uninterrupted communion is reserved for heaven." Spurgeon and Gill both noticed that the Bible itself ends the same way. Surely I am coming soon. Amen. Come, Lord Jesus.

John chapter seven, verses twenty-five through thirty-six.

We are still in the temple at the Feast of Booths. Now some of the people of Jerusalem, the locals who know what the leaders are plotting, are confused. Is not this the man whom they seek to kill? And here he is, speaking openly, and they say nothing to him! Can it be that the authorities really know that this is the Christ?

Then their objection, verse twenty-seven. We know where this man comes from, and when the Christ appears, no one will know where he comes from. There really was a belief like that. In the second century Justin Martyr records a Jewish teacher named Trypho saying the Messiah would be unknown, even to himself, until Elijah came to anoint him. The Messiah would appear out of nowhere.

But read five verses ahead, to verse forty-two, where another group says the Christ must come from Bethlehem. The same crowd held both ideas, and they got Jesus wrong both ways. They thought he came from Nazareth, and he was born in Bethlehem. They thought they knew his origin, and his origin was the Father. Calvin says that is what happens when you read half your Bible. Micah five gives the Messiah's birthplace and his everlasting origin in the same verse. "Hence we are reminded how pernicious it is to mangle the Scriptures, and even Christ himself, so as not to admit more than the half of him."

So Jesus cried out in the temple. The word is ekraxen, a loud cry, and John uses it of Jesus only a few times, and the next one opens tomorrow's reading. You know me, and you know where I come from. But I have not come of my own accord. He who sent me is true, and him you do not know. True there means real, genuine. A real Father has sent a real Son, and the very people who boasted of knowing God did not know him.

Verse thirty. So they were seeking to arrest him, but no one laid a hand on him, because his hour had not yet come.

His hour runs through this whole Gospel. Not yet at Cana. Not yet here. Then in chapter twelve, the hour has come. Augustine warns us not to hear fate in that word, as if Jesus were waiting for the stars to line up. "The Lord was not born under fate. This is not to be believed concerning you, much less concerning Him by whom you were made. If your hour is His good will, what is His hour but His good will?" Calvin stresses the Father's decree instead. They are describing the same plan from two sides. Matthew Henry gives the practical result. God can tie men's hands even when he does not change their hearts.

Verse thirty-one, many believed, though they were still counting signs. The leaders send officers, temple police. In two episodes they will come back empty handed saying, no one ever spoke like this man.

And Jesus answers the arrest warrant. I will be with you a little longer, and then I am going to him who sent me. You will seek me and you will not find me. Where I am you cannot come. About six months remain until the cross. Henry says these words, like the pillar of cloud and fire, have a bright side and a dark side. There is such a thing as too late. Cyril of Alexandria has a picture for it. Painters showed Opportunity with long hair in front and bald behind. You can catch her as she comes. Once she is past, there is nothing to grab.

But Augustine reminds us that many in that very crowd were later cut to the heart at Pentecost and forgiven, so no one should despair.

Finally, verse thirty-five. Does he intend to go to the Dispersion among the Greeks and teach the Greeks? It is meant as a joke. Within a generation, that is exactly what his apostles did. The word Dispersion is diaspora, and it appears only three times in the New Testament. Here, in James, and in the first verse of the letter we finish today.

First Peter chapter five, verses eight through fourteen, the end of the letter.

Last time Peter said, cast all your anxieties on him, because he cares for you. Now, in the very next breath, be sober-minded; be watchful. Robert Leighton said God's children are freed from anxious care, but never from watchfulness. Sober-minded is nepho, and this is the third time Peter has used it, after chapters one and four. Watchful is gregoreo, stay awake. You can hear Gethsemane in it. Simon, are you asleep? Could you not watch one hour? The man who slept now tells the church to stay awake.

Your adversary the devil prowls around like a roaring lion, seeking someone to devour. Adversary is antidikos, an opponent in a lawsuit. Devil is diabolos, the slanderer.

Now set this beside yesterday. In Lamentations three, the man said God was like a bear lying in wait, like a lion in hiding. Peter has another lion in view. Leighton, the Scottish archbishop who wrote one of the finest commentaries on this letter, put the two side by side. "Faith sets the stronger Lion of the tribe of Judah, against this roaring lion of the bottomless pit; that delivering Lion, against this devouring lion."

Verse nine. Resist him, firm in your faith. Not by arguing with him. The Puritan Thomas Brooks gave this advice. "It is safe to resist, it is dangerous to dispute. Eve disputes, and falls in paradise, Job resists, and conquers upon the dunghill. He that will play with Satan's bait, will quickly be taken with Satan's hook." And Spurgeon, preaching on this verse in 1861, told the tried believer that Satan may worry, but he cannot rend. He may wound, but he cannot kill.

You are not alone in it, either. The same kinds of suffering are being experienced by your brotherhood throughout the world.

Then verse ten. And after you have suffered a little while, the God of all grace, who has called you to his eternal glory in Christ, will himself restore, confirm, strengthen, and establish you.

Look at two of those verbs. Restore is katartizo, the word Mark uses for James and John mending their nets. Peter knew what it was to patch a torn net. Confirm is sterizo, and that is the word Jesus used the night before the cross. Simon, Satan demanded to have you, but I have prayed for you, and when you have turned again, strengthen your brothers. Peter is doing exactly that. And notice he does not say, I will strengthen you. He says, God will himself.

Then the farewell. By Silvanus, that is Silas, Paul's companion, I have written briefly, declaring that this is the true grace of God. Stand firm in it.

Verse thirteen. She who is at Babylon, who is likewise chosen, sends you greetings, and so does Mark, my son.

Who is she, and where is Babylon? She is almost certainly a church. Babylon is harder. Eusebius, the church historian, says Peter wrote from Rome and called the city Babylon by a figure. But Calvin, Poole, Henry, and Gill argued for the real Babylon on the Euphrates, where many Jews still lived, partly to deny Rome's claim to Peter's chair. Poole remarks that some would rather go to hell to seek that chair than not find it. I think Rome is more likely. But either way, notice what Peter has done. He began the letter by calling his readers elect exiles of the dispersion. He ends it signing off from Babylon. We have spent two weeks in Jeremiah and Lamentations watching Babylon swallow Jerusalem. Now the church takes the name of its captor and uses it for the world it lives in, and it is not afraid.

And Mark, my son. The young man who deserted Paul, restored, sitting beside Peter, the man who denied Jesus, restored. Early tradition says Mark wrote down what Peter remembered. Two restored failures sending greetings to suffering churches.

Greet one another with the kiss of love. Peace to all of you who are in Christ.

Two connections before we go.

First, the Dispersion. In John seven the crowd mocked the idea that Jesus would go to the diaspora and teach the Greeks. Peter's letter is addressed to that diaspora, and he writes it from a city he calls Babylon. The joke in the temple became the mission field, and the destroyer of Zion became the name for the place where the church waits to go home.

Second, restoration. Lamentations ends with a prayer and an unless. Restore us to yourself, O Lord. Peter ends with a promise and no unless at all. The God of all grace will himself restore you. Lamentations asked the question in the dark. Peter, the restored disciple, answers it.

So here is your question to carry through the day. If God gave you back everything you have lost, but not himself, would that be enough for you?

Tomorrow the plan starts three new books. John seven, verses thirty-seven through forty-four, the great day of the feast and rivers of living water. Second Peter chapter one, verses one through eleven. Job chapter one, and remember the land of Uz from Lamentations four, because there was a man in the land of Uz. And Ezekiel chapters one through three, the prophet among the exiles in Babylon.

Calvin closed his last lecture on Lamentations with a short announcement to his students. "As it has so seemed good to the brethren, I will begin tomorrow the explanation of Ezekiel." So will we.

This has been Old Paths. Grace and peace.
