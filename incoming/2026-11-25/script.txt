Daniel chapter ten, verse twelve. Fear not, Daniel, for from the first day that you set your heart to understand and humbled yourself before your God, your words have been heard.

From the first day. And then nothing, for three weeks. No answer, no angel, no word. Daniel kept fasting into silence. Here is what he could not see. A war was being fought over his prayer.

This is Old Paths. Today is day twenty-five of the eleventh month in the reading plan, day two hundred seventy-five of three hundred. The readings are Daniel ten through twelve, Job twenty-two, John thirteen, verses thirty-one through thirty-eight, and Jude, verses seventeen through twenty-five.

Last time we heard Daniel's prayer from chapter nine, and Gabriel arriving at the hour of the evening sacrifice with the seventy sevens. And in Jude yesterday we met Michael the archangel, disputing with the devil over the body of Moses. Today Michael steps onto the stage in Daniel. And today Daniel and Jude both come to their final words.

Daniel ten through twelve.

These three chapters are one vision, the last in the book. Matthew Henry points out that it comes not in signs and figures, like the beasts of chapters seven and eight, but in plain words.

Verse one dates it to the third year of Cyrus, about 536 B.C. Two years earlier Cyrus had let the Jews go home. About fifty thousand went. They built the altar and laid the foundation of the temple. And then, Ezra four tells us, the people of the land discouraged them and hired counselors against them, all the days of Cyrus. The work stopped.

And Daniel did not go home. He is past eighty, still in Persia. Calvin thinks that is why he is mourning in verse two. The return he had prayed for in chapter nine had stalled. So for three weeks he eats no meat, drinks no wine, does not anoint himself. And notice the date in verse four, the twenty-fourth of the first month. His fast ran straight through Passover.

Then by the Tigris he sees a man clothed in linen, the dress of a priest. His face like lightning, his eyes like torches, his voice like a multitude. The men with Daniel do not see it but run and hide. Daniel falls on his face.

Who is this man? Matthew Henry and Hippolytus say it is the Son of God himself. Calvin says it is an angel. Hold that question. We will come back to it at the end.

A hand touches him and sets him trembling on his hands and knees. And then verse thirteen. The prince of the kingdom of Persia withstood me twenty-one days, but Michael, one of the chief princes, came to help me.

Now the hard question. Who is the prince of Persia? The Hebrew word is sar, a prince or commander. Jerome thought it was the angel to whom Persia had been entrusted, standing before God and listing Israel's sins as a reason to keep them in exile. Calvin thought it was simply the king, Cambyses, the son of Cyrus, and the angel was working against him on the ground. Ligonier's Tabletalk calls him a demon with strong influence over the Persian empire.

I lean toward the angelic reading, because the same word, sar, names Michael in the same verse, and Michael is no earthly king. Behind the counselors bribed against Jerusalem stood something darker. John Piper, writing on this text for Ligonier, noticed the numbers. It is no accident, he wrote, that the messenger's struggle with the prince of Persia lasted exactly as long as Daniel's fast, twenty-one days. And then he added the warning we need. But the point is this: Daniel's praying was not about angels. And probably ours shouldn't be either.

Daniel knew none of it. Twice the messenger calls him, O man greatly loved. Fear not, peace be with you, be strong.

Then chapter eleven, and it reads like a history book written in advance. Three more Persian kings and a fourth richer than all, that is Xerxes, who invaded Greece. Then a mighty king who rules with great dominion, Alexander, whose kingdom is broken to the four winds. Then the king of the south and the king of the north. Those are the Ptolemies of Egypt and the Seleucids of Syria, with Israel caught in the middle.

Then verse twenty-one. A contemptible person, who obtains the kingdom by flatteries. That is Antiochus the Fourth. He called himself Epiphanes, God made manifest. Henry tells us people called him Epimanes, the madman. In 167 B.C. he stopped the sacrifices, set up an altar to Zeus in the temple, and that is the abomination that makes desolate of verse thirty-one.

And then verse thirty-two, one of the great lines in the book. The people who know their God shall stand firm and take action. Spurgeon said the book of Maccabees is the best commentary on this verse. An old scribe who would not eat pork, a mother and her seven sons who died rather than deny God. They knew him, so they stood.

It is so precise that a pagan critic named Porphyry, around the year 270, argued that it must have been written after the fact, in the days of Antiochus. Jerome answered that the attack itself proves the point. The prophecy was so accurate that its enemies could only explain it by saying it was written after the events.

From verse thirty-six the king seems to grow larger than Antiochus. He magnifies himself above every god and comes to his end at the time of the end. Jerome read this as the Antichrist. Calvin read it as Rome. Ligonier's Tabletalk says the prophet moves from Antiochus as a man in history to Antiochus as a type of the final enemy. I think that is right. The one tyrant is a preview of the last.

And then chapter twelve. At that time shall arise Michael, the great prince. A time of trouble such as never has been. But your people shall be delivered, everyone whose name shall be found written in the book.

And verse two. Many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life, and some to shame and everlasting contempt.

Two words to notice. Everlasting life. That exact phrase appears nowhere else in the Old Testament. This is its first appearance in Scripture. And contempt, in Hebrew dera'on, means abhorrence, a thing you turn away from in disgust. It appears in only one other place, the last verse of Isaiah.

Calvin says the prophets do not contain any clearer testimony than this to the last resurrection, particularly as the angel distinctly asserts the future rising again of both the righteous and the wicked. And Jesus took up this verse when he said that an hour is coming when all who are in the tombs will hear his voice and come out, some to the resurrection of life and some to judgment.

Jerome enjoyed this moment against Porphyry. Up until this point, he wrote, Porphyry somehow managed to maintain his position. But what can he say of this chapter, in which is described the resurrection of the dead, with one group being revived for eternal life and the other group for eternal disgrace?

Verse three. Those who are wise shall shine like the brightness of the sky. Those are the wise of chapter eleven, the ones who taught many and fell by the sword. And those who turn many to righteousness, like the stars. That phrase echoes Isaiah fifty-three, where the servant makes many to be accounted righteous.

Then the ending. Two figures on the banks ask, how long? Time, times, and half a time. And verse eight is the most honest line in the book. I heard, but I did not understand. Daniel, the man who read dreams for kings, does not understand. Then come twelve hundred ninety days, and thirteen hundred thirty-five. I will not pretend to give you a chart. Godly readers have never agreed on them.

What Daniel gets is not a chart. Verse thirteen. But go your way till the end. And you shall rest and shall stand in your allotted place at the end of the days. Rest means he will die. Stand means he will rise. Allotted place is the word for a lot, like the lots that divided the land under Joshua. The old exile never got back to his piece of Canaan. God tells him he has one waiting.

So here is the application. You will not understand every number in this book. Neither did Daniel. You do not need the timeline. You need to know that your name is written and your lot is kept. Then go your way. Do the ordinary next thing, and pray, and when it is time, rest.

Job twenty-two.

This opens the third and last round of speeches, and it is Eliphaz's last word. He has four moves. Verses two through four, God gains nothing from you. Verses five through eleven, the charges. Verses twelve through twenty, you think God cannot see. Verses twenty-one through thirty, come back to God and be restored.

The charges are new. For the first time a friend names specific sins. You stripped the naked, withheld bread from the hungry, sent widows away empty. Every one is false. Job will answer them line by line in chapter thirty-one. Eliphaz has no evidence. He reasoned backward from the suffering. Spurgeon said they read virtue off prosperity and sin off adversity, and he called that cruel logic.

Then verse twenty-one, which sounds beautiful. Agree with God, and be at peace. The King James says, acquaint now thyself with him. And verse twenty-five. Then the Almighty will be your gold.

But listen to what Eliphaz is really offering. Come back to God, and he will rebuild you, and you will be rich again. That is exactly what Satan said back in chapter one. Does Job fear God for nothing? Without knowing it, Eliphaz is arguing the devil's case, that people serve God for what they get.

Gregory the Great saw both sides. Now these things Eliphaz would have spoken aright, if he had been admonishing one that was weak; but when he looks down upon a righteous man on account of his scourges, what is this, but that he pours out words of knowledge in not knowing?

And the last verse is pure irony. Eliphaz says God will deliver even the guilty through the cleanness of your hands. At the end of the book, God will spare Eliphaz himself, because Job prays for him.

So, practically, true words aimed at the wrong person become cruel words. Before you tell a suffering friend what God is doing, be sure you know.

John thirteen, verses thirty-one through thirty-eight.

When he had gone out. Judas is gone into the night, and Jesus says, now. Now is the Son of Man glorified. In two verses he uses the word glory five times, and he is talking about the cross. Calvin calls the cross a magnificent theater, where the goodness of God is put on display before the whole world.

Then verse thirty-three. Little children. The word is teknia, a tender word, the only time Jesus uses it in John's Gospel. Then the new commandment, that you love one another, just as I have loved you. In Latin, mandatum novum, which is why we call it Maundy Thursday.

But love your neighbor is as old as Leviticus. What is new? Chrysostom answered. He made it new Himself by the manner; therefore He added, As I have loved you. I have not paid back to you a debt of good deeds first done by you, but Myself have begun, He says.

R. C. Sproul pointed to the timing. Judas has just walked out. So Jesus, Sproul wrote, demanded from His disciples and all who would follow Him a love that has no place for treason.

And then Peter. Lord, where are you going? You cannot follow me now. Augustine noticed the word now. Jesus did not say you cannot. He said you cannot now. Delay, but not despair. Peter says, I will lay down my life for you. And Jesus turns the sentence back on him. Will you lay down your life for me? Augustine hears the question underneath it. Would Peter do for Jesus what Jesus had not yet done for Peter? Before the rooster crows, Peter will deny him three times. Peter meant every word. But a vow is not strength.

Jude, verses seventeen through twenty-five.

Jude finishes today. He has spent the letter on false teachers. Now he turns twice with the words, but you, beloved.

First, remember. The apostles warned that scoffers would come, and Jude's wording is almost exactly Second Peter three. They are worldly, he says, devoid of the Spirit.

Then the heart of the passage, verses twenty and twenty-one. Building yourselves up in your most holy faith, praying in the Holy Spirit, keep yourselves in the love of God, waiting for the mercy of our Lord Jesus Christ that leads to eternal life. Piper points out that there is only one command there. Keep yourselves. The rest describe how. And then he ties it back to the first verse, where Jude wrote to those kept for Jesus Christ. Piper put it this way. Keep yourselves in the love of God, for God is the one who keeps you in his love.

Verses twenty-two and twenty-three give three kinds of people. Doubters, show them mercy. Some are in the fire, snatch them out. Others, show mercy with fear, hating even the garment stained by the flesh. That is Zechariah three, where the high priest Joshua is a brand plucked from the fire, standing in filthy garments. And in that same chapter the Lord says, the Lord rebuke you, Satan. That is the line Michael used in Jude nine. Jude has had that chapter in his mind all along.

Then the doxology. Now to him who is able to keep you from stumbling. Spurgeon has a sermon on it, published in 1893, the year after he died, and he noticed the word stumbling. A horse can stumble without falling, he said, but stumbling leads to falling, and God is able to guard you even from that.

Here is the real connection today. Peter promised to keep himself, and he could not. Jude says there is one who is able to keep you. Daniel was told that his people would be delivered, everyone whose name is written in the book. Daniel twelve gives us the phrase everlasting life for the first time, and Jude ends with the mercy that leads to eternal life. Peter would follow afterward. Daniel would stand in his lot. Neither one got there by his own vow.

So carry this through the day. What are you trusting to keep you, your own promise or the one who is able?

Revelation opens with Daniel's son of man coming with the clouds. Very soon John will see a man clothed to the feet, with eyes like fire and feet like burnished bronze. Then you can decide who stood by the Tigris.
