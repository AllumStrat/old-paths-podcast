Daniel chapter seven, verse thirteen. I saw in the night visions, and behold, with the clouds of heaven there came one like a son of man.

Daniel has watched animals climb out of the sea. A lion with wings. A bear with ribs in its teeth. A monster with iron teeth. And then, finally, a man. Not from the sea, but from heaven. So a question before we start. When the news is all beasts, where are you looking?

This is Old Paths. Today is day twenty-three of the eleventh month in the reading plan, day two hundred seventy-three of three hundred. The readings are Daniel seven and eight, Job twenty-one, verses one through twenty-one, John thirteen, verses twelve through seventeen, and Jude, verses one through seven. Jude is a new book.

Last time we stood in Belshazzar's banquet hall on his last night alive, and heard Daniel say, though you knew all this. Today the book steps backward. Chapter seven opens in the first year of Belshazzar. The court stories are finished. The visions begin.

Daniel seven and eight.

First, where we are. Chapters one through six were stories about Daniel in the courts of pagan kings. Chapters seven through twelve are visions Daniel himself received. The date is about 553 B.C., the year King Nabonidus left Babylon for the Arabian desert and put his son Belshazzar in charge. Babylon looks permanent. God shows an old exile what it really is.

One literary detail. Since chapter two, verse four, the book has been written in Aramaic, the language of the empire, and it switches back to Hebrew at chapter eight. So the Aramaic section opens with four kingdoms in Nebuchadnezzar's statue and closes with four kingdoms in these beasts. It is the same history told twice. In chapter two the empires look like gold and silver, the way a king sees himself. In chapter seven they look like predators, the way heaven sees them.

Verse two. The four winds of heaven were stirring up the great sea. In the Old Testament the sea is chaos, the restless deep that only God can rule. Out of it come four beasts. The first is a lion with eagles' wings. The wings are plucked, it is made to stand like a man, and the mind of a man is given to it. The church father Hippolytus saw Nebuchadnezzar there, the king of chapter four, who lived like an animal until he looked up to heaven and was restored.

The second is a bear raised up on one side, Media and Persia, with Persia the stronger partner. The third is a leopard with four wings and four heads, Alexander's Greek empire, fast as a leopard, divided among four generals after his death. And the fourth beast has no animal name at all. Iron teeth, bronze claws, ten horns. Like the iron legs of the statue, the old church and the Reformers alike took it to be Rome.

Then verse nine, and the camera swings from the sea to heaven. Thrones were placed, and the Ancient of Days took his seat. Hair like wool, a throne of flame, ten thousand times ten thousand standing before him. The court sat in judgment, and the books were opened.

Now verse eleven. I looked then because of the sound of the great words that the horn was speaking. Down on earth a little horn with a big mouth is talking. Up in heaven the court is seated. The horn speaks great words. The court opens books. And the beast is killed.

And then our verse. One like a son of man. The Aramaic is bar enash, a human being. After four beasts, a man. After the sea, the clouds. Some Jewish interpreters said this figure is simply Israel, because the saints receive the kingdom in verse eighteen. A Ligonier devotional answers that well. Since the son of man in today's passage is a singular entity, he could represent Israel but Israel itself cannot be the son of man. And then, He rides on the clouds of heaven, and only God uses the clouds as a chariot. A man who travels as only God travels.

Here is the first hard question. Is this the second coming? Read it closely. He came to the Ancient of Days and was presented before him. He is not coming down to earth. He is going up to the throne. Calvin was convinced this is the ascension, because that is when Christ began his reign. Henry and Hippolytus read it of the last judgment. I think Calvin has the direction right. The Son of Man received his kingdom when he ascended, and his return will make it plain to every eye. When the high priest asked Jesus whether he was the Christ, in Mark fourteen, he answered with this verse. You will see the Son of Man seated at the right hand of Power, and coming with the clouds of heaven. His favorite name for himself comes from here. And notice verse twenty-seven. The kingdom given to the Son of Man is then given to the people of the saints. The King shares it.

Chapter eight. Two years later, and back in Hebrew. Daniel sees himself in Susa, by the Ulai canal. A ram with two horns, the higher one coming up last. Then a goat from the west, crossing the earth without touching the ground, with one great horn between its eyes. The goat smashes the ram. At the height of its strength the great horn is broken, and four come up in its place. This time Gabriel gives the names. The ram is the kings of Media and Persia. The goat is the king of Greece. That is Alexander the Great, who died at thirty-two in 323 B.C., and his empire split four ways.

Then out of one of the four comes a little horn. He takes away the regular burnt offering and throws truth to the ground. This is Antiochus the Fourth, a Greek king of Syria. He called himself Epiphanes, God made manifest. The historian Polybius, as an old commentary notes, called him Epimanes, the madman. In 167 B.C. he set up an altar to Zeus in the temple at Jerusalem, and three years later Judas Maccabaeus cleansed it. How long, asks the holy one. Two thousand three hundred evenings and mornings. Count it as days or as sacrifices, either way God had set the clock before the madman was born.

That brings the second hard question. Are the little horn of chapter seven and the little horn of chapter eight the same man? In the third century a pagan named Porphyry said yes. Both are Antiochus, he argued, and the book was written after the fact. That is still the standard critical view. Jerome answered him from chapter seven itself. Let Porphyry answer the query, Jerome wrote, of whom out of all mankind this language might apply to. If he says Judas Maccabaeus beat Antiochus, then he must explain how Judas could be said to come with the clouds of heaven like unto the Son of man, and to be brought unto the Ancient of days.

So the horns are different. The horn of chapter eight grows out of the Greek goat. The horn of chapter seven grows out of the fourth beast, the one after Greece. Calvin took it as the Caesars who persecuted the church. Jerome and Hippolytus saw the final Antichrist. But the two horns rhyme. Antiochus is a preview of the pattern. A proud mouth, war on the saints, a limited time, and a sudden end. He shall be broken, verse twenty-five says, but by no human hand. Like the stone of chapter two.

Hippolytus gave the wisest sentence in the whole debate. So that we ought not to anticipate the counsel of God, but exercise patience and prayer, that we fall not on such times.

And look at Daniel in the last verse. I was overcome and lay sick for some days. Then I rose and went about the king's business, but I was appalled by the vision and did not understand it. The prophet who saw the most did not understand all of it. Spurgeon, preaching on Daniel in 1878, called him a man of many visions, and then added, his visions did not make him visionary.

So here is the application. These chapters were not given so that you could draw a chart. They were given so that you could stand up. The beasts are real, and the little horn talks big. But the court is seated, the books are open, and the kingdom has been handed to a man who rode the clouds to his Father's throne. You do not need to identify every horn to know who holds the kingdom. Look there, and then go about the King's business.

Job twenty-one.

Job finally answers Zophar directly, and this is the boldest speech so far. He begins, keep listening, and after I have spoken, mock on. Then the thesis, verse seven. Why do the wicked live, reach old age, and grow mighty in power?

Then he paints them. Their children are established, their houses safe, their cattle fruitful, their little ones dancing to the pipe. And verse thirteen, they spend their days in prosperity, and in peace they go down to Sheol. No long agony, no visible curse.

Then their creed, verse fourteen. They say to God, depart from us. We do not desire the knowledge of your ways. What is the Almighty, that we should serve him? Gregory the Great said, To say this in words even foolish men have not the boldness, yet all wicked persons say to God, not by their words but by their ways, Depart from us. Most people who push God away never say it out loud. They just live as though he had left.

Then verse seventeen. How often is it that the lamp of the wicked is put out? Albert Barnes points out that Job is quoting Bildad, who said in chapter eighteen that the light of the wicked is put out. Job asks, how often? Show me. And when they answer that God stores up punishment for the children, Job says that is not justice for the man himself.

Is Job denying that God is just? No. He is denying the friends' timetable. Matthew Henry's answer is that God lets the wicked prosper this way to show there is another world, which is the world of retribution, and not this one. So the practical wisdom is in two halves. Do not read anyone's heart from their bank account. And do not decide God is unjust because the reckoning has not come yet.

John thirteen, verses twelve through seventeen.

Last time Jesus knelt with the towel. Now he puts his outer garments back on, reclines at the table again, and asks, do you understand what I have done to you? You call me Teacher and Lord, and you are right, for so I am.

Then the argument. If I then, your Lord and Teacher, have washed your feet, you also ought to wash one another's feet. It is an argument from the greater to the less. If the highest does the lowest work, no one else gets to say it is beneath them.

So did Jesus institute a third sacrament? A few traditions have thought so. R. C. Sproul said no, because the cleansing this pictures belongs to baptism, the sign of entrance into the covenant. Calvin mocked the yearly ceremony in Rome, where feet were washed once and brothers despised the rest of the year. Christ commanded no ritual, he says, but a readiness, all our lives long, to serve our brothers and neighbors. Augustine adds a balance. He did think it good to do the literal thing at times, because when the body bends at a brother's feet, humility is awakened in the heart.

Chrysostom noticed the word as. Do just as I have done. But we are not the Lord. So what does as mean? With the same zeal, he says. Then this lovely line. Thus schoolmasters write the letters for children very beautifully, that they may come to imitate them though but in an inferior manner. Jesus wrote the letter perfectly. Your copy will wobble. Copy it anyway.

And then verse seventeen. If you know these things, blessed are you if you do them. Chrysostom again. To know belongs to all, but to do, not to all.

Jude, verses one through seven.

Now a new book, only twenty-five verses. Its author calls himself Jude, a servant of Jesus Christ and brother of James. That James led the church in Jerusalem, and Mark six names both James and Judas among the brothers of Jesus. So Jude grew up in the same house as the Lord. Clement of Alexandria noticed that Jude, knowing that relationship, did not call himself the Lord's brother. He called himself his servant. He probably wrote in the sixties of the first century, and his letter runs very close to Second Peter chapter two.

Verse three tells you why the letter exists. Jude meant to write a warm letter about our common salvation. He had to change plans. Contend for the faith that was once for all delivered to the saints. The faith here is not your trust. It is the content, the body of truth, handed over once and finished. Henry says nothing can be added to it and nothing taken away.

Why the change? Verse four. Certain people have crept in unnoticed. They pervert the grace of our God into sensuality. They do not deny grace. They love grace. They just turn it into permission.

Then three examples from the past. Israel saved from Egypt and then destroyed in the wilderness for unbelief. Angels who did not keep their position, whom God has kept in chains. And Sodom. And notice verse five in your English Bible. Jesus, who saved a people out of the land of Egypt. Many of the oldest manuscripts read Jesus there. Jude puts the Lord of the Exodus and the man Jesus in one name.

And why remind them, when they already knew it? Calvin, on verse five, says that the use of God's word is not only to teach what we could not have otherwise known, but also to rouse us to a serious meditation of those things which we already understand, and not to suffer us to grow torpid in a cold knowledge.

Here is the connection. It is about knowing and doing. The wicked in Job say, we do not desire the knowledge of your ways. Jude writes to people who once fully knew it, and warns them that knowledge can go cold. Jesus says, if you know these things, blessed are you if you do them. And Daniel, who did not understand his vision, still rose and did the king's business. Knowing God was never meant to stop at knowing.

So carry this question through the day. When the news roars today, will you watch the sea, or the throne?
