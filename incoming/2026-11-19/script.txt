Daniel chapter three, verse eighteen. But if not, be it known to you, O king, that we will not serve your gods.

Three men stand in front of a furnace. They believe God can save them. They do not know whether he will. They have a few seconds left to decide, and they do not need them. So here is the question for today. Is your obedience a deal with God, or is it simply obedience?

This is Old Paths. Today is day twenty-one of the eleventh month in the reading plan, day two hundred seventy-one of three hundred. The readings are Daniel three and four, Job nineteen, John twelve, verses thirty-seven through fifty, and Second John.

Last time, Nebuchadnezzar fell on his face before Daniel and said, your God is God of gods. And I told you to watch what he built next. He dreamed of a statue with a head of gold, and he was told that his kingdom would pass. Today he builds a statue that is gold from top to bottom. In Job, I promised you twice that the answer to where then is my hope was coming. It comes today. And in John, Jesus has just hidden himself from the crowd. His public ministry is over, and John tells us why it ended the way it did.

Daniel three and four.

First, where we are. At the end of chapter two the king promoted Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego over the province of Babylon. Daniel stays at court, and he is simply absent from chapter three. The Aramaic text gives no date, but the early Greek translations place this in Nebuchadnezzar's eighteenth year. That is 587 B.C., the year Jerusalem burned. If that tradition is right, then while the temple of the Lord lay in ashes, the king of Babylon raised a golden image on the plain of Dura, about ninety feet tall and nine feet wide, probably gold plate over a frame. Nobody today knows exactly where Dura was. Everybody knew what it meant.

Notice how the chapter is written. The list of officials appears three times. The list of instruments, horn, pipe, lyre, trigon, harp, bagpipe, appears four times. It sounds like a machine, because it is one. The whole empire kneels when the music starts. Then, in verse fifteen, the king asks the question of the chapter. Who is the god who will deliver you out of my hands?

Verse sixteen. We have no need to answer you in this matter. That is not rudeness. It means the question was settled before it was asked. Calvin recalls that Cyprian, the bishop of Carthage, answered the same way when courtiers begged him to save his life. In a matter that sacred, Cyprian said, there is nothing to deliberate.

And they had excuses ready, if they had wanted them. Calvin and Spurgeon both name the best one. We are in high office. We protect our people. If we die, wicked men take our places. We can do more good alive.

Then the famous words. Our God whom we serve is able to deliver us. And he will deliver us out of your hand, O king. But if not. Bryan Chapell, the Presbyterian pastor, preaching on this chapter, says these are great men of faith who say, Our trust is in what God does, not what we want to happen. They are sure of God's power. They are not sure of his plan. And their obedience does not wait to find out.

The furnace is heated seven times hotter, and the soldiers who throw them in are killed by the heat. Then the king jumps to his feet. Did we not cast three men bound into the fire? I see four men unbound, walking in the midst of the fire, and the appearance of the fourth is like a son of the gods.

So here is the hard question. Who is the fourth man?

Start with the words. In Aramaic it is bar elahin, a son of the gods, and elahin is plural. The Cambridge Bible says the old rendering, the Son of God, cannot stand as a translation. A pagan king is describing a heavenly being in pagan terms. And three verses later he calls this figure God's angel. Calvin takes it that way. God sent one of his angels to keep these men company in the fire, and to claim the king spoke by some inspired impulse, Calvin says, would be forced and frigid.

But the early church read it differently. Hippolytus of Rome, writing around the year 200, noticed something in verse twenty-six. The king calls out the three by name and stops. Hippolytus writes, The three youths he thus called by name. But he found no name by which to call the fourth. For He was not yet that Jesus born of the Virgin.

Jerome splits the difference. An angel in the history, he says, and a picture of Christ in its meaning. And John Gill, the Baptist, thinks it highly probable that it was the Son himself, and then adds this. It was better for these good men to be with Christ in the fiery furnace, than to be with Nebuchadnezzar in his palace without him.

Here is where I land. Nebuchadnezzar did not know what he was seeing. But in the Old Testament, the angel of the Lord who stands with God's people is very often the Lord himself. Matthew Poole calls him the Angel of the covenant. So the old reading is not fanciful, and I hold it with an open hand. What I hold without hesitation is the promise of Isaiah forty-three. When you walk through fire you shall not be burned. God did not keep these men out of the furnace. He met them in it. And Hebrews eleven remembers them among the faithful who quenched the power of fire.

Chapter four is written as the king's own proclamation to all peoples, nations, and languages. And watch the point of view. He tells the dream in the first person. When the madness comes, in verses twenty-eight through thirty-three, the story shifts to the third person, because the man telling it is not there to tell it. Then at verse thirty-four the first person returns, at the exact moment he says, my reason returned to me.

The dream is a great tree that feeds the world, and a watcher comes down from heaven. The Aramaic word is ir, a wakeful one. Theodoret of Cyrus explains that angels need no sleep. The watcher's sentence gives the point of the whole book in verse seventeen, repeated in twenty-five and thirty-two. The Most High rules the kingdom of men and gives it to whom he will.

Daniel is dismayed, and then he gives counsel. Break off your sins by practicing righteousness, and your iniquities by showing mercy to the oppressed. Jerome and Chrysostom read this as alms making up for sin. Calvin argues that the verb means to break off, so Daniel is calling for repentance, shown in justice toward the poor the king had crushed. I think Calvin is right. Daniel is not selling pardon. He is telling a tyrant to stop.

God waits twelve months. Then the king is walking on his palace roof. Is not this great Babylon, which I have built by my mighty power? Archaeologists have pulled thousands of bricks out of Babylon's ruins, stamped with his name. Nebuchadnezzar, son of Nabopolassar. He signed the city. And while the words are still in his mouth, a voice falls from heaven. A Ligonier devotional sums it up in one line. A man who thought himself a god was made a beast to learn that he was but a man.

Then verse thirty-four. At the end of the days I, Nebuchadnezzar, lifted my eyes to heaven, and my reason returned to me. Jerome noticed the order. The eyes go up first, and then the mind comes back.

Was he converted? Matthew Henry says there can be no reasonable doubt. Calvin is more reserved, and says the edict does not show a man long trained in God's school. But notice what is new. Before, he praised God's wisdom and God's power. Now, for the first time, he confesses his own sin. Those who walk in pride he is able to humble. I am hopeful. But the book's point is not finally his biography. It is that the Most High rules.

Here is the application. Settle the but if not before you get to the furnace. Most of us obey on the assumption that God will make it work out. Decide today what you will do if he does not. And when the Babylon you have built starts to look like your own work, do what the king finally did. Lift your eyes.

Job nineteen.

This is the turning point of the book. Job begins at the bottom. God has walled up my way. He has stripped my crown. He has pulled up my hope like a tree. Then the list of everyone who has left him. Brothers, guests, servants, his wife, even small children. Have mercy on me, have mercy on me, O you my friends. Why do you, like God, pursue me?

Then he wishes his words were cut into rock with an iron pen and filled with lead. Albert Barnes points out the irony. That wish was granted, and more permanently than stone. You read those words this morning.

And what are those words? For I know that my Redeemer lives. The Hebrew is goel, the next of kin who buys back the family land, avenges a murdered relative, and keeps a dead man's name alive. Job has already asked for an umpire, a witness, and a surety. Now he names a kinsman. Job is dying, and his Redeemer lives. At the last he will stand upon the dust, the dust of Job's grave.

The hard question is verse twenty-six. After my skin has been destroyed, yet in my flesh I shall see God. The Hebrew preposition can mean from my flesh or away from my flesh, and the Cambridge Bible prefers without my flesh. But verse twenty-seven decides it for me. My eyes shall behold, and not another. Eyes are bodily. One of the earliest Christian letters outside the New Testament reads it the same way. Clement of Rome, writing to Corinth near the end of the first century, gathers proofs of the resurrection and writes, and again, Job says, You shall raise up this flesh of mine, which has suffered all these things.

That is the wisdom here. Job does not know why he suffers. He does know who will stand over his grave.

John twelve, verses thirty-seven through fifty.

The public ministry is over, and John writes the verdict. Though he had done so many signs before them, they still did not believe. Then two texts from Isaiah. Isaiah fifty-three, Lord, who has believed what he heard from us? And Isaiah six, he has blinded their eyes. Then the hardest sentence. Therefore they could not believe.

How is that just? The fathers answer together. Augustine says they could not because they would not. And Chrysostom writes, He says not that the doing of virtue is impossible to them, but that because they will not, therefore they cannot.

Sproul, preaching this passage, puts it sharply. Satan saw Lazarus come out of the tomb, and it did not convert him, because it is not that Satan does not know the truth. It is that he hates the truth. More evidence never cures that. And then the judgment, Sproul says, is that God turns the sinner over to his sin.

Verse forty-one. Isaiah said these things because he saw his glory. Isaiah six is the throne, high and lifted up. John says the one Isaiah saw was Jesus.

Then a sad note. Many of the authorities believed, but would not confess, for fear of being put out of the synagogue. They loved the glory that comes from man more than the glory that comes from God. Do not miss the word. Isaiah saw the glory of the Lord on his throne. These men saw it too, in signs and in a raised man named Lazarus, and they traded it for the good opinion of a council. Secret faith that will not speak is not where John wants you to stay. And Jesus' last public words. Whoever sees me sees him who sent me. I did not come to judge the world but to save the world. But the word I have spoken will judge on the last day.

Second John.

This is a letter short enough for one sheet of papyrus. The elder writes to the elect lady and her children. Clement of Alexandria says she was a lady named Electa, and that the name points to the election of the church. Most readers think she is a congregation, since verse thirteen sends greetings from her elect sister.

The word truth appears five times in the first four verses. And love is defined, verse six, as walking according to his commandments. Then the danger. Many deceivers have gone out into the world, those who do not confess the coming of Jesus Christ in the flesh. And the instruction. If anyone comes to you and does not bring this teaching, do not receive him into your house.

Early Christianity depended on hospitality. Traveling teachers lodged in believers' homes, and a bed and a meal were an endorsement. Irenaeus passes on a story that came down through Polycarp about the apostle John himself. John went to bathe at Ephesus, saw the false teacher Cerinthus inside, and rushed out, exclaiming, Let us fly, lest even the bath-house fall down, because Cerinthus, the enemy of the truth, is within.

Does that mean no kindness to anyone who errs? No. Barnes says we are to be honest, kind, and just with such people as neighbors, but we must do nothing that sponsors them as teachers. A Ligonier devotional adds that John is not forbidding contact with unbelievers or efforts to persuade false teachers. He is forbidding endorsement. Notice that this is the same letter that says love one another. For John, truth and love are not rivals. Love that will not guard the truth leaves the children of the house exposed. So ask where your hospitality, your platform, and your money go.

And the letter ends with longing. I would rather not use paper and ink. I hope to talk face to face.

Here is the thread running through today, and it is in the texts. Every reading turns on seeing. Nebuchadnezzar lifts his eyes to heaven and gets his mind back. Job says, my eyes shall behold, and not another. John says Isaiah saw his glory, and Jesus says, whoever sees me sees him who sent me. And the elder wants to see his friends face to face. Pride looks at what it built. Faith looks up. The three men in the fire saw a fourth. Job, with nothing left, saw a Redeemer standing on his dust.

So carry this question through the day. If God does not do what you are hoping for, what will you still refuse to bow to?
