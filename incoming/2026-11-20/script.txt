Daniel chapter three, verse fifteen. And who is the god who will deliver you out of my hands?

The most powerful man on earth asks it, standing beside a furnace hot enough to kill the men who throw prisoners in. Three young men answer him. Our God is able. And then three words that have steadied believers for twenty-five centuries. But if not.

Would your faith survive those three words?

This is Old Paths. Today is day twenty-one of the eleventh month in the reading plan, day two hundred seventy-one of three hundred. The readings are Daniel three and four, Job nineteen, John twelve, verses thirty-seven through fifty, and the short letter of Second John, which begins and ends today.

Last time Nebuchadnezzar fell on his face and confessed, your God is God of gods. I told you to watch what he did next. Today he builds a statue, and this time it is gold from head to foot. Job asked, where then is my hope, and Bildad told him he did not know God. Today, twice promised, Job answers both of them. And in John, the public ministry of Jesus comes to its end.

Daniel three and four.

Chapter three carries no date. The old Greek translations put it in Nebuchadnezzar's eighteenth year, which would be about 586 B.C., the very year his army was burning Jerusalem. We cannot be sure of that. But the picture is fitting. While the city of God lies in ashes, the king of Babylon raises his own image on the plain of Dura.

The image is sixty cubits by six, roughly ninety feet high and nine feet wide, almost certainly wood overlaid with gold. The king had dreamed of a statue with a head of gold, and Daniel told him, you are that head. So he builds a statue that is gold all the way down. No silver kingdom after him. No iron. Babylon forever.

Now listen to how Daniel tells it. The list of officials appears twice, the list of instruments four times, and the phrase the image that King Nebuchadnezzar has set up again and again. The repetition is deliberate. It is the drone of empire. Everyone gathers, the music plays, everyone falls down. Matthew Henry puts it with his usual bite. There is nothing so bad which the careless world will not be drawn to by a concert of music, or driven to by a fiery furnace.

Then the accusation and the king's challenge. He is not only threatening three men. He is calling out their God.

Verses sixteen through eighteen. We have no need to answer you in this matter. If this be so, our God whom we serve is able to deliver us from the burning fiery furnace, and he will deliver us out of your hand, O king. But if not, be it known to you, O king, that we will not serve your gods or worship the golden image that you have set up.

Does but if not mean they doubted God's power? No. Keil points out that the word for able here speaks of what God may rightly do, not whether he is strong enough. They never questioned his power. They did not know his will. So they held two things together, complete certainty about who God is and what they must do, and real humility about what God would choose. Spurgeon, preaching on these verses in 1891, put his finger on it. They did not base their loyalty to God upon their deliverance. And then he says, No, they would serve Him for nothing! Theirs was not cupboard love.

The furnace is heated seven times hotter, and the men who throw them in are killed. Then the king leaps up. Did we not cast three men bound into the fire? But I see four men unbound, walking in the midst of the fire, and the appearance of the fourth is like a son of the gods.

Here is the first hard question. Who is the fourth man? The Aramaic is bar elahin, a son of the gods, and in the king's mouth it means a heavenly being. In verse twenty-eight he calls him God's angel. Calvin takes it that way. God sent one of his angels, and the king spoke as pagans did. Jerome leans the same way, and says honestly that he does not see how an ungodly king could have merited a vision of the Son of God.

But many of the early church took the fourth to be the Son himself, and so do Matthew Poole and John Gill. Hippolytus, writing about the year 200, noticed something in verse twenty-six. The king calls the three out by name, Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego. Hippolytus writes, The three youths he thus called by name. But he found no name by which to call the fourth. For He was not yet that Jesus born of the Virgin.

Where do I come down? The king's words do not settle it, because the king did not know. I think Poole is right that it may well have been the Angel of the Lord, who so often in the Old Testament speaks as God himself. But I hold that loosely, because the text's own emphasis is plainer. Isaiah forty-three had promised, when you walk through fire you shall not be burned. In the furnace that promise became a sight. The three were not spared the fire. They had company in it. The letter to the Hebrews remembers these men among those who by faith quenched the power of fire. That is my one New Testament move in the chapter.

Chapter four is a different kind of text. It is a royal letter, written in the king's own voice. I, Nebuchadnezzar, was at ease in my house. He dreams of a great tree at the center of the earth, feeding every creature, the same picture Ezekiel used to warn Pharaoh. Then a watcher, a holy one, comes down and cries, chop down the tree, but leave the stump.

The purpose of the dream is stated three times in the chapter, in verses seventeen, twenty-five, and thirty-two. That the living may know that the Most High rules the kingdom of men and gives it to whom he will. That is the thesis of the whole book.

Daniel is dismayed, and then gives counsel in verse twenty-seven. Break off your sins by practicing righteousness, and your iniquities by showing mercy to the oppressed. The Latin Bible rendered that redeem your sins with alms, and for centuries it was used to teach that giving can pay for sin. Calvin, Poole, and Ellicott all answer that the word means to break off, like an ox throwing off a yoke. Daniel is calling for repentance, proved by justice to the poor captives Babylon had crushed.

Twelve months pass. Nothing happens. Then the king walks on his palace roof and says, Is not this great Babylon, which I have built by my mighty power as a royal residence and for the glory of my majesty?

We have his own building records. A stone foundation tablet, now in the British Museum, lists his temples and walls, and ends with a prayer to his god Marduk that the temple he has built would endure for all time, and that his descendants would live there forever. That is the roof speech in stone.

While the words were still in his mouth, a voice fell from heaven. He was driven out, ate grass like an ox, and his hair grew like eagles' feathers. Then verse thirty-four. At the end of the days I, Nebuchadnezzar, lifted my eyes to heaven, and my reason returned to me. Notice the order. Jerome says that if he had not raised his eyes toward heaven, he would not have regained his mind.

The second hard question. Was Nebuchadnezzar truly converted? Matthew Henry has no doubt at all. He is sure the king truly repented and was received by God. Calvin disagrees flatly. He speaks of King Nebuchadnezzar who was never truly converted, a man who confessed the Most High but never abolished the other gods of Babylon. John Gill stands between them and calls it a question.

I lean toward Calvin's caution, but I keep Henry's hope. Daniel records a confession that is true, all his works are right and his ways are just, and he does not tell us what was in the king's heart. We do not need to know his final state to learn his lesson.

And here is the lesson. Pride is not mainly loud. It is a man alone on a roof, looking at what he has built, saying my power and my glory. Calvin remarks that the seven long periods show how slowly pride is cured once it sinks into the marrow. So the question is not whether you have a roof. You do. A career, a family, a reputation. The question is what you say when you stand on it. Look up before God has to bring you down.

Job nineteen.

Job answers Bildad, and the chapter moves in four steps. First a siege. God has walled up my way, his troops encamp around my tent. Then the desertion of everyone, down to his wife and his servants. I have escaped by the skin of my teeth. Then the plea in verse twenty-one, have mercy on me, O you my friends.

Then a wish. Oh that my words were written, engraved in the rock forever. Bildad had said that the wicked man's memory perishes from the earth. Job wants his words to outlast him. Matthew Henry points out the irony. God granted it. You read those words this morning.

And then verse twenty-five. For I know that my Redeemer lives. The word is go-el, the kinsman redeemer. He bought back family land. Boaz did it when he married Ruth to raise up a dead man's name. He was the avenger of blood. Job has no kinsman left. Everyone has walked away. So he reaches past all of them to God, the very God who seems to be besieging him, and calls him family. At the last he will stand upon the dust. That is the grave word in Job, the dust where Job will lie.

Then verse twenty-six. After my skin has been thus destroyed, yet in my flesh I shall see God. The footnote offers without my flesh, and good scholars defend it. But verse twenty-seven presses the point. My eyes shall behold, and not another. Jerome read it as resurrection and marveled. The Lord had not yet died, and the athlete of the Church saw his Redeemer rising from the grave.

Here is the wisdom. When your friends fail you and God seems to be your enemy, faith does not deny the pain. It appeals from God's hidden hand to God's revealed heart.

John twelve, verses thirty-seven through fifty.

Verse thirty-six ended with Jesus hiding himself. Now John steps back and gives his verdict on the whole public ministry. Though he had done so many signs before them, they still did not believe. John quotes Isaiah fifty-three. Lord, who has believed what he heard from us? Then Isaiah six. He has blinded their eyes. And verse thirty-nine says, therefore they could not believe.

Could not, or would not? Chrysostom softens it. They could not because they would not. Augustine says the root is their will, but that God also justly withdrew his help. Calvin sees a real judicial blindness, sent on people already set against the light. All three agree the guilt is theirs.

Then verse forty-one. Isaiah said these things because he saw his glory and spoke of him. The Lord high and lifted up in Isaiah six was the Son. Sproul says it bluntly. Isaiah saw Christ on the throne in His glory before Christ was even born.

Now follow the word glory. Many of the authorities believed, but would not confess, for they loved the glory that comes from man more than the glory that comes from God. Isaiah saw the glory of Christ. These men saw it and preferred their reputation. J. C. Ryle describes their kind as knowing too much of religion to be happy in the world, and clinging too much to the world to enjoy any religion.

Then Jesus cries out his last public words. I have come into the world as light. I did not come to judge the world but to save the world. And yet the word I have spoken will judge on the last day. Augustine draws the line plainly. Now is the time for mercy, and the judgment comes after.

Second John.

This little letter is from the elder, which is how the aged apostle John refers to himself, writing from Ephesus late in the first century to the elect lady and her children. Who is she? Clement of Alexandria said it was written to a Babylonian lady, by name Electa. But verse thirteen sends greetings from the children of your elect sister, and two sisters both named Electa seems unlikely. Most likely she was a real woman whose house hosted the church. Matthew Poole says the question is not worth a fight, and he is right.

The word truth appears five times in the first four verses. Then love, and John defines it. This is love, that we walk according to his commandments.

Then the danger. Many deceivers have gone out into the world, those who do not confess the coming of Jesus Christ in the flesh. Cerinthus taught that a heavenly Christ came upon the man Jesus at his baptism and left him before the cross. In verse nine John calls them the ones who go on ahead. They thought they were advanced. The old Cambridge commentary says that kind of advance is really desertion of the faith.

So, verse ten. Do not receive him into your house or give him any greeting. John means traveling teachers who lived on the hospitality of believers and taught in the homes where the church met. To house them and send them off with a Christian blessing was to share in their work. It is not a command to be cold to your unbelieving neighbor. Matthew Henry says such men may still be helped in real need.

The early church remembered John living this way. Irenaeus heard from Polycarp's hearers that John went to a bath house in Ephesus, saw Cerinthus inside, and rushed out of the bath-house without bathing, exclaiming, Let us fly, lest even the bath-house fall down, because Cerinthus, the enemy of the truth, is within.

And the letter ends with a pastor's ache. I would rather not use paper and ink. I hope to come and talk face to face.

Here is the thread that runs through today. Glory. Nebuchadnezzar stood on his roof and spoke of the glory of my majesty. The rulers in John loved the glory that comes from man. Isaiah saw the true glory, and it was Christ's. And Second John tells us that glory came in the flesh. Three young men in Babylon gave up the king's favor for God's glory and found the fourth man in the fire.

So carry this question through the day. Whose glory are you protecting when you stay quiet?
