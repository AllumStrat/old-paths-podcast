## Daniel 3-4 (Old Testament)

### Setting and background
- End of ch. 2 (2:46-49): Nebuchadnezzar falls on his face, confesses "your God is God of gods", promotes Daniel; Shadrach, Meshach, Abednego appointed over the affairs of the province of Babylon. Daniel "remained at the king's court" (explains his absence in ch. 3; Keil rejects guesses).
- Date of ch. 3 undated in the Aramaic. LXX and Theodotion prefix "In the eighteenth year" (Cambridge, Barnes, Gill, Pulpit) = about 587/586 B.C., the year before / year of Jerusalem's fall (2 Kings 25:8; Jeremiah 52:29 speaks of 832 captives in the 18th year). Prideaux puts the image then, ~19 years after the dream (JFB). Uncertain; at least a long interval after 2:47.
- Image (tselem): 60 by 6 cubits, about 90 by 9 feet; likely wood overlaid with gold (Ellicott, Keil, Henry, Isaiah 40:19, 44). Proportion explained by pedestal (Henry, Keil). Who it represents: Bel/Marduk (Barnes, Pulpit), Nebuchadnezzar himself (Cambridge: Assyrian kings set up "a great image of my royalty", salam sharruti), or the world-power suggested by the ch. 2 statue, "head of gold" now all gold (Keil, Gill, JFB, Spurgeon 2217: "made the image of his dream the pattern of the idol").
- Plain of Dura: Oppert found mounds called Tolul Dura about 6-12 miles SSE of Babylon/Hillah; one mound, el-Mokhattat, a brick square about 45 ft square and 20 ft high, he thought a colossal statue's pedestal (Cambridge; Keil quotes Oppert). Pulpit: on flat plains a 100-ft gilded obelisk visible ~13 miles.
- Gill: Jewish tradition makes Dura the plain of Shinar of Genesis 11 (cf. ESV cross-ref 3:1 to Gen 11:2) - Babel again.
- Music list: horn, pipe, lyre, trigon, harp, bagpipe (several Greek loanwords: kitharos, psanterin, sumphonia) - repeated four times; the official list repeated; refrain style mocks the machinery of empire. Spurgeon 2217: the three names occur thirteen times in the chapter.
- Ch. 4 is a royal proclamation (4:1-3 = Aramaic 3:31-33) in the first person, switching to third person in the madness (4:28-33) and back (4:34). Jerome: "The epistle of Nebuchadnezzar was inserted in the volume of the prophet..." Calvin: Daniel gives substance, speaks in third person per Hebrew/Chaldee custom.
- Archaeology for 4:30: East India House Inscription (British Museum, found before 1803 in Babylon ruins), a stone foundation tablet: Nebuchadnezzar lists his building works; prays to Marduk "may the temple I have built endure for all time and may I be satisfied with its splendour... may my descendants live therein forever" (Wikipedia translation, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_India_House_Inscription). Ellicott: Nebuchadnezzar's records mention eight temples he completed plus Marduk's temple. Ligonier: Hanging Gardens, empire from Egypt to Iran. Babylon's walls (Poole, Gill).
- Chronology of the madness: Gill (via Ussher/Prideaux): restored ~563 B.C., 42nd year, lived one more year (died 562). Henry: "It is thought that he did not live more than a year after his restoration."
- Aramaic words: tselem image; 'ir "watcher" (Jerome: the angels "because they ever keep watch"); 'iddanin "times/periods" (seven); peraq 4:27 "break off" (not "redeem", Vulgate/papal "redeem thy sins with alms"; Ellicott: metaphor of a beast casting off the yoke, Genesis 27:40; Calvin; Poole; Benson). bar-'elahin "a son of the gods" (Cambridge: elahin always true plural in Aramaic; LXX "angel of God").
- Structure: JFB: chs. 3 and 6 pair (deliverance of faithful), chs. 4 and 5 pair (God humbles proud kings).

### Interpretive questions
1. "But if not" (3:17-18): doubt of God's power? Text: "If this be so" (Aramaic hen 'ithai). ESV footnote: "If our God whom we serve is able to deliver us, he will deliver us". Keil: yekhil = "ethical ability", not omnipotence; "neither audacity, nor a superstitious expectation of some miracle..., nor fanaticism... but only the confidence of faith and a humble submission to the will of God". Ellicott: "They did not question His power; they did not know whether He would will to exercise the use of it." Pulpit: suppressed "if it be his good pleasure". Poole: "they did not presume to tie God to this deliverance absolutely". Calvin: double reason, God can, and life not worth denying God. Ligonier: did not serve God for what He could do but because He alone is God. Spurgeon: "not cupboard love". Answer: certainty about God's power and their duty, humility about God's plan.
2. Who is the fourth man (3:25, 28)? Christ: Hippolytus (Scholia), many fathers per Barnes (Tertullian, Augustine, Hilary), Gill ("highly probable"), Poole ("or rather of Jesus Christ, the Angel of the covenant"), Henry concise ("supported and encouraged by the presence of the Son of God"), Spurgeon 2217 (Son of God left heaven to stand with them), JFB ("Really it was the 'messenger of the covenant'"). Angel: Calvin, Jerome (literal; typologically Christ), Geneva, Cambridge, Barnes, Keil (Nebuchadnezzar's Babylonian theology), Ellicott ("the same 'Angel of God's presence'"). The king himself says "sent his angel" (3:28). Host view: the king spoke as a pagan ("son of the gods"); Daniel records what he said; whether this was the Angel of the Lord, as Poole thinks, we may hold with humility; the comfort of Isaiah 43:2 is the same: God's servant is not alone in the fire.
3. Was Nebuchadnezzar converted (4:34-37)? Henry: "There can be no reasonable doubt that Nebuchadnezzar was a true penitent, and an accepted believer." Calvin: "King Nebuchadnezzar who was never truly converted" (3:29 lecture); he mixed the Most High with lesser gods; on 4:37, a real but "outward" humbling, not "what is required of a pious man". Gill: "whether truly converted, is a question." Ellicott: progress but unclear. Spurgeon No. 949: "No one has ever numbered Nebuchadnezzar with the prophets..." yet his statement is "a most correct and vigorous statement of sublime doctrines". Jerome: had he not raised his eyes to heaven, he would not have regained his intelligence.
4. 4:27 "break off your sins by righteousness... mercy to the oppressed" - Vulgate "redeem thy sins with alms" used for satisfaction; Calvin, Poole, Ellicott, Benson: break off, repent, show it by justice. Jerome: delay of twelve months because of mercy to the poor. Calvin: no sign of repentance in the twelve months.

## 1. Setting and structure

**Context.** Chapter 19 is Job's reply to **Bildad's second speech (ch. 18)**. Bildad had painted the fate of "the wicked": a trapped man, terrors on every side, and his memory perished from the earth with "no name in the street" (18:17). Ellicott (bh_job19-25.txt) notes that Job 19:23–25 answers that threat head-on: "Bildad (Job 18:17-20) had threatened Job with the extinction of his name and memory, so he now appeals to the verdict of futurity, and with what success we ourselves who read and repeat and discuss his words are witnesses." Cambridge on 19:6 adds that Job's "net" picks up Bildad's net imagery in 18:8: "it was not his own feet that led him into the net, God had thrown it about him."

**Structure (ESV):**
- **vv. 2–6: Complaint against the friends.** "How long will you torment me and break me in pieces with words? These ten times you have cast reproach upon me." Verse 6 is the hinge: "know then that God has put me in the wrong and closed his net about me." Cambridge suggests reading "perverted my right," i.e. the cause is God's dealing with him, not Job's guilt.
- **vv. 7–12: God's siege.** "Violence!" with no answer (v. 7). God has walled up his way (v. 8), stripped his glory and crown (v. 9), uprooted his hope "like a tree" (v. 10), and counts him an adversary (v. 11). "His troops come on together; they have cast up their siege ramp against me and encamp around my tent" (v. 12; ESV footnote: "Hebrew *their way*").
- **vv. 13–22: Abandonment by all.** Brothers, acquaintances, relatives, close friends, house guests, maidservants, his servant (who won't answer even when Job pleads), his wife ("my breath is strange to my wife"), his own brothers, young children, intimate friends (vv. 13–19). Then his body: "My bones stick to my skin… I have escaped by the skin of my teeth" (v. 20). Cambridge: "with nothing else… The phrase is probably proverbial". Benson/Barnes list the competing explanations (gums, etc.). Then the plea: "Have mercy on me, have mercy on me, O you my friends, for the hand of God has touched me!" (v. 21), and "Why do you, like God, pursue me? Why are you not satisfied with my flesh?" (v. 22). Keil & Delitzsch say "eat someone's flesh" is a Semitic idiom for slander, like English "backbiting".
- **vv. 23–24: The inscription wish.** "Oh that my words were written! Oh that they were inscribed in a book! Oh that with an iron pen and lead they were engraved in the rock forever!" Barnes and JFB: letters were chiselled into rock and the grooves filled with lead. Commentators split on whether "my words" means his general protest of innocence (Cambridge, Poole's 2nd option) or the confession of vv. 25–27 that follows (Gill, Henry, Ellicott's "probably"). See Q(c) below.
- **vv. 25–27: The Redeemer.** "For I know that my Redeemer lives, and at the last he will stand upon the earth [fn: Hebrew *dust*]. And after my skin has been thus destroyed, yet in [fn: *Or without*] my flesh I shall see God, whom I shall see for myself, and my eyes shall behold, and not another. My heart faints within me!"
- **vv. 28–29: Warning to the friends.** "If you say, 'How we will pursue him!' and, 'The root of the matter is found in him' [fn: many Hebrew MSS *in me*], be afraid of the sword… that you may know there is a judgment." The Redeemer who vindicates Job is also the Judge the friends must face.

**Movement of the chapter:** besieged by God, then deserted by everyone, then a wish to be remembered in stone, then a leap of faith past all of it to a living Go'el, then the tables turned on his accusers. Barnes: "after he uttered these expressions we meet not with any such impatient or despairing passages as we had before". Poole says the same. Ligonier (2015) is more measured: "From chapters 3 through 19, Job vacillates from despair to the hope of vindication."

### Key Hebrew (Bible Hub interlinear and Strong's saved in raw/job/bh_interlinear_*, bh_hebrew_*)

**19:25** reads *wa'ani yada'ti go'ali chai, we'acharon 'al-'afar yaqum*: "But/And I, I know my Redeemer lives, and [as the] last, upon dust he will stand/arise."

- **go'el** (גֹּאֵל, participle of *ga'al*, Strong's H1350; KJV glosses include "avenger… kinsfolk(-man), purchase, ransom, redeem(-er), revenger"). The nearest male kinsman, whose duties were:
  - **redeem property** a poor relative had sold (Lev 25:25–26) and **redeem the relative himself** from slavery (Lev 25:48–49);
  - **raise up a name/seed** for the dead (Deut 25:5; Ruth 3–4, Boaz);
  - act as **"avenger of blood"** (*go'el haddam*, Num 35:12, 19–27; Deut 19:6–12; Josh 20:3).
  - Barnes: "His essential office was that of a vindicator - one who took up the cause of a friend, whether that friend was murdered, or was oppressed, or was wronged in any way."
  - Elsewhere God himself is Israel's Go'el (Isa 41:14; 43:14; 44:6, 24; 47:4; 49:7, 26; 54:5, 8; 59:20; 63:16; Ps 19:14; 103:4; Gen 48:16).
  - This builds on Job's earlier longings: a "daysman/arbiter" (9:33), "my witness is in heaven" (16:19), "my surety" (17:3).
- **'acharon** (אַחֲרוֹן, H314: "after(-ward), to come, following… last, latter… ut(ter)most"). It can be read three ways:
  - adverbially, "at the last" (ESV);
  - as a title, "the Last" (Isa 44:6; 48:12, "I am the first and I am the last"; JFB/Poole connect it to Christ, Rev 1:17);
  - as "one who comes after (me)," an after-man or vindicator (Cambridge; Ewald).
  Pulpit: "if a noun, 'the last one' as רִאשׁון is 'the first one'… if intended adverbially, 'at the last' - i.e, at the end of all things."
- **'afar** (עָפָר, H6083: "dust… earth"). ESV footnote at 19:25: "Hebrew *dust*." In Job, dust is the grave-word (7:21; 17:16; 20:11; 21:26). Cambridge: "The word 'dust' carries rather an allusion to the earth as that wherein Job shall have been laid before God shall appear for him." Ellicott argues against "over my dust" because 41:33 uses the same phrase to mean simply "upon earth." Pulpit prefers "he shall stand up over my dust."
- **yaqum** ("he will stand/arise"). This is the legal sense of rising to testify or to judge (Deut 19:15; Ps 12:5; Cambridge). The Vulgate turned it into Job's own rising: "in novissimo die de terra surrecturus sum" ("in the last day I shall rise from the earth"). Barnes: "Jerome has rendered this as though it referred to Job… But this is not in accordance with the Hebrew." That matters because **Gregory and Jerome both quote the Vulgate**, and their "I shall rise again from the earth… again be clothed with my skin" comes from that rendering.

**19:26** reads *we'achar 'ori niqqefu-zot, umibbesari 'echezeh 'eloah*: "and after my skin, [which] they have struck off thus, and from my flesh I will see Eloah."
- The words "worms" and "body" (KJV) are not in the Hebrew (Barnes, Cambridge, Pulpit, and Calvin himself).
- **mibbesari**, "from my flesh," is the crux. The preposition *min* can mean "from [the vantage point of]," i.e. *in/with* my flesh (ESV text, Pulpit, Poole, Gill, Henry, JFB). It can also be privative, "away from / without" my flesh (ESV footnote "Or *without*"; Cambridge; Keil & Delitzsch: "And without my flesh shall I behold Eloah"). Cambridge explains it with Regan's "answer from our home" in *King Lear*.

**19:27** reads *kalu kilyotay becheqi*. ESV: "My heart faints within me!"
- Literally "my kidneys [*kilyah*, H3629: "kidneys, reins… (figuratively) the mind (as the interior self)"] are finished/spent [*kalah*, H3615; BH interlinear glosses it "Yearn"] in my bosom."
- KJV: "though my reins be consumed within me."
- Most take it as longing, not decay. Poole: "the reins are oft put for earnest desires or affections." Barnes cites the margin "my reins within me are consumed with earnest desire for that day." Henry: "all my desires are summed up and concluded in this". JFB: "pine with longing desire for that day (Ps 84:2; 119:81)."
- Calvin (Golding) reads it as exhaustion: "my kidneyes are vvaſted in my boſome… that is too ſay, although there be no more ſtrength nor courage in me."
- "And not another" is literally **zar**, "a stranger." It means either "I and not a stranger shall see" (Cambridge, Gregory, Henry) or "I shall see Him, no longer estranged" (Bengel via JFB).

## 2. Interpretive questions

### (a) Who is the Redeemer in 19:25?

**Christ (the classic Christian and Reformed reading).** Gregory, Jerome, Poole, Gill, Matthew Henry, Benson, JFB, the Geneva Bible note, and Spurgeon all take this view.
- Gill argues by elimination: "he means not any mere man that should rise up and vindicate him… nor God the Father, to whom the character of a Redeemer is seldom or ever given… but the Son of God, the Lord Jesus Christ, who is our 'Goel'".
- Poole: the go'el's three duties (redeem the inheritance, avenge blood, raise up a name) "all which most fitly agrees to Christ, who is our nearest Kinsman and Brother, Hebrews 2:11".
- JFB keeps a guardrail: "The Spirit intended in Job's words more than Job fully understood (1Pe 1:12)."

**God himself as Vindicator.** Cambridge ("Job here names God his Goel"), Ellicott, Pulpit, Keil & Delitzsch, and Barnes's cautious summary take this view. They read it from inside Job's argument: the God who now seems to be his enemy (19:6–12) will at last stand up as his kinsman-champion. That is Job "appealing from God to God."
- Ellicott: "he has already recognised God as his judge, his umpire, his advocate, his witness, and surety".
- Calvin's sermon stays here too: "I know that my God is aliue". The point is that Job's defense is made before God, not before men.

**A human vindicator (an after-man who avenges him).** Pulpit mentions this view and rejects it: "unless in the case of a violent death at the hands of a man… there could be no avenger of blood." Gill rejects it as well.

**How to hold these together (for the show).** Within the book, Job's Go'el is God, who is both Job's opponent and his only hope. Cambridge says it best: "the ideas of Goel and redeemer virtually coincide." The canon then names him. Sean Michael Lucas (Ligonier) puts it this way: "Though he didn't know the Redeemer by name, we do. His name is Jesus." Spurgeon (No. 2909) admits Job "may not have known the full meaning of all that he said" and preaches it "in the full Evangelical sense."

### (b) Does 19:26 teach bodily resurrection, or vindication before death?

**Bodily resurrection.** Clement of Rome, Jerome, Gregory, the Geneva note, Poole, Gill, Henry, Benson, JFB, Spurgeon, and Ligonier 2015 hold this view.
- JFB argues that Job "uniformly despairs of restoration… in this life (Job 17:15, 16)". He adds that "it would be no full vindication if his soul alone were to be happy without the body… It was his body that had chiefly suffered".
- Poole notes that "with my flesh" means "with eyes of flesh… to show that he did not speak of a mental or spiritual, but of a corporeal vision, and that after his death."
- Verse 27 ("my eyes… not another") presses toward numerical identity of the body. Gregory and Jerome use it against Origenist "ethereal body" ideas, and Gregory against Eutychius.

**Seeing God after death, "without my flesh," i.e. as a disembodied spirit.** Cambridge (and K&D) argue that the context requires it, and that it "do[es] not express in what condition precisely, but after what events Job shall see God". Still, both insist the core is post-mortem vindication and seeing God in peace.

**Vindication before death / a prophecy of the theophany in ch. 38–42** ("Some late interpreters," per Poole and Benson). Barnes and Pulpit note that Job does see God in 42:5 ("now my eye sees you").
- **Calvin** combines both readings: "to ſee him in his fleſh, that is to ſay, by beeing reſtored againe". He admits that "hee ſpeaketh not here expreſſely of the Reſurrection." Yet he insists the words attribute to God a power "not to be ſeene as now in the common order of nature". He then applies it to the resurrection at "the laſt day."
- Pulpit's balance is useful: the verse "may be taken merely as a prophecy of the theophany… But the nexus with ver. 25… fully justify the traditional exegesis".
- Ellicott: "Do Job's words then teach the doctrine of the resurrection? Possibly not directly, but they express the firm conviction of that faith of which the resurrection is the only natural justification".

**Where the commentators disagree (to flag on air):**
1. **in vs. without my flesh.** ESV text vs. footnote. Pulpit and Gill say *in*; Cambridge and K&D say *without*.
2. **'acharon:** "at the last" (time), "the Last" (title), or "the one who comes after."
3. **"upon the earth/dust":** simply "on the earth" (Ellicott) or "over my dust/grave" (Pulpit, Cambridge, Henry, JFB).
4. **Which "words" Job wanted inscribed** (see Q(c)).
5. **The reins:** longing (most) vs. exhaustion (Calvin).
6. **The Vulgate factor:** Gregory and Jerome's strong resurrection reading rests partly on Jerome's rendering "I shall rise… be clothed again with my skin," which Barnes says the Hebrew does not support. Clement's citation ("You shall raise up this flesh of mine") follows the Septuagint ("to raise again upon earth this skin of mine", Barnes).
7. **Calvin vs. most of the Puritans** on how much Job himself understood. Calvin: "a little ſparke of light." Gill: "what a glorious and enlarged view had Job of the blessed Redeemer!"

### (c) The irony of the inscription wish

Job asked for his words to be written in a book and cut into rock. God gave him something better: they were written into Scripture.
- Matthew Henry: "That which Job here somewhat passionately wished for God graciously granted him."
- Gill: "his words are written and printed in a better book than he had in his view".
- Spurgeon (No. 504): "the Book of books embalms the words of Job". He notes the words are also cut into stone after all, "upon many and many a sepulchre."
- Lucas (Ligonier): "a book that has proved more permanent than iron, lead, or rock."
- Ellicott: "with what success we ourselves who read and repeat and discuss his words are witnesses." He also notes the confession "rightly stands at the head of the Christian Office for the Burial of the Dead". That is the funeral liturgy, and Handel's *Messiah* is a natural mention.
- **Contrary voice:** Calvin (Sermon 71) treats the wish as one of Job's excesses, an example of being "ouerhardie in our talke". God granted it anyway.
- **Second irony:** Bildad said the wicked man's "memory perishes from the earth" (18:17). Job's memory is now read in every language.

## 1. Setting and structure

**Place in John.** 12:37-50 closes the "Book of Signs" (chs. 1-12). At 12:36 Jesus "departed and hid himself from them" (ἐκρύβη). That is his last public act before the Upper Room (chs. 13-17), and John steps back to give his verdict on the public ministry. The section mirrors the Prologue: "He came to his own, and his own people did not receive him" (1:11) is now shown in narrative; the light/darkness theme of 1:4-9 comes back in 12:46; "we have seen his glory" (1:14) answers to Isaiah who "saw his glory" (12:41).

**Two movements.**
1. **12:37-43, the evangelist's verdict.** There were "so many signs," seven of them narrated, the last being the raising of Lazarus, yet "they still did not believe" (37). Unbelief fulfils Scripture (38, Isa 53:1). It is also a judicial inability (39-40, Isa 6:10). Isaiah's vision was of Christ's glory (41). There is an exception that does not really help: many authorities "believed" but would not confess (42-43).
2. **12:44-50, Jesus' last public proclamation.** "Jesus cried out." This is a compressed summary of the Gospel's main themes: believing in or seeing Jesus is believing in or seeing the Father (44-45; cf. 5:24, 14:9); he is light (46; cf. 8:12, 9:5); he came to save, not to judge (47; cf. 3:17); the rejected word will judge at the last day (48; cf. 5:45); his words are the Father's commandment, and that commandment is eternal life (49-50; cf. 7:16-17, 8:28).

**The Isaiah quotations.**
- **Isaiah 53:1** (John 12:38), "Lord, who has believed what he heard from us, and to whom has the arm of the Lord been revealed?" This opens the fourth Servant Song (52:13-53:12), whose servant "shall be high and lifted up" (52:13). That echoes John's "lifted up" sayings just before (12:32-34). The "arm of the Lord" is God's saving power. Augustine reads it as the Son himself, the Word by whom all things were made. Paul uses the same verse for Israel's unbelief (Rom 10:16).
- **Isaiah 6:10** (John 12:40), "He has blinded their eyes and hardened their heart…" This comes from Isaiah's temple call (6:1-13). The prophet who sees "the Lord sitting upon a throne, high and lifted up" (6:1, the same "high and lifted up" as 52:13) is sent to a people whose hearing will only harden them. The text is also used in Matt 13:14-15, Mark 4:12, Acts 28:26-27 and Rom 11:8. John's form is shortened and makes God the subject of the blinding.
- **12:41** links the two. Isaiah said these things "because he saw his glory and spoke of him." The "his" and "him" are Christ: the vision of Isa 6 was a vision of the pre-incarnate Son. (Ellicott, bh_john12-41: "The better text is, . . . because he saw His glory," i.e. ὅτι rather than KJV's ὅτε "when.")

**Greek notes.**
- **οὐκ ἠδύναντο πιστεύειν** (12:39), "they could not believe," imperfect of δύναμαι. Διὰ τοῦτο … ὅτι points forward: "for this reason they could not believe, namely, because Isaiah said…" (Pulpit Commentary, bh_john12-39). Meyer says the phrase is "not: nolebant (Chrysostom, Theophylact, Euth. Zigabenus, Wolf)" but a real inability grounded "in the divine judgment of obduracy." He also quotes Bengel: "Hic subsistit evangelista, quis ultra nitatur?" Compare 5:44, "How can you believe, when you receive glory from one another?"
- **δόξα** wordplay, 12:41 and 12:43. Isaiah "saw his glory" (τὴν δόξαν αὐτοῦ). The rulers "loved the glory of men (τὴν δόξαν τῶν ἀνθρώπων) more than the glory of God (τὴν δόξαν τοῦ θεοῦ)." KJV "praise" hides this. Vincent (bh_john12-43): "Much better, Rev., glory, because suggesting a contrast with the vision of divine glory referred to in John 12:41." ἤπερ ("more than / rather than") occurs only here in the NT (Vincent; Pulpit). The Cambridge Bible says "'more than' is not strong enough; it should be rather than."
- **ἐπίστευσαν εἰς αὐτόν** (12:42), "believed in him." This is John's usual formula for full faith, here used of faith that would not confess (Vincent, bh_john12-42). **οὐχ ὡμολόγουν** is imperfect, a continuing failure to confess (Expositor's Greek Testament).
- **ἀποσυνάγωγος** ("put out of the synagogue"). The word appears only in John: 9:22 (the parents of the man born blind), 12:42, and 16:2 ("they will put you out of the synagogues"). It meant social and religious excommunication. Calvin calls it a perversion of church discipline: "Excommunication ought to have been the sinew of holy discipline."
- **ἔκραξεν καὶ εἶπεν** (12:44), "cried out and said." κράζω is John's word for loud, public, solemn proclamation: 1:15 (the Baptist), 7:28 and 7:37 (at the Feast), here. Calvin: "The cry is expressive of vehemence." The Pulpit Commentary uses the word against the theory that 44-50 were spoken privately to the disciples, since "the word (ἔκραξε) 'cried aloud,' would not then have been used."
- Aorists **ἐλάλησα** (12:48-49, "the word that I have spoken") look back on a completed ministry. Meyer uses them as evidence that 44-50 is a retrospective summary.

## 2. Interpretive questions and how the voices answer

### (a) "Therefore they could not believe" (12:39): divine hardening or human responsibility?

- **Augustine (Tract. 53.4-11)** holds both. First, foreknowledge does not cause sin: "He foretold it, but did not cause it." Then he goes further: "if I am asked why they could not, I reply at once, because they would not." Isaiah's statement that God "blinded" them is also true, but God hardens "simply by letting alone and withdrawing His aid," in a judgment "hidden, although not … unrighteous." The root is pride: they sought "to establish their own" righteousness (Rom 10:3), and "denying the need of divine assistance, they are not assisted." He then refuses to probe further, since this is "too high for my stature." In 53.11 he adds a pastoral note: the blinding humbled some of them so that they later repented (Acts 2; Luke 23:34).
- **Chrysostom (Hom. 68.2)** softens the text the most. "Because" refers to the event, not the cause: "it was not because Isaiah spoke, that they believed not; but because they were not about to believe, that he spoke." "Could not" means "would not," because Scripture "is wont to term choice, power" (cf. Jer 13:23). "He blinded" is a "peculiar mode of speech" for God permitting and abandoning: "we begin the desertion, and become the causes of our perdition." Meyer (on Bible Hub) names Chrysostom as the source of the *nolebant* ("they would not") reading and rejects it.
- **Calvin (on 12:38-40)** gives the most explicitly judicial reading. The prophecy laid no "necessity" on the Jews, and foreknowledge alone causes nothing. But in this text "we ought to consider not so much the foreknowledge of God as his justice and vengeance." God declares "what He himself will do," which is to punish obstinate wickedness with blindness. He separates two steps: Isa 53:1 is free election (none believe except those God enlightens, "the reason of which does not appear"), while Isa 6:10 is penal hardening of the already rebellious. "They who do not attend to these steps mistake and confound passages of Scripture." The blinding is "accidental to the word of God": the word is life-giving by nature, and man's wickedness "turns life into death." In his Isaiah 6 commentary Calvin says that if one inquires into the first cause, "we must come to the predestination of God," but we should look at the evident cause, their rebellion.
- **Sproul** follows Calvin. He calls it "the relentless Calvinism of the Apostle John." It is a "judicial judgment": God "turns the sinner over to his sin." He also gives it a missions application: sovereignty does not cancel the duty to proclaim, because God ordains the means as well as the ends.
- **Ryle and Spurgeon** put the weight on human hardness. Ryle: "the desperate hardness of the human heart"; miracles do not convert, only "a new heart." Spurgeon: "They were willfully blinded… they hardened their hearts against it."
- **Bible Hub exegetes.** Cambridge: "'I will not' leads to 'I cannot.' Pharaoh first hardened his heart and then God hardened it." Bengel: "First, they do not believe, as being refractory; then, they cannot believe."
- **Where they disagree.** Chrysostom effectively turns "could not" into "would not." Augustine says it is "would not" in its root, but also a real, hidden divine withholding. Calvin and Sproul say it is a real inability imposed as judicial punishment, with election behind Isa 53:1. All of them agree that God does not cause sin and that the guilt belongs to the unbelievers.

### (b) 12:41: whose glory did Isaiah see?

- **John's own point:** the Lord on the throne in Isa 6 was the Son. This is one of the strongest NT texts for Christ's deity. YHWH's glory, which he "will not give to another" (Isa 42:8), is Christ's glory.
- **Augustine (53.12)** uses it against those who say "the Father is invisible, and the Son visible." Such people "think that the latter is a creature" (Arian-type subordinationists). Isaiah saw the Son "not as He is, but in some symbolic way," and in the form of God the Son is as invisible as the Father. (Augustine, not Calvin, makes the explicitly anti-Arian use of this verse in the saved texts.)
- **Chrysostom (Hom. 68)** takes the opposite reading: "Whose? The Father's." John (12:41) refers the vision to the Son and Paul (Acts 28:25) to the Spirit "as showing that the Dignity is one." So he still argues for Trinitarian unity, but he makes the primary referent the Father. This is a clear disagreement with Calvin, Sproul and Spurgeon.
- **Calvin.** On John 12:41 he says only that "the Evangelist takes for granted, that Isaiah saw the glory of Christ," with the point that Isaiah was a witness to Christ's coming kingdom. His Trinitarian argument is in the Isaiah commentary. On Isa 6:1, the Lord was Christ, "for God never revealed himself to the Fathers but in his eternal Word and only begotten Son," though it is "wrong… to limit this… to the person of Christ," because Christ was "the image of the invisible God." On Isa 6:9-10 (citing John 12:41): "it is evident… that Christ was that God who filled the whole earth with his majesty." He also uses Acts 28:25 to include the Spirit. On Isa 6:3 he grants that the angels "describe One God in Three Persons" but warns against weak proof-texts that hand heretics a triumph. In the John commentary on ch. 17 (calcom35, ~line 6875) he criticizes the fathers who, in "their dispute with the Arians," tortured texts out of context. **Correction to the brief:** in the saved Calvin texts he does not use 12:41 by name against the Arians. He uses it, through Isaiah 6, for Christ's deity and the Trinity.
- **Sproul:** "Isaiah saw Christ on the throne in His glory before Christ was even born." **Spurgeon (on Isa 6:1):** "Whenever you read in the Old Testament that any man saw the Lord--understand it is the Second Person of the Divine Trinity."

### (c) The secret believers (12:42-43): genuine faith?

- **Severe readings.** Westcott, quoted in Expositor's on Bible Hub: "This complete intellectual faith (so to speak) is really the climax of unbelief. The conviction found no expression in life." Barnes: "speculative acknowledgment… which fears man more than God." Chrysostom: "they were not rulers, but slaves in the utmost slavery." Ellicott notes that in 5:44 seeking human glory "is inconsistent with the existence of any true belief in God." Ryle: they were "evidently convinced," but "like cowards, they held their peace."
- **Mediating readings.** Calvin: they "embraced the doctrine of Christ… but… have not a lively faith"; their faith was "weak," "lukewarm, or rather cold." He does not think they were "altogether silent," but he gives no quarter: cowardly concealment "admits of no excuse," and indirect denial is no "venial sin." Augustine 53.13: John "marked and disapproved of some, who yet, he said, believed on Him." Their hope was to move on through "this gateway of faith" to Paul's "glory… in the cross."
- **Hopeful readings: Nicodemus and Joseph** (3:1-2, 7:50-51, 19:38-39, where Joseph is "a disciple of Jesus, but secretly for fear of the Jews"). Expositor's: Westcott's severe verdict is true of most "but cannot apply to all (see John 7:50, John 19:38-39)." Cambridge: "Joseph and Nicodemus confessed their belief after the crisis of the Crucifixion." JFB, citing Webster and Wilkinson, calls 12:43 "a severe remark, considering that several at least of these persons afterwards boldly confessed Christ." Chrysostom: "this fear was afterwards done away… The grace of the Spirit having come, made them all firmer than adamant" (cf. Acts 6:7, "a great many of the priests").
- **Synthesis for the episode.** As described in 12:42-43, this is defective faith, and John's verdict on it is negative. Its fruit would show whether it was genuine. In at least two cases the cross brought it into the open. The point for the listener is Calvin's "golden fetters" and Ryle's "knowing too much of religion to be happy in the world, and clinging too much to the world to enjoy any religion."

### (d) When and where were 12:44-50 spoken, given 12:36 "he hid himself"?

- **A retrospective summary by John.** Ellicott says the probable explanation is "a summary of words previously uttered by our Lord, but grouped together here." Meyer calls it "an energetic summing up, a condensed summary of that which Jesus has hitherto clearly and openly preached." His evidence is that it echoes earlier sayings, no audience is addressed, and the aorists ἐλάλησα look back on a finished ministry.
- **Spoken as he left.** Bengel: "He spake in the very act of departure… wherefore He is said to have cried." John simply mentions the hiding first.
- **A later public occasion.** Chrysostom (Hom. 68.2): "when He had retired, and given in, and had come to them again, He speaks with them in a lowly manner." This fits his pattern of Jesus withdrawing and then returning with humble words.
- **Not private teaching to the disciples.** The Pulpit Commentary objects that "the word (ἔκραξε) 'cried aloud,' would not then have been used."
- **Calvin** treats it as a real public cry meant to steady believers and rebuke the fearful rulers of 12:42-43: "it contains also an implied reproof, by which he intended to correct that perverse fear."
- **Takeaway.** Most commentators read it as John's closing summary, placed outside chronological order, of what Jesus "cried out" publicly. It is his final public word in this Gospel, and its message is mercy now and judgment by the rejected word later.

## IMPORTANT CORRECTION: Calvin wrote no commentary on 2 John

The CCEL text (calcom45, Calvin's Commentaries on the Catholic Epistles, trans. John Owen) goes straight from "end of the first epistle of John" to the Argument of James. Owen's translator's preface says plainly:

> The Second and the Third Epistle of John were evidently not deemed by Calvin as "catholic;" and for this reason, as it seems, he omitted them.

- John Owen (translator), Translator's Preface, Calvin's Commentaries on the Catholic Epistles. URL: https://www.ccel.org/ccel/c/calvin/calcom45/cache/calcom45.txt. Local file: raw/2john/calvin_calcom45.txt (around line 60).
- So there is **no "Calvin's view" on whether "lady" is a name** to cite from this source. Do not attribute one to him. The closest Calvin material is his comments on 1 John 2:22 and 4:2-3, the parallel "come in the flesh" texts (quotes C2-C4 below). He also comments on 1 Peter 5:13 ("elected together with you"), but there he only argues for a literal Babylon against the Roman reading, which is not useful for 2 John.

## Introduction facts (with source support)

- **Author:** "the elder" (ho presbyteros). Traditional view: John the apostle in old age at Ephesus. Irenaeus (AH 1.16.3) quotes 2 John 10-11 as from "John, the disciple of the Lord". Papias names both "John" among the disciples and "the presbyter John". From that, Eusebius (HE 3.39.6) inferred two Johns and two tombs at Ephesus, and Jerome later gave 2-3 John to "John the presbyter" (as reported by JFB). One-line answer: the Pulpit Commentary calls the second John "a theory of Eusebius, based upon a doubtful interpretation of an awkwardly worded passage in Papias", and notes that Irenaeus, who knew Polycarp, "seems to know nothing of any such person."
- **Date and place:** late first century, at or near Ephesus, probably after 1 John. JFB cites Eusebius HE 3.25 for John's visitation tours after Patmos. Ligonier's devotional notes that some scholars put 2 John *before* 1 John ("Many scholars think this letter may have been actually written before 1 John"), so say "probably after."
- **Recipient views:** (1) an individual Christian woman: Matthew Henry, Poole, Gill, Barnes, MacArthur, Ligonier. (2) "Kyria" as her proper name: Bengel, cited by JFB, Neander and Alford; Ellicott and Benson allow it. (3) "Electa" as her name: Clement's Adumbrations, but Gill, Ellicott, Cambridge and Pulpit reject it because of v13 (two sisters both named Electa). (4) A church personified: JFB/Fausset ("A particular Church, probably that at Babylon"); Cambridge leaves it open; Clement already says the letter "indicates the election of the holy Church."
- **Structure:** greeting (1-3); walk in truth and love (4-6); deceivers and abiding in the teaching (7-11); close (12-13). Poole's argument line divides it 1-4 / 5-9 / 10-11 / 12-13.
- **Relation to 1 John:** the same false teachers. 2 John 7 echoes 1 John 2:18-22 and 4:1-3. Irenaeus (AH 3.16.8) reads 2 John 7-8 together with 1 John 4. Cerinthus taught that "Christ" descended on the man Jesus at his baptism and left him before the cross (AH 1.26.1). Docetists said Christ only "seemed" to have a body (Ligonier).

## Interpretive questions: short answers

**(a) Who is the elect lady?** Most likely a real Christian woman and her household. The household likely hosted a church in its home, so a letter to her house also reached the congregation. "Elect" describes her; it is not her name. That follows from v13: "your elect sister" would otherwise give two sisters the same name. A church personified (with "elect sister" as a sister church) is an old and respectable minority view (JFB, and hinted at already in Clement). Poole: the question "is of no great importance." The application is the same either way.

**(b) "Do not receive him into your house or give him any greeting" (vv10-11).** In context this refers to *itinerant teachers* who arrive on a teaching mission ("bring this teaching") and depend on believers' hospitality and their homes, which were often the places where the church met. The "greeting" is the Christian welcome (chairein) that commends a teacher as a brother and wishes his work success. It does not forbid ordinary courtesy, kindness or evangelism toward unbelievers or neighbors. Matthew Henry: such may be "relieved in their pressing necessities". See also Barnes and the Ligonier devotional. Today it applies to platforming, funding, endorsing, or hosting teachers who deny the apostolic Christ (Ligonier applies it to charitable giving). It does not apply to being rude to the Jehovah's Witness at the door. The Cambridge and Pulpit commentaries both warn against turning it into a blanket rule. MacArthur and Irenaeus/Polycarp read it more sharply.

**(c) "Goes on ahead" (proagon, v9).** The better-attested text reads proagon ("goes on ahead", "runs ahead") rather than parabainon ("transgresses", KJV). It describes "advanced thinkers" who claim to have moved past the apostolic teaching about Christ. John does not condemn growth in understanding. He condemns "progress" that leaves Christ's doctrine behind. Plummer (Cambridge): "such an advance is not progress but apostasy."

### 19th-century evangelical commentators (Bible Hub; useful balance)

**N1. Plummer, Cambridge Bible, 2 John 9 (proagon)**
> These antichristian Gnostics were advanced thinkers: the Gospel was all very well for the unenlightened; but they knew something higher. ## Disagreements worth voicing on air
- **Lady = woman or church?** Individual: Henry, Poole, Gill, Barnes, MacArthur, Pulpit (leans). Church: JFB/Fausset. Open: Cambridge. Electa as a name: Clement, rejected by Ellicott, Gill, Cambridge and Pulpit on the basis of v13. Kyria as a name: Bengel, Alford, Neander. Calvin: no comment (no commentary on 2 John).
- **How strict is v10-11?** Strict and generalized: Irenaeus, the Polycarp tradition, MacArthur. Bounded and situational: Pulpit ("a special case, not laying down a general principle"), Cambridge (a list of questions to ask first), Barnes, Henry ("relieved in their pressing necessities"), Ligonier ("not forbidding contact with non-believers in general").
- **v9 text:** KJV "transgresseth" (parabainon) vs. ESV "goes on ahead" (proagon, the earlier manuscripts). Cambridge, Pulpit and Vincent prefer proagon. Gill and Henry comment on the KJV reading.
- **Date relative to 1 John:** most say after; Ligonier notes some scholars put it before.
