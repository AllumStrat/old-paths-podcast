Here's a question that will tell you a lot about yourself. What do you do with a promise when keeping it gets expensive?

Not when it's easy. Anyone keeps their word when it costs nothing. I mean when the pressure lifts and nobody's watching. Or when the pressure is on, and keeping your word looks foolish.

That's the thread running through today's reading. Jeremiah thirty-three through thirty-five. Ecclesiastes ten. John chapter four. And the opening of First Peter. And there's one word I want you to hold onto the whole way through. Kept. God keeps. People break. A few people keep. And the good news of the gospel is that you are kept.

This is Old Paths. Let's get into it.

Start with Jeremiah thirty-three. Context matters here. Jeremiah is under arrest. Babylon's army is outside the walls. People in Jerusalem are literally tearing down their own houses to shore up the city's defenses. If you wanted a picture of a situation with no upside, this is it.

And into that moment, God says: Call to me, and I will answer you, and show you great and hidden things that you do not know.

Notice the order. God doesn't start with the strategy. He starts with prayer. Most of us flip that. We exhaust every option, every contact, every plan, and then we pray as a last resort. God says, start here.

Then come the promises. Healing. Cleansing from sin. A city full of joy again. The voice of the bride and the bridegroom. People bringing thank offerings to the house of the Lord. And at the center of it all, a righteous Branch from the line of David, and a name. The Lord is our righteousness.

Sit with that for a second. Your problem before God is not that you need a little moral improvement. A better morning routine for your soul. Your problem is that you need a righteousness you do not have. And God says, I will provide it. In Reformed theology we call this justification. The righteousness of Christ counted as yours, received by faith alone. You don't earn the name. You're given it.

Then God ties His promise to the sunrise. If you can break my covenant with the day and my covenant with the night, then you can break my covenant with David. So the next time you see the sun come up, that's not just astronomy. It's a receipt. God keeps.

Chapter thirty-four is the contrast, and it's uncomfortable. Under siege, Jerusalem's leaders make a covenant to release their Hebrew slaves, something God's law had required all along. They seal it by cutting a calf in two and walking between the pieces. That was an ancient oath that basically said: if I break this, let me end up like this animal.

Then the Babylonian army pulls back for a while. The pressure is off. And they take the slaves back.

Be honest. That's the most human thing in the chapter. The crisis promise. God, get me through this and I'll change. Then the crisis passes, and so does the change.

God's answer is brutal irony. You wouldn't proclaim liberty? Fine. I proclaim liberty for you. Liberty to the sword, to pestilence, and to famine.

Now, the lesson isn't just try harder at keeping promises. Though the Westminster Confession takes oaths and vows very seriously, and so should you. The deeper lesson comes when you hold this next to Genesis fifteen. There, God made a covenant with Abraham in the very same way. Animals cut in two. But only God passed between the pieces. Abraham was asleep. God put the curse on Himself.

Judah's covenant failed because it rested on Judah. Our covenant stands because it rests on God. And at the cross, Christ actually bore that curse. As Paul says in Galatians three, He became a curse for us.

Then chapter thirty-five. One of the strangest and best chapters in the whole book. God tells Jeremiah to bring a family called the Rechabites into the temple and set wine in front of them. And they refuse. Why? Because roughly two hundred and fifty years earlier, their ancestor Jonadab told them: no wine, no houses, no vineyards, live in tents. And they did it. Generation after generation.

Quick theological note so nobody walks away with the wrong takeaway. This isn't God commanding everyone to abstain from alcohol. Scripture elsewhere calls wine a gift, and Presbyterians hold to Christian liberty in matters like that. The point is the contrast. God says, these people obeyed a human father's word for centuries. I have spoken to you, rising early and speaking, over and over, and you will not listen.

And then God gives the Rechabites a promise that echoes chapter thirty-three. Jonadab will never lack a man to stand before me.

Here's the application I can't shake. Jonadab built a family culture that outlasted him by two and a half centuries. What will yours outlast you by? What will your kids, or your future kids, inherit from the way you live? Not just your rules. Your faithfulness.

Ecclesiastes ten is the practical wisdom section. And honestly, it reads like a list of ways smart people sabotage themselves.

Verse one. Dead flies make the perfumer's ointment stink. A little folly outweighs wisdom and honor. You can spend ten years building a reputation, and one careless decision becomes the thing people remember. Small leaks sink ships.

Verse ten is my favorite. If the iron is blunt, and you don't sharpen the edge, you have to put in more strength. But wisdom helps you succeed. This is the oldest productivity advice in the world. A lot of people are swinging a dull axe harder and harder and calling it hustle. Sometimes the faithful move is to stop and sharpen. Rest. Learn. Pray. The Sabbath is sharpening.

Verse eighteen. Through laziness the roof sags, and through idle hands the house leaks. Neglect never announces itself. It drips.

And verse twenty. Don't curse the king, even in your thoughts, because a bird of the air will carry your voice. Translation for today: assume everything you say about someone will eventually get back to them. And speak accordingly.

The Preacher's point is that wisdom is a way of keeping your life. Keeping the edge sharp. Keeping the roof sound. Keeping your tongue.

Now John four. The disciples come back from buying food and find Jesus talking with a Samaritan woman. They're shocked, but nobody dares to ask. And she leaves her water jar, runs back into town, and says: Come, see a man who told me everything I ever did. Could this be the Christ?

Two things. First, she left the jar. The thing she came for became secondary the moment she met Him. Second, look at what her testimony actually is. He told me everything I ever did. The very thing she'd been hiding from, maybe the reason she came to draw water at noon when nobody else was around, becomes the headline of her witness. Christ doesn't ask you to pretend your past didn't happen. He takes it, and turns shame into testimony.

Then the disciples say, Rabbi, eat. And Jesus says, I have food to eat that you do not know about. My food is to do the will of Him who sent me, and to finish His work.

So here's the question. What actually feeds you? Not what you consume. What sustains you. Jesus says obedience to the Father was nourishment to Him. And Reformed theology says that matters for you personally. Because Christ's perfect obedience, what we call His active obedience, is credited to everyone who trusts in Him. He kept the Father's will completely. Where Judah broke its word. Where you and I break ours. And that brings us right back to the name. The Lord is our righteousness.

Which lands us in First Peter. Peter writes to elect exiles. Scattered people. After Jeremiah, that word should ring a bell. They are chosen according to the foreknowledge of God the Father, set apart by the Spirit, for obedience to Jesus Christ and sprinkling with His blood. The whole Trinity is at work in your salvation before you ever lift a finger.

Then Peter says this. God, in His great mercy, has caused us to be born again to a living hope, through the resurrection of Jesus Christ from the dead. To an inheritance that can't perish, can't be defiled, and can't fade. Kept in heaven for you. And you, by God's power, are being guarded through faith.

There's the word again. Kept. The inheritance is kept for you. And you are kept for the inheritance. This is what Presbyterians mean by the perseverance of the saints. It's not that you hold on tight enough. It's that you are held.

Does that mean no hardship? No. Peter says that for a little while you may be grieved by various trials. So that your faith, more precious than gold, which is tested by fire, may be found to result in praise and glory and honor when Jesus Christ is revealed. Fire doesn't destroy real gold. It proves it's real.

And then the line that gets me every time. Though you have not seen Him, you love Him. Though you do not now see Him, you believe in Him, and rejoice with joy that is inexpressible and filled with glory.

So let's pull it all together.

God keeps His covenant the way the sun keeps rising. Judah broke its word the moment the pressure lifted. A family of tent dwellers kept a father's word for two hundred and fifty years. The Preacher says keep your edge sharp and your roof sound. Jesus kept the Father's will as His daily bread. And Peter says the inheritance is kept for you, and you are kept for it.

So here's your homework this week. One. Pray first, not last. Call to Him. Two. Find the dull edge in your life, and take the time to sharpen it. Three. Think of one promise you made under pressure and quietly let slide. And keep it. And four, the most important one. Rest in the fact that your standing with God doesn't rest on your track record. It rests on His.
