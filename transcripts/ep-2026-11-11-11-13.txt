Ezekiel thirty-three, verse twenty-one. A man stumbles in from the road, a fugitive from Jerusalem, and he says it. The city has been struck down.

In Hebrew that is two words. Hukkatah ha'ir. Years of warnings, and a burned city, and a dead wife, and a silent prophet, all land in two words.

So here is today's question. When God's word finally comes true, will you be surprised?

This is Old Paths. Today is day thirteen of the eleventh month in the reading plan, day two hundred sixty-three of three hundred. The readings are Ezekiel chapters thirty-three and thirty-four, Job chapter twelve, John chapter ten, verses twenty-two through forty-two, and First John chapter two, verses twenty-four through twenty-nine.

Last time Pharaoh went down into the pit and found nothing there but company. Today two threads we have been pulling for weeks finally tie off. Since chapter three we have tracked Ezekiel's silence, a prophet bound and mute except when God gave him a word. Today his mouth is opened. And yesterday I said that John ten stands on Ezekiel thirty-four. Today you read the chapter Jesus was standing on.

Ezekiel thirty-three.

Step back and see the shape of the book. Chapters one through twenty-four are judgment on Jerusalem, ending with the siege. Chapters twenty-five through thirty-two are the oracles against the nations, which we just finished. Chapters thirty-three through forty-eight are hope. Chapter thirty-three is the hinge. As Matthew Henry puts it, the prophet has come off his circuit as judge over the nations, and now he returns to the children of his people.

So God recommissions him. Verses one through nine restate the watchman charge from chapter three. Notice one detail. In the parable, the people of the land choose their watchman. But verse seven says, So you, son of man, I have made a watchman. Henry points out that Israel would never have set this man on the wall. God did it for them.

Then verse ten. Our transgressions and our sins are upon us, and we rot away because of them. How then can we live? These are people who laughed at Ezekiel's warnings for years, and now they have swung straight into despair. Henry observes that people hardened by presumption when they are warned often sink into despair when they are called to repent.

And God answers with an oath. Verse eleven. As I live, declares the Lord God, I have no pleasure in the death of the wicked, but that the wicked turn from his way and live; turn back, turn back from your evil ways, for why will you die, O house of Israel?

That raises the hardest question in these chapters. If God takes no pleasure in the death of the wicked, why do the wicked die? And how does a Reformed man, who believes God chose his people before the foundation of the world, read this verse without blinking?

Calvin commented on these exact words where they first appear, in Ezekiel eighteen. He does not blunt them. He says God desires nothing more earnestly than that those who were perishing and rushing to destruction should return into the way of safety. Then he takes the objection head on. If someone says, then there is no election, Calvin answers, the Prophet does not here speak of God's secret counsel, but only recalls miserable men from despair, that they may apprehend the hope of pardon, and repent and embrace the offered salvation.

That is the right instinct. Deuteronomy twenty-nine says the secret things belong to the Lord, and the revealed things belong to us. Verse eleven is revealed. It is God's heart, under oath, spoken to men who think there is no way back. Calvin admits we cannot see how the two fit together. But you are not asked to solve God's decree before you obey his invitation. You are asked to turn.

Then verse twenty-one. The twelfth year of our exile, the tenth month, the fifth day. That is about January of 585 B.C., almost a year and a half after the city fell. The Syriac version reads the eleventh year instead, and the Cambridge Bible accepts that, since eleven and twelve are easily confused in Hebrew. But Keil keeps the text. With Ezekiel living far off in Mesopotamia, and the chaos that followed the catastrophe, a year and a half might very easily pass.

And verse twenty-two. The hand of the Lord had been upon me the evening before, and he had opened my mouth. Back in chapter twenty-four God promised this exact day. The man proved right about Jerusalem may speak again.

Then verses thirty through thirty-two, and I think this is the most uncomfortable paragraph in the book. The exiles talk about Ezekiel by the walls and in the doorways. Come, let's hear what the word from the Lord is today. They sit in front of him like God's people. And verse thirty-two. You are to them like one who sings lustful songs with a beautiful voice and plays well on an instrument, for they hear what you say, but they will not do it.

Ezekiel has become a concert. Matthew Henry says it plainly. Men may have their fancies pleased by the word, and yet not have their consciences touched nor their hearts changed, the itching ear gratified and yet not the corrupt nature sanctified.

Verse thirty-three answers it. When this comes, and come it will, then they will know that a prophet has been among them.

Ezekiel thirty-four.

Now God turns to the shepherds of Israel. Keil and Ellicott agree that shepherds here means the rulers, kings and princes and officials, the way Jeremiah twenty-three uses it.

Here is the Hebrew key. The word for shepherd is ro'eh, which is simply the participle of the verb ra'ah, to feed or to pasture. A shepherd is a feeder. So verse two is a bitter pun. Ah, shepherds of Israel who have been feeding yourselves! Should not shepherds feed the sheep? They are feeders who feed only on the flock.

Verse three. You eat the fat, you clothe yourselves with the wool. Augustine preached a long sermon on this chapter, and he said the milk is the support the people give their pastors, and the wool is the honor they give them. Neither is wrong to receive. The sin is taking the milk and the wool and walking away from the sheep.

Now listen to verse four, then verse sixteen. Verse four, the indictment. The weak you have not strengthened, the sick you have not healed, the injured you have not bound up, the strayed you have not brought back, the lost you have not sought. Then verse eleven. Behold, I, I myself will search for my sheep. And verse sixteen runs the same list again with God as the subject. I will seek the lost, and I will bring back the strayed, and I will bind up the injured, and I will strengthen the weak. Every failure of the shepherds, God takes on himself.

Then verse twenty-three. I will set up over them one shepherd, my servant David, and he shall feed them.

David had been dead nearly four hundred years. Keil dismisses the idea that Ezekiel expected King David to rise from the grave. The prophets already called the coming king simply David, in Hosea three and Jeremiah thirty, because he is the Branch from David's line. And notice the strange overlap. Verse fifteen says, I myself will be the shepherd of my sheep. Verse twenty-three says, my servant David will be their shepherd. Which is it? A Ligonier Tabletalk devotional on this chapter puts it well. Our Creator was revealing that He and David, the chosen Messiah and Son of David, would be one and the same.

Here the New Testament move is warranted, because Jesus deliberately takes up this chapter. I am the good shepherd, he says in John ten, and there will be one flock, one shepherd. That is Ezekiel thirty-four, verse twenty-three. The one shepherd who is both the Lord himself and David's son.

The last verse, in the ESV, says, You are my sheep, human sheep of my pasture. Spurgeon preached on that verse in November of 1884, after a run of funerals at the Tabernacle. They are men, he said, that is to say, they are only men; for the best of men are but men at the best. Then he turned it into comfort. If the promises rested on you for keeping, then they would never be kept, for you are men. But the verse ends, and I am your God.

So bring it home, and stay with verse thirty-two. You are about to spend fifteen minutes with a podcast about the Bible. You might enjoy it. That is exactly the danger. Ezekiel's hearers loved the voice. What is the one thing the text asked of you this week that you have enjoyed hearing and have not done? Name it, and do it before the song ends.

Job chapter twelve.

Zophar has finished, and now Job begins his longest answer yet. It runs through chapter fourteen and closes the first round of speeches. Henry divides chapter twelve into three parts. Job rebukes their conceit, he insists that the wicked often prosper, and then he outdoes them in praising God.

Verse two drips with sarcasm. No doubt you are the people, and wisdom will die with you. Then verse five, a line for anyone who has been on the ground. In the thought of one who is at ease there is contempt for misfortune. And verse six, the tents of robbers are at peace.

Then verses seven through ten. Ask the beasts, and they will teach you. Zophar had said, can you find out the deep things of God? Job answers, the animals know this much. Who among all these does not know that the hand of the Lord has done this? Henry notices something easy to miss. That is the only place in all the speeches between Job and his friends where the covenant name of God, Yahweh, appears.

Then verse eleven. Does not the ear test words as the palate tastes food? Gregory the Great draws out the image. For it is one thing to hear food named only, and another thing to taste of it also. Job's friends can name every dish in the theology of providence. Job, in his pain, has tasted it.

Verses thirteen through twenty-five are a hymn to God overturning the great. Counselors stripped, judges made fools, kings unbelted, priests led away. Read verse five again before you read the hymn. When someone near you slips, do you feel pity, or that quiet, comfortable contempt?

John chapter ten, verses twenty-two through forty-two.

About two months have passed since the last section. It is the Feast of Dedication, Hanukkah, which Judas Maccabaeus set up in 164 B.C. after Antiochus Epiphanes defiled the temple. Calvin notes that John's word for it means renewal. Ellicott adds that it was called the Feast of Lights, and Jesus had already called himself the light of the world.

It was winter, John says. Augustine could not let that pass. It was winter, and they were chill; because they were slow to approach that divine fire.

They surround him in Solomon's colonnade. Tell us plainly. Jesus says he has told them, and they do not believe because they are not his sheep. Then verses twenty-seven and twenty-eight. My sheep hear my voice, and I know them, and they follow me. I give them eternal life, and they will never perish, and no one will snatch them out of my hand. Remember Ezekiel thirty-four, verse ten. I will rescue my sheep from their mouths. Spurgeon pictured it this way. The sheep are in the hand of the Son, and then the Father lays his hand over the Son's. You are inside two hands.

Then verse thirty. I and the Father are one.

Here the voices split. Calvin says the ancients misused this verse to prove Christ shares the Father's essence. In context, he says, Jesus is talking about his agreement with the Father, so the Father's power stands behind his promise. Augustine and Chrysostom read it more strongly. Chrysostom argues from the flow of the passage itself. Speaking here with reference to Power, for concerning this was all His discourse; and if the power be the same, it is clear that the Essence is also. Augustine adds that the Jews understood what the Arians did not, because they picked up stones.

Calvin is right about the subject of the sentence. But I think Chrysostom follows it to its end. Who can have the Father's own power except one who is God? The men holding the stones understood him exactly.

He answers with Psalm eighty-two and says, Scripture cannot be broken. Then he goes back across the Jordan, where John first baptized. And the people say, John did no sign, but everything John said about this man was true.

First John chapter two, verses twenty-four through twenty-nine.

Last time John warned about the antichrists who went out. Now he tells the ones who stayed what to do. Abide. The word comes again and again in these few verses. Let what you heard from the beginning abide in you. Calvin is careful here. John does not mean that old is automatically true. Clinging to whatever you were first taught, simply because you were taught it, would be stubbornness, he says, not perseverance. John can say it because what they heard from the beginning was the apostles' gospel.

Verse twenty-seven sounds like it abolishes teachers. You have no need that anyone should teach you. But John is teaching them as he writes. Calvin says fanatics have twisted this verse to throw out the ministry. Augustine gives the balance. The preacher's words strike the ear, but the real Teacher is within. The anointing of the Spirit makes the truth land.

Then verse twenty-eight. Abide in him, so that when he appears we may have confidence and not shrink from him in shame at his coming. The word for confidence is parrhesia, open, unafraid speech. It is the same word the crowd used in John ten. Tell us plainly. Calvin captures what John means. But a godly confidence delights to look on God. Hence it is, that the godly calmly wait for Christ, nor do they dread his coming.

And verse twenty-nine slips in a phrase John will open up next time. Everyone who practices righteousness has been born of him.

One connection runs through all four readings today, and it is about hearing.

Ezekiel's congregation heard every word and did none of them. Job says the ear tests words the way the mouth tastes food. Jesus says, my sheep hear my voice, and they follow me. And John says the Spirit teaches from within, so that the words do not stop at the ear. Hearing the shepherd's voice is not the same as enjoying a beautiful song.

So carry this question today. Which word from God have you been enjoying that you have not yet obeyed?
