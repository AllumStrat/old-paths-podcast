We have swallowed her!

That is what Jerusalem's enemies shouted, standing in the ashes in the summer of 586 B.C. Their mouths wide open. This is the day we longed for. Now we have it. We see it.

And they were wrong. Not about the ashes. The ashes were real. They were wrong about who did it.

Four times in this morning's chapter, before the enemy ever opens its mouth, the poet has already told you who swallowed Jerusalem. And it was not Babylon.

What do you do when the hand against you is God's?

This is Old Paths. Today is day twenty-three of the tenth month in the reading plan, day two hundred forty-eight of three hundred. The readings are Lamentations chapter two, the Song of Songs chapter seven, John chapter seven, verses one through thirteen, and First Peter chapter four, verses twelve through nineteen.

Last time we opened Lamentations and sat with Zion in the ruins. She said, Is it nothing to you, all you who pass by? And she said the hardest and truest thing in the chapter, The Lord is in the right. And five times the poem tolled the same bell. No one to comfort her.

I left two things hanging. In chapter two two letters of the alphabet trade places, and we will see why. And Rashi noticed the little word like in like a widow. Today we meet like an enemy, and we have to ask whether it means the same kind of thing.

In the Song, the refrain takes its last step today. And in John, after Peter's to whom shall we go, we meet more people close to Jesus who do not believe. His own brothers.

Lamentations chapter two. If chapter one was about Zion, chapter two is about God.

Count the verbs in the first eight verses. The Lord has set the daughter of Zion under a cloud. He has cast down. He has not remembered. He has swallowed up. He has broken down. He has withdrawn his right hand. He has burned like a flaming fire. He has bent his bow. He has killed. He has poured out his fury. Nearly every verb has the same subject. Babylon is barely mentioned.

The setting has not moved. It is still the summer of 586 B.C., the month of Av, the temple burned and the walls down. But the poet has turned his eyes from the ruins to the one who made them. Matthew Henry puts his finger on it. "The grief is not so much that such and such things are done as that God has done them, that he appears angry with them; it is he that chastens them, and chastens them in wrath and in his hot displeasure; he has become their enemy, and fights against them; and this, this is the wormwood and the gall in the affliction and the misery."

The poem is another acrostic, twenty-two stanzas, and it moves in three parts. In verses one through ten the narrator describes what the Lord did. In verses eleven through nineteen the poet weeps and then speaks to Zion. In verses twenty through twenty-two Zion speaks to God.

And there is the curious detail I promised you. In chapter one the alphabet runs in the usual order, ayin and then pe. In chapters two, three, and four it flips. Pe comes first, in verse sixteen, and ayin follows in verse seventeen. John Gill collects the old guesses. Lightfoot thought it hinted at the seventy years, because ayin is the number seventy. Rashi, drawing on the Talmud, said pe means mouth and ayin means eye, so the mouth comes before the eye, because people spoke about what they had never seen. Keep that one in mind for verse fourteen.

But the simplest answer came out of the ground. At Izbet Sartah, an early Israelite village, archaeologists found a twelfth-century potsherd with a practice alphabet scratched on it, and pe comes before ayin. Ancient Israel apparently knew two orders. Lamentations is not making a mistake, though I grant the rabbi's version preaches better.

Verse one. How the Lord in his anger has set the daughter of Zion under a cloud! He has not remembered his footstool in the day of his anger.

That cloud should make you shiver. The cloud led Israel out of Egypt and filled Solomon's temple. Now it comes again in anger. And the footstool is the ark of the covenant. First Chronicles twenty-eight and Psalm ninety-nine both call it that. After 586 the ark vanishes from history. Calvin says God had not truly forgotten his footstool. It only looked that way from the ground.

Verse two. The Lord has swallowed up without mercy all the habitations of Jacob. Hold onto the word swallowed. In Hebrew it is bala. It comes twice more in verse five, again in verse eight, where destroying is literally swallowing, and then in verse sixteen the enemies take it into their own mouths.

Verse three, he has withdrawn his right hand, the hand of Exodus fifteen that shattered the enemy. Verse four, his right hand is set like a foe. The withdrawn hand turns around.

And verse five. The Lord has become like an enemy.

Here is our hard question. Is God Israel's enemy?

There is that little word again, like. Last time it was like a widow, and Rashi said her husband would return. Here it is ke'oyev, like an enemy. Does that soften it?

The commentators split. Gill reports that the Puritan Hebraist Hugh Broughton translated it, "the Lord is become a very enemy," "taking 'caph' for a note of reality, and not of similitude." Caph is the Hebrew prefix that means like. Broughton said it does not mean resembling an enemy. It means an enemy in fact. Gill himself leans the other way, and Matthew Henry is firm. "God is not really an enemy to his people, no, not when he is angry with them and corrects them in anger."

So who is right? I think both, depending on whom you mean.

First, do not let the word like make it gentle. God did everything an enemy does. He bent the bow and drew the line and filled the streets with the dead. If you were in Jerusalem that week, you could feel no difference. And many in that city were Israelites by blood and pagans by heart. For them this was not like wrath. It was wrath.

But inside that city there was a remnant, Jeremiah, Baruch, Ebed-melech, and others. For them like is the right word. Our Confession says in chapter eleven that the justified can never fall from that state, but they may by their sins fall under God's fatherly displeasure until they humble themselves and confess. Fatherly displeasure can feel exactly like enmity. The difference is the aim. An enemy wants you finished. A father wants you back.

Verses six and seven bring it to worship. He has laid waste his booth like a garden. The word is sok, a shelter, and Ellicott says it means a booth like those built at the Feast of Tabernacles. God pulled down his own house like a shed in an orchard after harvest. He has made Zion forget festival and Sabbath, and the enemy raised a clamor in the house of the Lord as on the day of festival.

Verse eight. He stretched out the measuring line, a builder's tool, used here to take a wall down. This was not rage out of control. It was measured.

Then verse nine. Her gates have sunk into the ground. Her king and princes are among the nations; the law is no more, and her prophets find no vision from the Lord.

The law is no more. Torah, instruction. No temple, no priests, no teaching. The prophets go looking for a word and find nothing. Psalm seventy-four, a lament over the burned sanctuary, says the same. There is no longer any prophet. For forty years Jeremiah spoke and they laughed, and now God is silent.

Calvin draws the lesson. "For when any promise of God is set before us, it is like a small light kindled in darkness. Though then our misery were like a thick darkness, yet when God shews some token of favor by his promises, that ought to be sufficient to give us hope and joy." The worst thing in verse nine is not the broken gates. It is the silence. Which means one of the best things you own is an open Bible.

Then verse eleven, and the poet speaks for himself. My eyes are spent with weeping, because infants and babies faint in the streets of the city. You read it. I will not linger. Only notice who is weeping. It is the man who was proved right. He predicted this for decades and he does not say, I told you so. A man who was right and still weeps is rarer than you think.

Verse thirteen continues last time's thread. What can I liken to you, that I may comfort you? He tries the oldest comfort there is. You are not the only one. And he cannot find a single comparison. Your ruin is vast as the sea; who can heal you?

And then verse fourteen. Your prophets have seen for you false and deceptive visions; they have not exposed your iniquity to restore your fortunes.

Your prophets. Not God's. You remember Hananiah breaking Jeremiah's yoke and promising the exiles home in two years. Matthew Poole says those smooth stories tickled their humors instead of uncovering their sins. And look at the logic of the verse. They did not expose your iniquity to restore your fortunes. The only road back from captivity ran through the exposure of sin. The false prophets thought they were being kind. They were blocking the only door.

Gregory the Great, writing a handbook for pastors around the year 590, used this verse on preachers who lose their nerve. "For the language of reproof is the key of discovery, because by chiding it discloses the fault of which even he who has committed it is often himself unaware." A true word about your sin is a key, not a club. It opens the door to mercy.

So here is the application, and I want you to sit with it. Who in your life is allowed to expose your iniquity? Not to humiliate you. To restore your fortunes. If the answer is no one, if every voice you let in tells you that you are fine, you have chosen your prophets the way Jerusalem did. Sit under a pastor who will let the text cut. Keep one friend who will tell you the truth. And do not punish him when he does.

Verse fifteen, the passers-by mock her with her own hymnbook, Psalm forty-eight and Psalm fifty. Is this the city that was called the perfection of beauty, the joy of all the earth? Then the pe verse, the mouth verse. They open their mouths wide. We have swallowed her! This is the day we longed for!

So who swallowed Jerusalem? Four times the poem said the Lord did. Now Babylon says, We did. And the next verse, the ayin verse, the eye verse, answers them. Verse seventeen. The Lord has done what he purposed; he has carried out his word, which he commanded long ago.

Babylon was only a mouth God used. And long ago means Moses. Even the unspeakable thing in verse twenty, mothers eating their children, is written in Deuteronomy twenty-eight, verse fifty-three.

Now set verses nine, fourteen, and seventeen side by side. No word. False words. And God keeping his true word. The false prophets believed the promises without the threats. Lamentations says God keeps all of it. And that is good news, because the same Moses promised in Deuteronomy thirty to restore their fortunes when they returned. If God kept the hard half, he will keep the other.

Here is the one place I will take you to the New Testament, because the word swallow invites it. Isaiah, writing before all this, used the same verb about the end of history. He will swallow up death forever; and the Lord God will wipe away tears from all faces. Paul quotes it in First Corinthians fifteen. Death is swallowed up in victory. In Lamentations God swallows his own city. The last swallowing in the Bible is God swallowing death. And the God who wipes away tears is the same God who made Zion weep.

Verse eighteen turns from description to command. O wall of the daughter of Zion, let tears stream down like a torrent day and night! The poet is talking to a wall. Calvin's comment is the sharpest thing in his lectures. "Hitherto no one of God's servants could draw even one tear from your eyes, so great was your hardness; but now the very walls weep, for they dissolve, as though they would send forth rivers of waters. Therefore the very stones turn to tears." For forty years the people would not cry. Now the stones do.

Then verse nineteen. Arise, cry out in the night, at the beginning of the night watches! Pour out your heart like water before the presence of the Lord! Lift your hands to him for the lives of your children.

That stanza has four lines. Every other has three. The poem breaks its own pattern at the moment it tells you to pray.

And notice where it sends her. Not away from the God who struck her. To him. Spurgeon, in his Treasury of David on Psalm sixty-two, pour out your heart before him, has this. "Turn the vessel of your soul upside down in his secret presence, and let your inmost thoughts, desires, sorrows, and sins be poured out like water. Hide nothing from him, for you can hide nothing."

So in the last three verses Zion does it. Look, O Lord, and see! With whom have you dealt thus? She is reminding God whose people these are. Calvin says she is setting before him his covenant with the fathers. She does not plead her worthiness. She pleads his choosing.

And the chapter closes in verse twenty-two by turning the festivals of verse six inside out. You summoned as if to a festival day my terrors on every side. Terror on every side, magor missabib, was Jeremiah's signature phrase. In chapter twenty he gave it, from the Lord, as a new name to Pashhur, the priest who put him in the stocks. The pilgrims who used to stream up to Jerusalem from every side for the feasts have been replaced by armies.

The poem stops there. No answer yet. But she is talking to God, and that is not nothing.

The Song of Songs, chapter seven. You heard the call at the end of chapter six. Return, return, O Shulammite, that we may look upon you. Why should you look upon the Shulammite, as upon a dance before two armies? In the Hebrew Bible that verse is actually chapter seven, verse one, which tells you how closely the two belong together. She is dancing. And so this third song of praise, what scholars call a wasf, a descriptive love song, runs the opposite direction from chapter four. Chapter four started at her eyes and moved down. This one starts at her feet and moves up. Ellicott's guess is the natural one. When a woman dances, you see her feet first.

Verse one. How beautiful are your feet in sandals, O noble daughter! Noble daughter is bat nadib. She was a vineyard girl from the country in chapter one, sunburned and embarrassed. Now she is called a prince's daughter.

Then the images climb. Her eyes are pools in Heshbon, the old royal city of Sihon across the Jordan, with its great reservoir. Still water that shimmers. Her nose is like a tower of Lebanon looking toward Damascus, a frontier watchtower. Her head crowns you like Carmel, the headland over the sea. And verse five ends with a smile. A king is held captive in the tresses. Solomon has armies and chariots, and he has been taken prisoner by her hair.

Be honest about what this is. It is a husband's frank delight in his wife's body, and verse eight, I will climb the palm tree, is plainly about the marriage bed. God put that in your Bible. Do not hunt for a symbol in every detail. The point is the whole. She is beautiful, and he says so.

Then in verse nine she interrupts him, and verse ten is the line I told you was coming. I am my beloved's, and his desire is for me.

Three times now we have heard this refrain. Chapter two, my beloved is mine and I am his. Chapter six, I am my beloved's and my beloved is mine. And now, I am my beloved's, and his desire is for me. She does not even mention her claim on him anymore. Hudson Taylor called this last stage all of him and none of self.

But here is the word I most want you to see. Desire is teshuqah. It is one of the rarest words in the Old Testament. It appears only three times. Genesis three, verse sixteen, to the woman after the fall: your desire shall be for your husband, and he shall rule over you. Genesis four, sin's desire is for Cain. And here.

In Genesis the woman's desire meets a husband who rules. In the Song, the man's desire turns toward her, and nobody rules anybody. The curse of the fall is not the last word on marriage. Matthew Poole saw it in the seventeenth century. Reading the bride as the church, he says her desire toward her husband is her duty, according to God's sentence in Genesis three, "but it is a wonderful condescension in him, that his desire is towards me." Scholars argue about exactly what desire means in Genesis three, and I will not settle that here. But the echo is real. The Song is a glimpse of love put back the way God made it.

So here is the practical word for you as a husband. Does your wife know that your desire is for her? Not only in the bedroom, but in your attention, your time, your words. The fall bent marriage toward grasping and ruling. Grace bends it back toward desire that gives.

Then in verses eleven through thirteen the bride takes the lead. Come, my beloved, let us go out into the fields. There I will give you my love. She wants him alone, away from court.

Verse thirteen, the mandrakes give forth fragrance. Dudaim, love apples, from dod, beloved. The only other place they appear is Genesis thirty, where Rachel bargains with Leah for them. In the Song nobody bargains. And beside our doors are all choice fruits, new as well as old, which I have laid up for you.

The church has always heard Christ and his people here too. James Durham, the Scottish Presbyterian, asked why the bridegroom praises her yet again, a third time, and longer than before. His answer, in modern spelling, is that "He is not kind only at fits, as men sometimes use to be, and do not continue." At fits means in fits and starts. Human affection runs hot and cold. His does not.

And the bride's answer is to lay up fruit for him, new as well as old. Old graces kept, new ones added. Ask yourself what you are saving for him.

John chapter seven, verses one through thirteen. John jumps about six months. Chapter six was near Passover in the spring. Now it is autumn, and the Feast of Booths is at hand.

Booths, Sukkot, was the happiest week of Israel's year, seven days living in shelters of branches to remember the wilderness, and a harvest home. Every Jewish man was expected to go up, so the roads from Galilee filled with caravans. Hold on to the feast, because next time Jesus will stand up in the middle of it and cry, If anyone thirsts.

Verse one. He would not go about in Judea, because the Jews were seeking to kill him. In John that often means the authorities in Jerusalem. The crowds in verse thirteen are Jewish too, and they fear the Jews.

Then his brothers speak. Leave here and go to Judea, that your disciples also may see the works you are doing. If you do these things, show yourself to the world. And John adds the saddest aside in the chapter. For not even his brothers believed in him.

Mark six names them, James, Joses, Judas, and Simon, most naturally his younger half brothers. They grew up at the same table and saw his life up close for thirty years. And they did not believe.

But notice what kind of unbelief it is. They are not mocking him exactly. They are managing him. R. C. Sproul, preaching on this text, says, "The brothers are the self-appointed managers of Jesus' political campaign." Go to the capital. Do your miracles where they count. Get on the platform. They believe in a Jesus they want, a celebrity Messiah, and that is just another kind of unbelief.

Then Jesus answers in verse six. My time has not yet come, but your time is always here. The word is kairos, the right moment. Any day will do for them. He is on the Father's schedule, and he will not be hurried by his brothers any more than by his mother at Cana.

Verse seven. The world cannot hate you, but it hates me because I testify about it that its works are evil. Bishop Ryle puts it in one sentence. "In short, they could have tolerated His opinions if He would only have spared their sins." The world can make room for a teacher. It cannot make room for a witness against its works. And that is a question for you. If the world finds nothing in you to resist, is that because you are wise, or because you have stopped testifying?

Then verse eight. You go up to the feast. I am not going up to this feast. Verse ten, after his brothers had gone up, then he also went up, not publicly but in private.

Did Jesus lie? The pagan philosopher Porphyry used this verse in the third century to call him fickle. Many manuscripts read not yet, and the King James follows them. But even without the yet, there is no lie. The verb is present tense. I am not going up now, with you, in the caravan, as a public show. And he did not. He went later, quietly, to teach. Cyril of Alexandria insists that Jesus never said one thing and did another, because guile was never found in his mouth. He refused their kind of going up. His public entry into Jerusalem comes at the next Passover, on a donkey, and it will be the road to the cross.

Verses eleven through thirteen. Where is he? And there was much muttering about him among the people. Muttering is the same word John used for the grumbling over the bread in chapter six. Some said, He is a good man. Others said, No, he is leading the people astray. Notice that neither verdict is enough. Leading the people astray is the charge Deuteronomy thirteen brings against a false prophet. Good man sounds kind, but a merely good man does not say what he has been saying. And verse thirteen, for fear of the Jews no one spoke openly of him.

Before we leave it, there is a happy ending to the brothers. Chrysostom saw it sixteen centuries ago. "But observe, I pray you, the power of Christ. Of those who said these things, one became first Bishop of Jerusalem, the blessed James." First Corinthians fifteen says the risen Jesus appeared to James. Acts one puts his brothers in the upper room, praying. One of them wrote the letter of James and another the letter of Jude. If someone in your family is still at the stage of John seven, verse five, do not give up. Jesus' own brothers were there once.

First Peter chapter four, verses twelve through nineteen. Last time Peter said, The end of all things is at hand, and told the churches to love and serve. Now he starts the last movement of the letter with the same word he used in chapter two. Beloved.

Verse twelve. Beloved, do not be surprised at the fiery trial when it comes upon you to test you, as though something strange were happening to you.

Do not be surprised is xenizo again, the word from verse four. The pagans were surprised that Christians would not run with them. Now Peter says, Do not you be surprised when they turn on you. They think you are strange. Do not think your suffering is strange.

The fiery trial is one word, pyrosis, a burning. It picks up chapter one, faith tested by fire like gold. The fire is for testing, not destroying. Peter is probably writing from Rome around A.D. 63 or 64, and Nero's burning of Christians as torches after the fire of 64 may be just over the horizon. But what the letter describes so far is slander and insult.

Verse thirteen. Rejoice insofar as you share Christ's sufferings, that you may also rejoice when his glory is revealed. Joy now, because suffering for him is fellowship with him, and greater joy later.

Verse fourteen. If you are insulted for the name of Christ, you are blessed, because the Spirit of glory and of God rests upon you. That is Isaiah eleven, the Spirit resting on the shoot of Jesse. The Spirit that rested on the Messiah rests on those insulted for his name.

Then verses fifteen and sixteen draw a line. Let none of you suffer as a murderer or a thief or an evildoer or as a meddler. Meddler is allotriepiskopos, found nowhere in Greek before Peter, an overseer of other people's business. John Gill, with a Baptist's dry wit, offers the rendering a bishop in another man's diocese. Being disliked for being a busybody is not persecution.

Yet if anyone suffers as a Christian, let him not be ashamed, but let him glorify God in that name. Christian appears only three times in the New Testament, a label outsiders pinned on believers at Antioch. Within fifty years, in the very provinces Peter addresses, Pliny the governor of Bithynia was asking the emperor Trajan whether to punish the name itself, and executing those who kept confessing it. Tertullian, writing around the year 197, mocked the way pagans talked about believers. "A good man, says one, is Gaius Seius, only that he is a Christian." A good man, except for the one thing that makes him good.

Then verse seventeen. For it is time for judgment to begin at the household of God; and if it begins with us, what will be the outcome for those who do not obey the gospel of God?

Peter is reaching back to Ezekiel nine, begin at my sanctuary, and Jeremiah twenty-five, disaster beginning at the city called by my name. For the church that judgment is not condemnation. Christ bore that. It is the refining fire of Malachi three. A father corrects his own children first. But Peter argues from the lesser to the greater. If God is this serious with the people he loves, what will it be for those who refuse his gospel?

Verse eighteen quotes Proverbs eleven in the Greek. If the righteous is scarcely saved, what will become of the ungodly and the sinner?

Scarcely saved. Does that mean you barely make it, that heaven is a coin toss? No. Calvin is emphatic that Peter means the present road, not the final verdict. Our life, he says, is like sailing between rocks through a thousand deaths, but we are in no danger of shipwreck while God is our pilot. The salvation is certain. The road is hard.

And Spurgeon, preaching on this verse, turned the hardness into comfort. If you find the Christian life a struggle, that is not proof you are lost. "A dead fish finds no difficulty in floating down the stream, it is only the living fish that can swim against the current." The struggle is a sign of life.

Then verse nineteen. Therefore let those who suffer according to God's will entrust their souls to a faithful Creator while doing good.

Entrust is paratithemi, to hand over something for safekeeping, like a deposit. It is the very word Jesus used on the cross, quoting Psalm thirty-one. Father, into your hands I commit my spirit. Peter tells suffering Christians to pray the dying prayer of Jesus. And he calls God Creator. The one who made you can keep you.

But notice the last three words. While doing good. Entrusting yourself to God is not sitting still. You hand your soul to God and you go on doing good to the people who are hurting you.

Two connections before we go, and both of them are real.

First, Lamentations two, verse six. He has laid waste his booth; the Lord has made Zion forget festival. And in John seven, six centuries later, the booths are back. The pilgrims are streaming up to Jerusalem from Galilee. The festival is not forgotten anymore. God kept his promise to restore the fortunes of Zion. And standing quietly in that crowd, not yet showing himself, is the one Israel's whole calendar was waiting for.

Second, Peter's line. It is time for judgment to begin at the household of God. Peter is echoing Ezekiel nine, a vision given six years before the fall, of this very judgment beginning at the sanctuary. Lamentations two is what it looked like when judgment began at the house of God. Peter tells the church that the same God still begins with his own house, but for those in Christ the fire is not the end of them. It is a refiner's fire. And the command in both books is the same. Lamentations says, Pour out your heart like water before the presence of the Lord. Peter says, Entrust your souls to a faithful Creator. When the hand on you is God's, you do not run from him. You run to him.

So here is your question for today. When God's hand is heavy on you, do you take your heart to him, or do you take it everywhere else?

Next time we read Lamentations chapter three, where the darkest chapter turns into great is your faithfulness, the Song of Songs chapter eight, verses one through seven, love strong as death, John seven, verses fourteen through twenty-four, where Jesus teaches openly at the feast, and First Peter five, verses one through seven, cast all your anxieties on him.

This has been Old Paths. Grace and peace.
