Moab has been at ease from his youth and has settled on his dregs; he has not been emptied from vessel to vessel, nor has he gone into exile; so his taste remains in him, and his scent is not changed.

That is God describing a nation as a jar of wine left too long in the cellar. Nobody has moved it. Nobody has poured it off. Centuries of quiet, and the wine has gone thick and harsh in the dark.

Most of us would call that kind of life a blessing. No upheavals, no moves, no losses, no exile. Jeremiah calls it the problem.

So here is the question for today. What if the things that keep unsettling your life are not God's neglect of you, but his care?

This is Old Paths. Today is day seventeen of the tenth month in the reading plan. The readings are Jeremiah forty-seven and forty-eight, the Song of Songs chapter two, John chapter five, verses thirty-one through forty-seven, and First Peter chapter two, verses nine through seventeen.

Last time Jeremiah turned from Judah to the nations. Chapter forty-six announced Egypt's defeat at Carchemish and ended with a promise to Jacob: of you I will not make a full end. Today the prophet keeps walking the map. He goes west to the Philistine coast, and then east across the Jordan to Moab. Two neighbors, two very different endings, and Moab's oracle, the longest in the section until Babylon, closes with a word nobody expects.

We also follow three threads from last time. In the Song of Songs the sunburned bride confessed she had not kept her own vineyard, and today someone asks for help catching the foxes in it. In John, Jesus has claimed equality with the Father, and now he calls his witnesses. And in First Peter, the priesthood of believers we met in verse five gets its full description in verse nine.

Jeremiah forty-seven first. It is seven verses, and it begins with a date that is harder than it looks. The word of the Lord that came to Jeremiah the prophet concerning the Philistines, before Pharaoh struck down Gaza. Which Pharaoh, and when? The Greek historian Herodotus says that Pharaoh Neco, after his victory at Megiddo in 609 B.C., the battle where King Josiah died, went on to take a great city he calls Kadytis, and many identify that with Gaza. Calvin thought instead of Pharaoh Hophra, around 588, plundering Gaza on his way home from his brief attempt to relieve Jerusalem. The text does not settle it, and it does not need to. The point of the heading is that the word came while Philistia was still standing and the threat seemed to be Egypt in the south.

But verse two says the danger comes from somewhere else. Behold, waters are rising out of the north, and shall become an overflowing torrent. North means Babylon. Verse three gives one of the most terrible pictures in the prophets. At the rumbling of the chariot wheels, the fathers look not back to their children, so feeble are their hands. Panic so complete that a father runs and does not turn around.

We have already met the fulfillment. Back in chapter thirty-six, Jehoiakim's officials proclaimed a fast in the month of Kislev, 604 B.C. That was the month the Babylonian Chronicle says Nebuchadnezzar captured Ashkelon, took its king, and turned the city into a mound and heaps of ruins. Verse five: Ashkelon has perished. There is a remarkable letter from about this time, written in Aramaic on papyrus and found at Saqqara in Egypt. A Philistine king named Adon writes to Pharaoh that the army of the king of Babylon has reached Aphek, and he begs Pharaoh to send a force and rescue us. No force came. That is verse four exactly. The Lord will cut off from Tyre and Sidon every helper that remains. And verse four calls them the remnant of the coastland of Caphtor, which is almost certainly Crete. The Philistines came to Canaan from the sea, and within about two centuries of Jeremiah they had disappeared from history as a distinct people.

Then come verses six and seven, the heart of the chapter. Ah, sword of the Lord! How long till you are quiet? Put yourself into your scabbard; rest and be still! And then the answer. How can it be quiet when the Lord has given it a charge? Against Ashkelon and against the seashore he has appointed it.

Who is crying out to the sword? Here the commentators part company, and I find the disagreement useful. Calvin says the prophet is playing a part, speaking as the Philistines would speak. "Here the Prophet assumes the character of another, as though he wished to soothe with blandishments the sword of God, and mitigate its fury." And then, firmly: "The Prophet, it is certain, had no such feeling." For Calvin, the whole point is verse seven. The plea is raised only so that it can be refused.

Matthew Poole, the Puritan, allows that reading but leans the other way. Others, he says, make these the words of the prophet himself, lamenting the havoc he foresaw, "for good men are affected with the miseries even of the worst of men." The Philistines had been Israel's enemies since Samson. And still Jeremiah cannot watch the sword fall without crying, how long?

Hold on to that question, because chapter forty-eight will press it harder.

But first, one word. In verse six, be quiet is the Hebrew verb shaqat, to be at rest, undisturbed. Keep it in your ear, because Jeremiah uses the same root a few verses later about Moab. The sword cannot be quiet, and the reason, as we will see, is that Moab has been quiet far too long.

Now chapter forty-eight, forty-seven verses on one small nation. Moab sat on the high plateau east of the Dead Sea. Its people descended from Lot. Its king Balak hired Balaam to curse Israel. Moab oppressed Israel under Eglon in the days of the judges, and Moabite raiders harassed Judah under Jehoiakim. Yet Ruth came from Moab.

The chapter opens with a roll call of towns. Woe to Nebo, for it is laid waste! Kiriathaim is put to shame. Heshbon, Horonaim, Dibon, Aroer, Jahaz, Beth-diblathaim, Beth-gamul, Beth-meon. To us it sounds like a list from an old atlas. But in 1868 a missionary named Frederick Klein was shown a black basalt stone at Dibon. It is the Mesha Stele, now in the Louvre, set up around 840 B.C. by Mesha king of Moab to celebrate throwing off Israel's rule. It is the earliest known mention of the name of Israel's God outside the Bible. Mesha writes that Omri king of Israel oppressed Moab many days, and Chemosh was angry. Then, he says, Chemosh told him to go and take Nebo from Israel, and he boasts, I took from it the vessels of Jehovah, and offered them before Chemosh. Mesha lists the towns he built or took, among them Kiriathaim, Aroer, Beth-diblathaim, Horonaim, and Jahaz.

Now read Jeremiah forty-eight with that stone in your mind. Jeremiah walks town by town through the very cities Mesha boasted Chemosh had given him, and announces that each one will fall. And verse seven tells you why. Because you trusted in your works and your treasures, you also shall be taken; and Chemosh shall go into exile with his priests and his officials. Mesha said Chemosh drove out Israel. Jeremiah says Chemosh will be loaded on a cart and hauled off to Babylon with everyone else. Verse thirteen adds, then Moab shall be ashamed of Chemosh, as the house of Israel was ashamed of Bethel, their confidence. The northern kingdom trusted its golden calf at Bethel, and it went into exile anyway. A god you can carry cannot carry you.

Then comes verse ten, one of the most quoted and most misquoted verses in Jeremiah. Cursed is he who does the work of the Lord with slackness, and cursed is he who keeps back his sword from bloodshed. The older translations say deceitfully, but the Hebrew word remiyyah means slack, negligent, the slack hand of Proverbs.

You have probably heard the first half from a pulpit, about doing the Lord's work wholeheartedly. Calvin would not have been pleased. "This passage has been very absurdly explained, and it is commonly quoted as though the Prophet had said, that special care ought to be taken by us, not to omit anything of what God commands. But they thus misrepresent the meaning." In context, the work of the Lord is the destruction of Moab, and the curse is on any Babylonian soldier who goes easy. Calvin's point is that Babylon's army, marching out of greed and ambition, was still doing God's work, whether it knew it or not.

John Trapp, the Puritan, read the same verse and did exactly what Calvin warned against. He grants that the destroying of Moab is mainly meant. "But the text taketh in all lawful employments; these are God's works, and must be done vigorously, with all our might, in obedience to God, and for his greatest glory."

Who is right? Calvin is right about the verse. It is a word to executioners, and we should not hang a sermon on half of it while ignoring the sword in the other half. And frankly, the second half has been used to bless wars it was never written for. But Trapp's principle is true. It just stands on other texts, such as Malachi's curse on the man who offers God a blemished animal while a good one is in his flock, or Proverbs on the slack hand. Read the verse for what it says. God's judgment, when it comes, is not done by halves.

Now verse eleven, our opening verse, and I think the most important in the chapter. Moab has been at ease from his youth and has settled on his dregs. At ease is sha'anan, complacent. And the next phrase, in the Hebrew, says Moab has been shoqet on his dregs, quiet, undisturbed. That is the same root as the sword in chapter forty-seven, verse six. The sword cannot be quiet, because Moab has been quiet too long.

The picture comes from the winery. New wine was left in jars to let the sediment, the lees, sink to the bottom. The Hebrew word is shemarim. Then the vintner carefully poured the clear wine off into a fresh jar, and again, and again. That racking clarified the wine and refined its flavor. Wine left sitting too long on its lees turns thick and harsh. Zephaniah uses the same image for the men of Jerusalem who say in their hearts, the Lord will not do good, nor will he do ill.

So Moab has never been poured. No exile, no upheaval, from his youth. So his taste remains in him, and his scent is not changed. Moab's character in Jeremiah's day was the same as in Balak's. And verse twelve says the pouring is coming now, all at once. I shall send to him pourers who will pour him, and empty his vessels and break his jars in pieces.

Trapp, who loved a pun, notices where Moab lived, on the shore of the Dead Sea. "And his ease hath destroyed him." Then, "He dwelleth near the mare mortuum, and is become a very mare mortuum." Mare mortuum is Latin for the Dead Sea. Moab lived beside a dead sea and became one. And Trapp finishes, "Because he hath had no changes, therefore he feareth not God." That comes almost straight from Psalm fifty-five in the old King James wording: because they have no changes, therefore they fear not God.

On Sunday the twenty-first of July, 1867, Charles Spurgeon preached on this verse at the Metropolitan Tabernacle. That same Sunday, across London at Gower Street Chapel, the Strict Baptist J. C. Philpot was preaching on the same text. Spurgeon turned it on believers, and on himself, admitting he feared he was suffering from the very disease he was describing. And he said this about how God cures it. "His usual way is by pouring our settled wine from vessel to vessel. If we cannot handle prosperity, the Lord will not give it to us."

Calvin adds a pastoral note. Faithful Israelites watching Moab in those years could easily envy it. Judah was being besieged and deported, while Moab across the Jordan sat quiet with its flocks and vineyards. Calvin says the prophet is telling them not to envy the happiness of the Moabites, because God would at last stretch out his hand against them. That is Psalm thirty-seven. Fret not yourself because of evildoers.

Then the reasons pile up. Verse twenty-six: make him drunk, because he magnified himself against the Lord. Verse twenty-seven: was not Israel a derision to you? Whenever you spoke of him you wagged your head. Moab mocked Judah's fall. Then verse twenty-nine. We have heard of the pride of Moab, he is very proud, of his loftiness, his pride, and his arrogance, and the haughtiness of his heart. Six words for pride in one verse, four of them from a single Hebrew root, ga'ah, to rise up. Jeremiah heaps the words up like Moab heaped up its own self-praise. We have heard, he says, and Calvin observes how. The Moabites were heralds of their own power. Their own tongues testified against them.

Now the hardest question in the passage. Listen to verses thirty-one and thirty-six. Therefore I wail for Moab; I cry out for all Moab. Therefore my heart moans for Moab like a flute. The flute is the funeral pipe, the instrument of mourners. And verse thirty-three is the Lord speaking: I have made the wine cease from the winepresses. So who is weeping here? And how can the one who decrees a judgment also mourn it?

Calvin again says this is the prophet taking on the voice of others. Jeremiah does not mourn for Moab. He paints how Moab will mourn, so that the coming calamity feels real. John Gill, the Baptist, follows him. Matthew Henry and Poole take the tears at face value. The prophet means it. And the Lutheran scholars Keil and Delitzsch quote the medieval Jewish commentator Rashi, who saw something important here. Rashi said that "the prophets of Israel differ from heathen prophets like Balaam in this, that they lay to heart the distress which they announce to the nations."

That is a sharp contrast, because Balaam is in this chapter. Moab hired him to curse Israel for money. He would have been glad to announce disaster for a fee. Jeremiah announces disaster on Moab, and it breaks his heart.

Here is where I come down. I think the tears are real, and I think Calvin's concern is also right. The tears do not mean the judgment is uncertain or unjust. Verse thirty-eight is plain: I have broken Moab like a vessel for which no one cares, declares the Lord. But the God who breaks Moab is the God who says through Ezekiel, I have no pleasure in the death of the wicked. And Isaiah, in the chapters Jeremiah is echoing here, says the same: my inner parts moan like a lyre for Moab. Notice that Jeremiah quotes Isaiah fifteen and sixteen all through this chapter, and in one place he changes Isaiah's raisin cakes of Kir-hareseth into the men of Kir-hareseth. Isaiah grieved for the vineyards. Jeremiah grieves for the people. The prophet's sorrow is not a crack in God's purpose. It is a window onto God's heart.

This is one of the places where the New Testament genuinely completes the picture. The Lord Jesus stood on the Mount of Olives, announced Jerusalem's destruction, and wept over the city as he did it. Judgment and tears in the same mouth. Jeremiah prefigured that, and Jesus fulfilled it.

And that should shape you. If you have to tell someone a hard truth about where their life is heading, about sin, about judgment, Rashi's line is the test. Do you lay it to heart, or do you enjoy being right?

The chapter ends with two more surprises. Verses forty-five and forty-six quote the book of Numbers. A fire has gone out from Heshbon, a flame from the house of Sihon; it has destroyed the forehead of Moab. Woe to you, O Moab! The people of Chemosh are undone. That is the old victory song from Numbers twenty-one, mixed with Balaam's own oracle in Numbers twenty-four, the star out of Jacob and the scepter that shall crush the forehead of Moab. Moab hired Balaam to curse Israel, and centuries later Balaam's words are the epitaph on Moab's tomb.

And then the last verse. Yet I will restore the fortunes of Moab in the latter days, declares the Lord. Thus far is the judgment on Moab.

Restore the fortunes is shub shebut, literally turn the turning, the phrase Jeremiah uses over and over for Israel's return from exile. Here it is given to Moab. Moab never came back as a nation. Josephus says Nebuchadnezzar made war on the Moabites five years after Jerusalem fell, in 582 B.C., and they slowly faded from history. So what does the promise mean?

Matthew Henry, Poole, and Trapp all read it as the calling of the Gentiles in the days of the Messiah. Calvin says it was given mainly for Judah's comfort. If God shows mercy even to Moab, how much more to his own people? And then Calvin points out that God had never wholly given up on Moab, because from that nation Christ himself descended, through Ruth the Moabitess, the great-grandmother of David. Notice that the latter days in verse forty-seven echo Balaam, who prophesied the star and the scepter for the latter days. The same oracle that crushes Moab's forehead brings a King, and that King has Moabite blood.

So bring it home. Go back to verse eleven. Moab's tragedy was a life that was never disturbed. If you are a Christian, God will not leave you on your lees. He pours. A move you did not choose, a job that ended, a diagnosis, a church that broke apart, a season in which every jar you settled into was tipped over. Those are not proofs that God has forgotten you. They may be the clearest sign that he has not. And when you look across the river at people whose lives never seem to be shaken, and who never think of God at all, do not envy them. A quiet life is no proof of a blessed one. Ask God to keep you from settling, and when the pouring comes, trust the hand that tilts the jar.

Now the Song of Songs, chapter two. Last time we met a bride, sunburned from working her brothers' vineyards, confessing that her own vineyard she had not kept. Chapter two finishes the Song's first movement and then opens a spring poem.

She begins modestly. I am a rose of Sharon, a lily of the valleys. Forget the garden rose. The ESV note says it is probably a bulb, a crocus or a narcissus, a wildflower of the coastal plain. She is saying, I am nobody special, just a meadow flower. Many hymns put this verse in Christ's mouth, but the Hebrew puts it in hers. He answers instantly. As a lily among brambles, so is my love among the young women. If you are only a wildflower, then every other woman is a thornbush. That is how love talks. She lowers herself, and he lifts her up.

Then the house of wine and the banner, a public sign over her. This love is not hidden. And then the refrain that will come back twice more in the book: I adjure you, O daughters of Jerusalem, by the gazelles or the does of the field, that you not stir up or awaken love until it pleases. The point is timing. Love is strong. Do not force it, rush it, or wake it before its time, which in this book means marriage. Older translations said, until he please, and that made some older interpreters read love as a person, so Poole could gloss it, until he please, meaning never. Translation matters. The modern reading is simpler and very practical. If you are single, or you are raising teenagers, here is wisdom from God in a love song. Do not light that fire early.

Then the spring poem, verses eight through seventeen, framed by a gazelle on the mountains at both ends. He comes leaping, stands at the lattice, and calls her: Arise, my love, my beautiful one, and come away, for behold, the winter is past. Flowers, figs, the voice of the turtledove. And then verse fifteen. Catch the foxes for us, the little foxes that spoil the vineyards, for our vineyards are in blossom.

Last time her vineyard was neglected. Now it is in blossom, and blossom is when a vineyard is most fragile. Foxes do not uproot vines. They gnaw shoots and dig holes. It is small damage, done early, that ruins the harvest. In the plain sense this is a young couple guarding a love that is just beginning. Matthew Henry calls the foxes the first risings of sin. Hudson Taylor lists little compromises with the world and disobedience in little things. Spurgeon makes the point that a big sin will not destroy a true believer, but a small one can make him miserable. Married or not, what small thing are you letting gnaw at what is in bloom?

Then verse sixteen. My beloved is mine, and I am his. James Durham, the Scottish Presbyterian who preached through this book in the 1650s, says this mutual belonging, not the feeling of his nearness, is what holds her up. "This relation, which the believer hath to Christ, is the great ground of his happiness and consolation, and not any sensible presence." Sensible there means felt. The relation is the ground, even on days when the feeling is gone. And Bernard of Clairvaux, preaching on the same verse, reminds us whose love came first. "For His love is greater than yours, and it is before yours."

And the chapter closes with a not yet. Until the day breathes and the shadows flee, turn, my beloved. Love is real, and promised, and waits.

John chapter five, verses thirty-one through forty-seven. Jesus has claimed to do the Father's works, give life, and judge. Now he calls witnesses, because this is a courtroom. Verse thirty-one. If I alone bear witness about myself, my testimony is not true. That sounds strange next to chapter eight, where he says, even if I do bear witness about myself, my testimony is true. John Gill gives the cleanest answer. True here means legally valid. Deuteronomy nineteen requires two or three witnesses, and no court accepts a man's word about himself alone. So Jesus says, fine, I will not rest on my own word, and he brings four witnesses.

The first is John the Baptist. He was a burning and shining lamp, and you were willing to rejoice for a while in his light. A lamp, not the light. Augustine says a lamp in the night is meant to make you look for the sunrise. They enjoyed the lamp and ignored the sun. The second witness is the works the Father gave him to do. The third is the Father himself. And the fourth is the Scriptures.

Verse thirty-nine is famous, and it can be read two ways. The Greek word can be a command, search the Scriptures, or a statement, you search the Scriptures. The King James Version has the command. Chrysostom and Augustine read it that way, and so did Calvin. But Cyril of Alexandria, in the fifth century, disagreed with what everyone else was saying. "The smooth, and passable to the many, and beaten explanation of the passage persuades us to suppose that it was spoken in the imperative mood." Cyril says no. Jesus is blaming them, because they search, and still, verse forty, you refuse to come to me that you may have life. The ESV agrees with Cyril.

I think Cyril is right, and the warning is sobering. These men knew their Bibles better than most of us ever will. They searched, and they missed the one the whole book was about. Verse forty says the problem was not information. You refuse to come. The word is will.

Then verse forty-four goes to the root. How can you believe, when you receive glory from one another and do not seek the glory that comes from the only God? J. C. Ryle, the bishop of Liverpool, has a wonderful picture of the man who wants faith but also wants the approval of people. "He does not see that he is like a child sitting on the lid of his box, and wishing to open it, but not considering that his own weight keeps it shut." The weight on the lid is his own reputation.

And then the tables turn. Jesus has been the defendant. Now he names the prosecutor. Do not think that I will accuse you to the Father. There is one who accuses you: Moses, on whom you have set your hope. For if you believed Moses, you would believe me; for he wrote of me. Deuteronomy eighteen promised a prophet like Moses, to whom you shall listen. The man they trusted to defend them will testify against them. So ask yourself where you need the lid lifted. Whose approval are you sitting on?

First Peter, chapter two, verses nine through seventeen. Last time Peter called believers living stones and a holy priesthood. Now, verse nine, he piles up Israel's titles from Exodus nineteen. But you are a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, a people for his own possession, that you may proclaim the excellencies of him who called you out of darkness into his marvelous light. Moses said those words to Israel at Sinai, and Peter gives them to scattered churches of Jews and Gentiles in Asia Minor. Augustine says we call all believers priests because they are members of the one Priest. Your priesthood is not something you own apart from Christ. It comes from being joined to him.

Verse ten borrows from Hosea, whose children were named Not My People and No Mercy. Once you were not a people, but now you are God's people.

Then verse eleven. Beloved, I urge you as sojourners and exiles to abstain from the passions of the flesh, which wage war against your soul. A sojourner lives in a place without belonging to it. An unknown second-century Christian, writing what we call the Letter to Diognetus, described believers this way. "They dwell in their own countries, but simply as sojourners. As citizens, they share in all things with others, and yet endure all things as if foreigners."

Verse twelve says the pagans will speak against you as evildoers, so keep your conduct honorable, so that they may see your good deeds and glorify God on the day of visitation. Sinclair Ferguson notes that Peter is not telling us to manufacture spiritual conversations. He assumes the world will notice something different. "Therefore, our lives speak with a different accent." People ask where you are from when they hear an accent.

Then the hard part, verses thirteen through seventeen. Be subject for the Lord's sake to every human institution, whether it be to the emperor as supreme. The emperor at the time was Nero, and Peter would die under him. Peter is not naive about rulers. He says, for the Lord's sake. Verse sixteen sets the frame. Live as people who are free, not using your freedom as a cover-up for evil, but living as servants of God. Calvin catches the paradox in a phrase. "In short, it is a free servitude, and a serving freedom."

Is there a limit? Look at verse seventeen. Honor everyone. Love the brotherhood. Fear God. Honor the emperor. Proverbs twenty-four in the Greek Old Testament says, fear God and the king. Peter changes it. Fear goes to God alone. The emperor gets honor, the same word Peter uses for everyone. The Westminster Confession, chapter twenty-three, puts it this way: we owe magistrates honor, taxes, prayer, and obedience to their lawful commands, and a ruler's unbelief does not cancel his authority. Tertullian, writing to a persecuting Roman governor, said Christians honor the emperor as far as is lawful for them and good for him. We honor, pray, pay, and obey, until obeying would mean we no longer fear God.

Two connections before we go, and I think both are really in the text.

First, Moab and Peter's churches. Moab's whole problem, verse eleven said, was that it had never gone into exile. It had never been poured out, and it settled and spoiled. Peter tells his churches, you are exiles. They had been poured from vessel to vessel by persecution and scattering. To Moab, exile was the judgment it never feared. To the Christian, exile is a calling, and part of how God keeps us from settling on our lees.

Second, the last verse of Moab's oracle and Peter's verse ten. The law said no Moabite could enter the assembly of the Lord. Then a Moabite widow named Ruth said to Naomi, your people shall be my people, and your God my God. Yet I will restore the fortunes of Moab. Once you were not a people, but now you are God's people. Ruth is that verse in a single life.

So here is a question to carry through your day. Where is God pouring you from vessel to vessel right now, and will you call it neglect, or will you call it care?
