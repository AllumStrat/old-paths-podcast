There is a crack running down a mud-brick wall in Jerusalem. Everyone can see it. A man walks up with a bucket of whitewash and a brush, and in a few minutes the crack is gone. The wall gleams. People stop and admire it. Then the sky goes dark over the hills to the north. Rain comes in sheets, then hail the size of fists, then a wind that tears at the roof beams. By morning the wall is a heap of mud in the street, and someone standing over it asks the painter a single question. Where is the coating with which you smeared it?

What are you painting over?

This is Old Paths. Today is day four of the eleventh month in the reading plan, day two hundred fifty-four of three hundred. The readings are Ezekiel chapters thirteen through fifteen, Job chapter four, John chapter eight, verses twelve through twenty, and Second Peter chapter two, verses ten through sixteen.

Last time, Ezekiel twelve ended with two proverbs people were repeating in Jerusalem. The days grow long, and every vision comes to nothing. And, he prophesies of times far off. God answered both, and in the middle of that answer, chapter twelve, verse twenty-four, he made a promise: there shall be no more any false vision or flattering divination within the house of Israel. Today he keeps that promise. Chapter thirteen goes after the flatterers by name.

Something else happens today that the reading plan arranged. Yesterday we watched Job curse the day he was born. Today, in Ezekiel fourteen, God himself names Job, alongside Noah and Daniel, as one of the most righteous men who ever lived. And on the very same morning, over in the book of Job, Job's friend Eliphaz opens his mouth for the first time and suggests that Job is not righteous at all. Keep both verdicts in your head. We will put them side by side at the end.

In John, the Feast of Booths has just ended, and in the court where the great lamps burned all week, Jesus says, I am the light of the world. And in Second Peter, a donkey preaches.

Ezekiel chapters thirteen through fifteen.

We are still in the year that began at chapter eight, verse one, the sixth year of King Jehoiachin's exile. That is late summer of 592 B.C. The next date in the book does not come until chapter twenty, a year later. So everything from chapter twelve to chapter nineteen belongs to that one tense year. Zedekiah is on the throne in Jerusalem, quietly flirting with rebellion against Babylon. Ezekiel is in Tel-abib by the Chebar canal, still mute except when God opens his mouth. The glory of the Lord, last time we saw it, was standing on the Mount of Olives, looking back at a city it had left.

And everywhere, in Jerusalem and among the exiles, prophets were saying it would all be fine. We know some of their names from Jeremiah: Hananiah in the temple, promising Babylon would fall within two years, and in Babylon itself Ahab son of Kolaiah and Shemaiah, promising a quick trip home. Ezekiel's people were hearing two kinds of preaching, and one was much more pleasant.

These three chapters answer three false securities. Chapter thirteen: our prophets say peace. Chapter fourteen: we have godly people among us, and we are consulting the real prophet. Chapter fifteen: we are God's vine. Each one is knocked down.

Chapter thirteen opens with the diagnosis. They prophesy, verse two, from their own hearts. They follow their own spirit, and have seen nothing. The message starts inside the man instead of coming down to him.

Then verse four. Your prophets have been like jackals among ruins, O Israel. Picture a jackal in a ruined town. It is not rebuilding anything. It is living off the wreckage. And verse five: You have not gone up into the breaches, or built up a wall for the house of Israel, that it might stand in battle in the day of the Lord. When an enemy breaks a hole in a city wall, a real soldier runs toward the hole. These men ran away from it. Matthew Poole, the Puritan, says that, fox-like, they shifted out of harm's way.

What does that mean for a prophet? Calvin restricts it to teaching, calling the people to repentance in time. Matthew Henry adds prayer, and says these men were not praying prophets. Henry is right to keep both, because Psalm one hundred six says Moses stood in the breach before God to turn away his wrath. A true prophet stands in the gap before the people with the truth and before God with intercession. These men did neither.

Now the central image, verses ten through sixteen. Precisely because they have misled my people, saying, Peace, when there is no peace, and because, when the people build a wall, these prophets smear it with whitewash, say to those who smear it with whitewash that it shall fall!

Two Hebrew words are worth your time here. The wall in verse ten is a chayits, a word that appears only here in the whole Old Testament. It means a thin partition wall, the kind you would put up inside a house, not a city rampart. The people are building a flimsy thing and calling it a defense. And the whitewash is tafel. It is an interesting word, because elsewhere tafel means tasteless. In Job six, verse six, Job asks, can that which is tasteless be eaten without salt? Tafel. In Lamentations two, verse fourteen, the visions of the false prophets are called false and deceptive, and that word is tafel too. A coating with no substance. A message with no salt.

Calvin thought the stuff was sand with no lime, so it never sets. Jerome thought it was mortar without chaff. Spurgeon, preaching on this text in May of 1868, said it was mud never mixed with straw. They agree on the point. It looks like a repair. It is not one.

Notice who does what. The people build the wall, their alliance with Egypt, their confidence in the temple. The prophets paint it and make it look holy.

You know what this looks like. A seller has a crack in the basement wall skim-coated with fresh plaster the week before the showing. Everything looks fine. The crack has not gone anywhere.

Then the storm of verse eleven, rain, great hailstones, and a stormy wind, which is the Babylonian army. And verse twelve stings: when the wall falls, will it not be said to you, Where is the coating with which you smeared it?

Calvin puts his finger on what kind of peace the false prophets were selling. God really does offer peace, he says, but on a condition. "This, then, is one way of being at peace with God by becoming enemies to ourselves, and fighting earnestly against the depraved and vicious desires of the flesh. But how do false prophets preach peace? Why! so that miserable and abandoned men may sleep in the midst of their sins."

There is the difference. True peace with God means war with your sin. False peace means a truce with your sin.

And Spurgeon, in that 1868 sermon, explains why the painters never run short of work. "Sing men a soft song of peace in sin and safety out of Christ, and they will cry your name up to the skies! You shall have a ready market, for every man will be a buyer."

John Trapp, the Puritan, turns the text on his own profession. He complains of ministers who "shoot off at best, a few popguns against gross sins; or when they have done their worst at it, lick them whole again with, I hope better things of you, or, I hope there are none such here". You have heard that sermon. It names a sin, and then it apologizes.

Here is my first New Testament move, and Henry and Trapp both warrant it, because they hear Jesus picking up this picture at the end of the Sermon on the Mount: the rain fell, the floods came, the winds blew and beat on that house, and great was the fall of it. Same storm. Same question. What is underneath?

The second half of chapter thirteen turns to women who prophesy, and it is one of the strangest passages in the book. Verse eighteen: Woe to the women who sew magic bands upon all wrists, and make veils for the heads of persons of every stature, in the hunt for souls. Nobody is sure what the bands and veils were. The old translations said pillows and kerchiefs, which Keil calls a guess. Calvin compares the veils to the Roman augurs, who covered their heads to read omens. The purpose is plain. They were props for spiritual theater, sold, verse nineteen, for handfuls of barley and pieces of bread.

Gregory the Great, the bishop of Rome around the year 590, wrote a handbook for pastors and used the old pillow translation as a picture of flattery. To put a cushion under every elbow, he said, is what happens "when the hardness of reproof is withdrawn from one who sins, and when the softness of favour is offered to him, that he may lie softly in error, while no roughness of contradiction troubles him."

Lie softly in error. That is the business these women were in.

And verse twenty-two gives the result. Because you have disheartened the righteous falsely, although I have not grieved him, and you have encouraged the wicked. False religion always does both at once. It loads fake guilt on tender consciences, and it hands real comfort to hard ones. It frightens the wrong people and soothes the wrong people.

Chapter fourteen. Certain of the elders of Israel came to me and sat before me. Same posture as chapter eight, verse one. Respectable men, seated in the prophet's house, waiting for a word from God. And before they say anything, God speaks to Ezekiel. Son of man, these men have taken their idols into their hearts. The Hebrew says, literally, they have made their idols go up upon their heart. Ezekiel's favorite word for idols is gillulim, a word that probably sounds deliberately like a word for lumps of dung. These are not statues in their living rooms. They have carried them in, inside. And the question in verse three is withering. Should I indeed let myself be consulted by them?

Matthew Henry says it so well that I will just read it. "Many who have no idols in their sanctuary have idols in their hearts, which is no less a usurpation of God's throne and a profanation of his name."

And verse four: I the Lord will answer him as he comes with the multitude of his idols. They want guidance while keeping the idol, so God answers according to the idol. Humble worshipers, Henry says, God answers according to the multitude of his mercies.

Then verse nine, and here is the hardest question in today's reading. And if the prophet is deceived and speaks a word, I, the Lord, have deceived that prophet, and I will stretch out my hand against him and will destroy him.

How can God deceive anyone? Doesn't Scripture say God cannot lie?

Some of the church fathers softened it to mere permission. Calvin refused that way out. He points to First Kings twenty-two, where Micaiah sees the Lord asking, who will entice Ahab? A spirit offers to be a lying spirit in the mouth of Ahab's prophets, and the Lord sends him. The Hebrew verb here, pittah, to entice or persuade, is the same verb. Keil agrees with Calvin, with a guard: it does not destroy human freedom, and it falls only on people who have already let evil in.

Read it in context. These elders want a prophet who will tell them what they want to hear, and God's judgment is to let them find one. Calvin says the prophet is deceived because the people's wickedness deserves it. Henry gives the cleanest answer I know. "Both are sins in him who does them, and so they are not from God; both are punishments to him to whom they are done, and so they are from God."

Our Confession, in chapter five, says the same thing more formally. God's providence reaches even to the sins of men and angels, and not by a bare permission. He bounds them, orders them, and governs them to his holy ends. And yet the sinfulness comes only from the creature, never from God, who is not and cannot be the author of sin. And verse eleven gives his purpose even here: that the house of Israel may no more go astray from me, but that they may be my people and I may be their God.

Then verses twelve through twenty-three, and the famous names. God imagines a land that sins, and four judgments, famine, wild beasts, sword, and pestilence, straight out of Leviticus twenty-six. And four times he says it: even if these three men, Noah, Daniel, and Job, were in it, they would deliver but their own lives by their righteousness.

Why Daniel? Noah and Job lived long before, but Daniel was alive. Carried to Babylon thirteen years earlier as a teenager, he was now a high official at Nebuchadnezzar's court. Spurgeon guessed he was about thirty, sandwiched between Noah and Job. Some scholars think Ezekiel means an ancient Canaanite hero named Danel, but there is no need. W. A. Criswell, the Baptist preacher, saw the point: nobody from the distant past held as large a place in the exiles' hopes as their own Daniel. We have a man in the palace, they thought. God says, it will not help.

Keil sees a rising ladder, as Jerome did long before him. Noah saved his family. Daniel saved his friends. Job could not even save his children.

And by their righteousness does not mean they were sinless. Augustine notes that Noah was once drunk, and nobody should conclude from Ezekiel that drunkenness is no sin. Their righteousness was the life of men who trusted God and walked with him. It saved themselves. It could not be spread over anyone else.

Spurgeon preached on verse twenty in 1882, for the children of godly homes. "No proxy in these matters is possible." He still told them they were right to ask Christians to pray for them. Your mother's prayers are a gift. They are not a substitute for your own repentance.

Then verse twenty-two. Survivors will come to Babylon, and when you see their ways, you will be consoled. How? Henry thinks they arrive repentant. Calvin and Keil say they arrive so plainly wicked that the exiles stop complaining God was too hard. I side with Calvin, because verse twenty-three says, you shall know that I have not done without cause all that I have done.

Chapter fifteen is short and devastating. How does the wood of the vine surpass any wood? A vine is good for one thing, grapes. You cannot even carve a peg from it to hang a pot on. And this vine is already charred. Calvin reads the burned ends as the northern kingdom and the towns of Judah, with Jerusalem the blackened middle. Useless whole, how much less now?

Israel loved to call itself God's vine, from Psalm eighty and Isaiah five. Ezekiel says a vine is only precious for its fruit. My second New Testament step is Jesus' own, and Calvin makes the link: I am the true vine; if anyone does not abide in me he is thrown away like a branch, and the branches are thrown into the fire. We have no sap of our own.

So here is what I want you to carry from Ezekiel today, and it comes from chapter fourteen, verses three and four. Those elders came for guidance with their minds already made up. The thing they loved was already enthroned inside, and they wanted God's blessing on it. You and I do this. We pray about a decision we have already made. We ask for counsel and quietly plan to ignore any answer but one. Before you ask God for direction this week, ask yourself a harder question first. Is there an answer I have already decided not to accept? Deal with that idol, and then ask.

Job chapter four.

Seven days of silence, then Job's curse in chapter three. Now Eliphaz the Temanite answers. Teman was in Edom, and it was famous for wise men. Jeremiah forty-nine asks, is wisdom no more in Teman? Eliphaz is probably the eldest of the three, and his first speech is the gentlest, which makes it more dangerous.

It comes in three movements. Verses two through six turn Job's own past against him. Verses seven through eleven state a law of the harvest. Verses twelve through twenty-one tell of a vision in the night.

He starts politely. If one ventures a word with you, will you be impatient? Then verse three: Behold, you have instructed many, and you have strengthened the weak hands. Your words have upheld him who was stumbling. That is real praise. Gregory the Great noticed something lovely in it: the best evidence of Job's character comes from the mouth of his critic. Gregory says, "the testimony to our manner of life is never so strong, as when commendable things are told by him, who aims to fasten guilt upon our head."

Then verse five. But now it has come to you, and you are impatient; it touches you, and you are dismayed. Matthew Henry caught the word. It touches you. That is Satan's word from chapter one and chapter two: stretch out your hand and touch all that he has. Eliphaz does not know what happened in heaven, but he uses the devil's verb, and he uses it to make Job's suffering small. It only touched you.

Notice too the little word but. John Trapp says, "To commend a man with a but is a wound instead of a commendation". He compares it to Naaman, a great man, a mighty man of valor, but a leper. You know that kind of compliment. It is a slap with a glove on.

Verse seven is the heart of Eliphaz's theology. Remember: who that was innocent ever perished? Or where were the upright cut off? As I have seen, those who plow iniquity and sow trouble reap the same. Then he brings in lions, and Henry points out that Hebrew has five different words for lion and Eliphaz uses all five. Even the strongest predator starves when God turns on it. Job has just lost seven sons and three daughters in a windstorm, and Eliphaz is talking about wicked lions whose cubs are scattered. He may not intend the cruelty. It lands anyway.

Is verse seven true? Gregory answers that in this life the innocent often do perish, as Isaiah says, the righteous man perishes and no one lays it to heart, and yet they are kept for eternal glory. Henry asks, did not righteous Abel perish being innocent? Eliphaz has taken a true principle, that God judges sin, and made it a rule you can run backwards: great suffering means great sin. That rule is the error of the whole book.

Then the vision. A word was brought to me stealthily. Dread came upon me, and trembling. A spirit glided past my face; the hair of my flesh stood up. It is genuinely frightening writing. And the voice says, Can mortal man be in the right before God? Can a man be pure before his Maker?

So here is the hard question. Was this vision from God? Trapp lists the old views. Some thought it was Eliphaz's imagination. Some thought it was an illusion from Satan. The Reformation scholar Mercer held it was a true vision from God which Eliphaz then misapplied. Henry thinks it was a good angel, and says it would have served to rebuke Job's murmuring but never to condemn him as a hypocrite.

I land with Mercer and Henry. The content is true. Job himself will ask almost the same question in chapter nine: how can a man be in the right before God? But Eliphaz uses a true sentence to prove a false conclusion, and in chapter forty-two God will say plainly that Eliphaz and his friends have not spoken of him what is right. Derek Thomas, teaching this chapter for Ligonier, calls Eliphaz a miserable comforter and faults his misuse of the principle that what you sow you reap.

Here is the practical wisdom. Most bad counsel is not made of lies. It is made of true things applied to the wrong person at the wrong time. When you sit with a suffering friend, you may be right about God and still be wrong about your friend. Leave the but at home.

John chapter eight, verses twelve through twenty.

Again Jesus spoke to them, saying, I am the light of the world. Whoever follows me will not walk in darkness, but will have the light of life.

Where is he standing? Verse twenty tells us. These words he spoke in the treasury, as he taught in the temple. John Gill explains that the treasury was where the thirteen offering chests stood. R. C. Sproul adds that they were shaped like the shofar, the ram's horn. Those chests stood in the Court of the Women. And according to the Mishnah's description of the Feast of Booths, which we looked at a few episodes ago, that was the very court where enormous golden lampstands were lit every night of the feast, so bright that there was no courtyard in Jerusalem that did not shine with their light. The feast has just ended. The lamps are dark. And in the same court, Jesus says, I am the light. Remember Isaiah nine, which came up two episodes ago: the people who walked in darkness have seen a great light, in Galilee of the nations. The man from Galilee is standing in the temple claiming it.

John Chrysostom reads the claim this way: "I am the light of the world. Not of Galilee, not of Palestine, nor of Judæa." Augustine, preaching on this verse, had to warn his people against the Manichees, who thought Christ was literally the sun. Augustine's answer is that Christ is not the sun that was made but the one by whom the sun was made.

Calvin presses the exclusive claim. The light is peculiarly Christ's own, he says, and "out of Christ there is not even a spark of true light". Then he adds a sentence worth remembering. "There may be some appearance of brightness, but it resembles lightning, which only dazzles the eyes." Lightning is very bright. You cannot walk by it.

The Pharisees object in verse thirteen. You are bearing witness about yourself; your testimony is not true. The law said a matter is established by two or three witnesses. Deuteronomy nineteen, verse fifteen. A man vouching for himself proves nothing.

And here is the puzzle. Back in chapter five, verse thirty-one, Jesus himself said, if I alone bear witness about myself, my testimony is not true. Now he says, even if I do bear witness about myself, my testimony is true. Is that a contradiction? Calvin says that in chapter five Jesus was speaking by way of concession: on your terms, judging me as an ordinary man, set my word aside, and look at the other witnesses. Here he steps out of that ordinary category. I know where I came from and where I am going. An ordinary witness does not know his origin and his destiny. He does. And he still meets their demand. The law asks for two witnesses: I am one who bears witness about myself, and the Father who sent me bears witness about me.

They answer, Where is your Father? Sproul says they heard it like a boy on the playground: I'm going to get my dad. And Jesus says, you know neither me nor my Father.

Sproul, preaching this passage, was struck by the boldness of what they said to Jesus' face, and said this. "It terrifies me to think of anyone standing before Christ and saying, 'Your testimony is false.'" Every unbeliever, he went on, is saying exactly that. Augustine adds that the light needs no lamp to prove it is light. It shows itself, and it opens sound eyes. These men wanted lamps, other witnesses, because their eyes could not bear the day.

And then verse twenty again. No one arrested him, because his hour had not yet come. They were standing next to him, and they could not touch him. The light walks among people who hate it, and it goes out only when the Father says so.

Second Peter chapter two, verses ten through sixteen.

Verse nine ended with a promise: the Lord knows how to rescue the godly, and to keep the unrighteous under punishment until the day of judgment. Verse ten continues the sentence: and especially those who indulge in the lust of defiling passion and despise authority. Two marks, then. They give the flesh whatever it wants, and they take orders from no one.

Then Peter piles up words. Bold and willful. Willful is authadeis, literally self-pleasing. Augustine, in the City of God, says pride is when the soul abandons God and becomes a kind of end to itself, and adds, "And therefore the holy Scriptures designate the proud by another name, self-pleasers." That is what a false teacher is at bottom. His own satisfaction is his final authority.

They do not tremble, verse ten, as they blaspheme the glorious ones. Who are the glorious ones? Calvin was sure Peter meant civil magistrates, rulers whose office comes from God. Most interpreters today, reading Peter alongside Jude, think he means angelic powers, and probably fallen ones. Jude tells us that even Michael the archangel, contending with the devil, did not dare pronounce a blasphemous judgment but said, the Lord rebuke you. That fits verse eleven exactly: angels, though greater in might, do not pronounce a blasphemous judgment against them before the Lord. I lean that way. These teachers mocked spiritual realities they did not understand, the way people still laugh at the idea of a devil. But Calvin's point stands either way. People who mock what is above them are no wiser than the beasts.

And that is verse twelve. Like irrational animals, creatures of instinct, born to be caught and destroyed. An animal walks into the trap because it smells the bait. That is all it knows. And notice whom these men catch, verse fourteen: unsteady souls. Calvin says the remedy is simple. If your faith has roots, the bait does not work on you. Unstable people are not the victims of bad teachers by accident. They are the target market.

Verse thirteen: they are blots and blemishes. In Greek, spiloi kai momoi. Hold that phrase up against Peter's first letter, chapter one, verse nineteen, where he says you were ransomed with the precious blood of Christ, like that of a lamb without blemish or spot. Same two words. The church is gathered around a spotless Lamb, and these men are the spots at the table. Some manuscripts say they revel in your love feasts. Either way, they are eating with you.

Verse fourteen. They have eyes full of adultery, insatiable for sin. And that word entice is deleazo, a fishing word, to catch with bait. Remember who is writing. Peter knew exactly how a lure works. It looks like food, and there is a hook inside. And they have hearts trained in greed. Trained is the word we get gymnasium from. They have worked out at greed until they are in excellent shape.

Then Balaam, the son of Beor, who loved gain from wrongdoing. The king of Moab hired him to curse Israel. God would not let him, so he found another way, and Numbers thirty-one says he counseled Moab to seduce Israel into sin.

Was Balaam a true prophet? Matthew Henry gives the best answer. "he was among the prophets of the Lord as Judas among the apostles of Jesus Christ". He had real access to real words from God, and he loved money more.

And so verse sixteen. He was rebuked for his own transgression; a speechless donkey spoke with human voice and restrained the prophet's madness. Calvin noticed the humiliation built into the story. The angel showed himself to the donkey before he showed himself to the prophet. The great seer was the last one on the road to see what God was doing. When you love money, you go blind in a very particular way. Your donkey can see more than you can.

Two connections between today's readings, and both are really there in the text.

The first is the price of a prophet. In Ezekiel thirteen, the women sold oracles for handfuls of barley and pieces of bread. In Second Peter two, Balaam loved the wages of wrongdoing. One sold cheap and one sold dear, but both sold a word from God. And notice what was being bought. Nobody pays for bad news. The market Spurgeon described is the market for peace when there is no peace. The way to stay free of that market is not to become cynical about every preacher. It is to stop being a customer for whitewash.

The second connection is the one I mentioned at the start. In Ezekiel fourteen, verse fourteen, God names Job as one of the three most righteous men he can think of. In Job four, verse seven, Eliphaz looks at Job on his ash heap and asks, who that was innocent ever perished? Eliphaz judged by what he saw. God judged by what he knew. And in John eight, Jesus says the same thing about his own accusers: you judge according to the flesh. The verdict that matters on Job, on you, and on Jesus himself, comes from the Father, and the Father has already spoken.

Here is your question for the day. Where have you asked someone to paint your wall? Where have you gone looking for the friend, or the book, or the preacher, who will tell you that the crack does not matter?
