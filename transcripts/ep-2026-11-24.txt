Daniel chapter nine, verse two. I, Daniel, perceived in the books the number of years that, according to the word of the Lord to Jeremiah the prophet, must pass before the end of the desolations of Jerusalem, namely, seventy years.

An old man, past eighty, a top official of the empire, sitting with a scroll of Jeremiah. Doing what you did this morning. And when he found a promise, it put him on his knees. What do you do when you find one?

This is Old Paths. Today is day twenty-four of the eleventh month in the reading plan, day two hundred seventy-four of three hundred. The readings are Daniel nine, Job twenty-one, verses twenty-two through thirty-four, John thirteen, verses eighteen through thirty, and Jude, verses eight through sixteen.

The plan stepped forward a day to match the calendar, so yesterday's readings had no episode, and Jude is new to the show today.

Last time we left Daniel at his upper window, open toward Jerusalem, praying three times a day, as he had done previously. Today we hear what he said at that window. And the answer to his prayer is one of the most argued-over paragraphs in the Bible.

Daniel nine.

First, where we are. Verse one dates it to the first year of Darius the Mede. Babylon has just fallen, so this is 539 or 538 B.C. Chapters seven and eight, which you read yesterday, gave Daniel visions of beasts and horns, and one like a son of man receiving the kingdom. Chapter eight ended with Daniel sick and appalled, saying he did not understand it. And the angel who tried to explain it was Gabriel. Keep that name.

Matthew Henry thinks this chapter probably comes before the lions' den. If so, this is what those open windows were for.

So what is Daniel doing in verse two? He is reading Jeremiah. Jeremiah twenty-five and twenty-nine said Babylon would rule for seventy years and then God would bring his people home. Daniel was carried off in 605 B.C. He can count. The seventy years are nearly up.

Henry points out that Daniel was a prophet himself and the prime minister of an empire, and still he made time to study another prophet's book. Then he says, The greatest and best men in the world must not think themselves above their Bibles.

And notice what the promise does to him. Verse three. He turns his face to the Lord God with prayer, fasting, sackcloth, and ashes. Henry again: God's promises are meant not to replace our prayers but to stir them up. A promise is not a reason to stop asking. It is the reason you dare to ask.

Now the prayer itself, verses four to nineteen. It has two movements. Verses four through fourteen are confession. Verses fifteen through nineteen are plea.

Listen to the pronouns in verse five. We have sinned and done wrong and acted wickedly and rebelled. Not they. We. Daniel is about the most blameless man in the Old Testament, and he puts himself in line with the kings who filled Jerusalem with idols.

Why pray this way? Because Leviticus twenty-six, verse forty, promised that if Israel in exile would confess their iniquity, God would remember his covenant. Daniel is praying the terms of the covenant back to God. Verse seven sets the two sides against each other. To you, O Lord, belongs righteousness, but to us open shame. The Hebrew is literally shame of face.

John Piper preached this chapter under the title How to Pray for a Desolate Church. He noticed that almost every phrase in it comes from earlier Scripture, from Leviticus, Deuteronomy, Exodus, the Psalms, Jeremiah. And he said this. What I have seen is that those whose prayers are most saturated with Scripture are generally most fervent and most effective in prayer. And where the mind isn't brimming with the Bible, the heart is not generally brimming with prayer.

Then the plea. Verse eighteen is the hinge of the whole chapter. For we do not present our pleas before you because of our righteousness, but because of your great mercy. And verse nineteen, the prayer speeds up, short cries, one after another. O Lord, hear. O Lord, forgive. O Lord, pay attention and act. Delay not, for your own sake.

Before he finishes, the answer comes. Verse twenty-one. Gabriel comes in swift flight at the time of the evening sacrifice. There has been no temple and no evening sacrifice for nearly fifty years. But Daniel still keeps the hour.

And Gabriel says, at the beginning of your pleas a word went out, for you are greatly loved. The Hebrew is hamudot. It means precious, treasured. Heaven calls this old exile a treasure.

Now the hard part. Verse twenty-four. Seventy weeks are decreed about your people and your holy city. The word is shabuim, sevens. Daniel was reading about seventy years. Gabriel answers with seventy sevens. Leviticus twenty-six warned that if Israel would not repent, the discipline would come seven times over. So the exile of seventy years is ending, but the full restoration is seventy sevens away.

What will they accomplish? Six things. To finish the transgression, to put an end to sin, to atone for iniquity, to bring in everlasting righteousness, to seal both vision and prophet, and to anoint a most holy place. Spurgeon preached on this verse in September 1882, and he read the first three as shut up, seal up, cover up. Spurgeon said, it is put into the dungeon and the door is shut upon it; after this the door is sealed and then it is covered up, so that the place of sin's sepulchre cannot be seen any more for ever.

But what are the seventy weeks, and when do they run? Calvin admits the difficulty. This passage has been variously treated, and so distracted, and almost torn to pieces by the various opinions of interpreters, that it might be considered nearly useless on account of its obscurity. But no prediction is in vain, he says, so we keep reading.

Calvin and Henry read the weeks as one continuous run of time. Seven weeks to rebuild Jerusalem, sixty-two more to the anointed one, and the last week belongs to Christ, who confirms the covenant with many and makes the sacrifices obsolete. Henry thinks it the plainest prophecy of the Messiah anywhere in the Old Testament.

But Hippolytus, writing on Daniel around the year 200, already does something different. He runs the sixty-two weeks to Christ, and then he says, when the threescore and two weeks are fulfilled, and Christ has come, and the Gospel is preached in every place, the times being then accomplished, there will remain only one week, the last. He puts that last week at the end of the age, under the Antichrist. That is the ancestor of today's popular view, with a gap between the sixty-ninth and seventieth weeks. Martyn Lloyd-Jones, discussing that view, urged humility all around.

And the critics read it all as Antiochus Epiphanes in the 160s B.C. The Cambridge Bible, which favors that view, admits its own numbers come out sixty-seven years too long.

Where do I land? Look at verse twenty-six. After the sixty-two weeks, an anointed one shall be cut off and shall have nothing. Then the people of the prince who is to come shall destroy the city and the sanctuary. An anointed one is killed, and after that the city and the temple fall. I know of only one history that fits that order. The Messiah cut off outside Jerusalem around A.D. 30, and the Romans burning the temple in A.D. 70. Jesus himself sent his disciples to this chapter when he spoke of the abomination of desolation standing in the holy place. So I read it with Calvin, and I hold the arithmetic loosely. Spurgeon, in that same sermon, refused to fix the dates. He told his people they would be better fed on the Lord himself than on times and seasons.

So here is the application, and it comes from verse two, not verse twenty-seven. Daniel read a promise, and it drove him to confession and to prayer. Do you read that way? Tomorrow, when a promise jumps off the page, do not just underline it. Pray it back to God, in the first person plural, for his name's sake.

Job twenty-one, verses twenty-two through thirty-four.

This finishes Job's answer to Zophar and the second round of speeches. In yesterday's half Job asked, why do the wicked live, reach old age, and grow mighty? Now he presses the point.

Verse twenty-two. Will any teach God knowledge? Job is not attacking God. He is saying that his friends have built a theory of how God must run the world and are now lecturing God with it.

Then two deaths. One man dies in his full vigor, wholly at ease. The Hebrew is striking. He dies in the very bone of his completeness. The word for completeness comes from the same root as blameless, the word used for Job in chapter one. Another man dies in bitterness of soul, never having tasted prosperity. And verse twenty-six. They lie down alike in the dust, and the worms cover them. You cannot read a man's heart from his death.

Then Job appeals to the travelers. Have you not asked those who travel the roads? Gregory the Great loved this line, and he turned the traveler into a picture of the believer. He wrote, he is called a wayfarer, who minds that the present life is to him a way and not a native land.

Here is the puzzle in verse thirty. The King James says the wicked is reserved to the day of destruction. The English Standard Version says he is spared in the day of calamity. Barnes preferred reserved, and Keil preferred spared. In context, Job means the wicked man escapes now and is buried with honors, a guard over his tomb. The friends said judgment always comes quickly. The travelers have not seen it.

And verse thirty-four. How then will you comfort me with empty nothings? The word is hebel, the vapor of Ecclesiastes. Barnes draws the lesson. Real comfort has to be built on what is true about God. Anything else is vapor.

John thirteen, verses eighteen through thirty.

We left Jesus with a towel, washing feet, Judas's included. In verse eighteen he quotes Psalm forty-one. He who ate my bread has lifted his heel against me. R. C. Sproul notes that the whole psalm is not about Jesus, since David confesses his own sin in it. Jesus takes the one line about the friend at the table. And he says it beforehand, so that when it happens, you may believe that I am he. That is the divine name again, I am.

Verse twenty-one. Jesus was troubled in his spirit. One of you will betray me. They look at each other. Augustine said Judas was one of them in number, not in merit.

They reclined at table on the left elbow. John, the disciple whom Jesus loved, is close enough to lean back and whisper, Lord, who is it? Jesus says, the one I give this morsel to. Passing a dipped morsel was an honor a host gave a favored guest. Sproul thinks Judas was in the place of honor that night, close enough for Jesus to hand it to him. Chrysostom asked who would not be won over by bread from that hand. And Judas was not.

After the morsel, Satan entered into him. Calvin says the bread was the occasion, not the cause. Judas had been opening that door since verse two. Then Jesus says, what you are going to do, do quickly. The others assume Judas, who kept the moneybag, is off to buy for the feast or to give to the poor. Nobody suspects him, even now. Augustine draws the line clearly. Judas handed Christ over for money. Christ handed himself over for us.

And verse thirty. He immediately went out. And it was night. Augustine adds one sentence. And he that went out was himself the night.

Jude, verses eight through sixteen.

Jude is new to the show today, so a compact introduction. The author calls himself a servant of Jesus Christ and brother of James. That makes him a half brother of the Lord, though he never claims the title. His name in Greek is Judas, the same as the man who just walked out into the night. He wrote probably in the 60s A.D., to believers steeped in the Old Testament and Jewish tradition. He meant to write about our common salvation, but had to write instead, contend for the faith once for all delivered to the saints, because ungodly people had crept in.

Yesterday's verses gave three old examples of judgment, Israel in the wilderness, the angels, and Sodom. Today he keeps saying these people, these people. Verse nine. The archangel Michael, disputing with the devil over the body of Moses, would not pronounce a blasphemous judgment, but said, the Lord rebuke you. Calvin thinks Satan wanted the grave of Moses found to make it a shrine. Spurgeon draws a simpler lesson. Hard words are a sign of a weak cause.

Then Cain, Balaam, and Korah, and a string of pictures. Hidden reefs at your love feasts. The Greek word is spilades, rocks under the water that tear out the hull. Waterless clouds, fruitless trees, wild waves, wandering stars.

Now the hard question. In verses fourteen and fifteen Jude quotes Enoch, the seventh from Adam. That line matches a Jewish book called First Enoch, written between the Testaments. Does quoting it make the book Scripture? The fathers split. Tertullian accepted Enoch as genuine Scripture. Jerome reported the trouble plainly. Of Jude's letter he wrote, because in it he quotes from the apocryphal Book of Enoch it is rejected by many. Nevertheless by age and use it has gained authority and is reckoned among the Holy Scriptures. Augustine said Enoch spoke some divine words, because Jude says so, but the book under his name is not canonical. Calvin guessed Jude had an unwritten tradition. My answer is Paul in Athens, quoting a Greek poet. Quoting a true sentence does not make the book it came from Scripture. Enoch's sentence is true. The Lord comes.

Here is the connection. Judas ate bread at Jesus' own table, in the place of honor, and went out into the night. Jude, who has the same name, warns about hidden reefs at the love feast, people who eat with you and are not of you. Spurgeon preached on Jude's verse in 1868 and went straight to the upper room. Our Lord had twelve, and one of them was a devil. And in all three readings, judgment waits but is fixed. The wicked man in Job is spared for now. The desolator in Daniel has a decreed end. And Enoch says, behold, the Lord comes.

So carry this question through the day. When you sit at the Lord's table, do you come like Daniel, saying we have sinned, or like Judas, with your mind made up?
