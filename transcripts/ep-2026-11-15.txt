Ezekiel forty-three, verse two. And behold, the glory of the God of Israel was coming from the east. And the sound of his coming was like the sound of many waters, and the earth shone with his glory.

Nineteen years earlier, Ezekiel watched that same glory lift off the temple, move to the east gate, and leave. Today he watches it come home.

This is Old Paths. Today is day seventeen of the eleventh month in the reading plan, day two hundred sixty-seven of three hundred. The readings are Ezekiel chapters forty-two through forty-four, Job chapter fifteen, verses seventeen through thirty-five, John chapter eleven, verses forty-five through fifty-seven, and First John chapter four, verses one through six.

Last time we walked through a beautiful, measured house with no one living in it. I left you with a line Patrick Fairbairn quoted, that the inner room stands empty, waiting for the entrance of the Lord. Today the waiting ends. And in John, where we left Lazarus walking out of his tomb, today the rulers of the temple meet to decide what to do about the man who called him out.

Ezekiel forty-two through forty-four.

We are still inside the vision dated in chapter forty, about 573 B.C. Nebuchadnezzar rules Babylon, and Jerusalem has been rubble for fourteen years.

Chapter forty-two finishes the tour with the priests' holy chambers, then measures the whole compound from outside, five hundred on every side, a perfect square. Verse twenty tells you why the wall exists. To make a separation between the holy and the common. Remember that phrase. The rest of today's reading is about who may come near, and how.

Then chapter forty-three. The guide brings Ezekiel back to the east gate, and the glory comes. Not from the west, not from the north, but from the east. Keil points out that this is the exact road it left by. In chapter ten the glory went to the east gate, and in chapter eleven it rose and stood on the mountain east of the city. Now it comes back the same way, as if retracing its own steps.

Verse three says this vision was just like the one when he came to destroy the city, and the one by the Chebar canal. Same God, same glory. The one who left in judgment returns in mercy. And Ezekiel does what he always does. He falls on his face.

The Hebrew word for glory is kavod, and its root idea is weight. The glory of God is the sheer heaviness of who he is. In verse five, that weight fills the house.

Theodoret, the fifth century bishop of Cyrus, is quoted by Keil on this passage. The Lord appears, and fills the house with His own glory; showing that the house will not only be built, but will be filled with the power of God. A building is not a temple because of its stones. Two chapters of measurements gave you a house. Verse five gives you a dwelling. Matthew Henry made the same point by reaching back to chapter thirty-seven. Without the glory, he says, the temple is like those dead bodies in the valley, with no breath until the Spirit of life entered them.

Then a voice speaks from inside the house, verse seven. Son of man, this is the place of my throne and the place of the soles of my feet, where I will dwell in the midst of the people of Israel forever.

Ellicott notices the one word that sets this house apart. Forever. The tabernacle passed away. Solomon's temple burned. This house, God says, he will never leave.

And God names what drove him out. Verse eight. They set their threshold by my threshold, and their doorposts beside my doorposts, with only a wall between me and them. The royal palace stood right against the temple, and the kings filled God's courts with their idols. Henry's comment stings. Too often, he says, the people nearest the church are furthest from God. You can live next door to the house of God for years and never know him.

But listen to how the promise is worded. The house of Israel shall no more defile my holy name. That is not a command. It is a future tense, a promise. Henry caught it. This is pure gospel. The precept of the law says, You must not defile my name: the grace of the gospel says, You shall not. That is the new heart of chapter thirty-six, now walking into the temple courts.

Then verse twelve, a sentence framed on both ends. This is the law of the house. The word is torah. And the law is this: the whole territory on the top of the mountain all around shall be most holy. In Solomon's temple, one small room was the Most Holy Place. In this vision the whole mountain is. The holiness that used to be locked in one room now covers everything inside the wall.

The rest of chapter forty-three describes the altar and a seven-day consecration, ending with the words, I will accept you. The sacrifices belong to the same picture language we discussed last time. Henry's concise commentary says it plainly. We offer no atoning sacrifices now, because by one offering Christ has perfected forever those who are being sanctified. He is quoting Hebrews ten. That is the one New Testament move I will make in this segment. What the vision shows in the shapes Ezekiel knew, the cross accomplished.

Now chapter forty-four, and the hardest question today. Ezekiel is brought back to the outer east gate, and it is shut. Verse two. This gate shall remain shut; it shall not be opened, and no one shall enter by it, for the Lord, the God of Israel, has entered by it.

What does the shut gate mean? The church has given a lot of answers. Ambrose of Milan, or a writer under his name, said the gate is Mary, closed because she remained a virgin. Jerome reports that reading too, but in his own commentary on Ezekiel he went another way and made the gate the Scriptures, sealed until Christ opened them. Matthew Henry's concise commentary says Christ alone entered heaven by his own holiness, and that way is shut to everyone else, who must enter as sinners by faith in his blood. John Gill surveys all of these and admits that the Marian reading was held by many Protestant writers as well as Roman Catholics.

So where do I land? On the reason the verse itself gives. Because the Lord, the God of Israel, has entered by it. Ellicott, Poole, and the Pulpit Commentary all read it this way. The gate is closed because of who walked through it. It is like a family that keeps the chair where the father used to sit. Nobody else sits there, and the empty chair keeps his memory. Henry says God keeps one way for himself alone. The shut gate is a permanent sermon. He came in here. Do not treat this ground as common.

Only the prince, the nasi, may sit in that gate to eat bread before the Lord. We will meet him properly next time.

Then the priests. The Levites who went astray after idols are demoted to keeping gates and slaughtering for the people. Only the sons of Zadok, who kept the charge of my sanctuary when the people of Israel went astray, will come near.

Why Zadok? Ellicott reminds us of First Kings chapter one. When Adonijah tried to seize David's throne, the high priest Abiathar went with him. Zadok stayed loyal. Solomon put Zadok in Abiathar's place. So a son of Zadok means a priest who stayed faithful when the leadership around him did not. Fairbairn sees the pairing. The promise, therefore, of a priesthood of the house of Zadok, he writes, entirely corresponded to the promise of a shepherd with the name of David. A faithful king and faithful priests, both named for the men who were loyal.

Henry sees mercy even in the demotion. The Levites are not thrown out; they still serve at the gates. And verse twenty-eight says of the priests, I am their inheritance. No land. Just God.

Here is the application, and it comes from verse twelve. Spurgeon preached on the law of the house in September 1881, and he drew this out of the whole mountain being most holy. It is not merely her ministers that are to be most holy, but her common members; not her sacraments only, but her ordinary meals; not her Sabbaths only, but her work-days; not her worship only, but her daily labour.

So think about tomorrow morning. Your desk, your truck, your kitchen table. That is inside the wall now. Is it holy?

Job chapter fifteen, verses seventeen through thirty-five.

Eliphaz finishes his second speech. Last time he called Job a windbag. Now he lectures from what he has seen and from the pure, old tradition of the fathers.

The lecture is a portrait of the wicked man. First, his inner life. Verse twenty. He writhes in pain all his days. Verse twenty-one. Dreadful sounds are in his ears. Then the cause, verses twenty-five and twenty-six. He stretched out his hand against God, running stubbornly against him with a thickly bossed shield. Then the end. Verse thirty-one. Let him not trust in emptiness, deceiving himself, for emptiness will be his payment. The word is shav, emptiness, used twice. He trusted nothing and gets paid in nothing. He is a vine that drops its grapes unripe, an olive tree that sheds its blossoms.

Is Eliphaz right? Partly. Gregory the Great, in his Morals on Job, says the inner terror is real. A man who plots against others is always afraid someone is plotting against him. On verse twenty-four, Gregory says that the path of truth is smooth and the road of falsehood is hard going. Lying is exhausting. That part is true.

But Eliphaz says the outward ruin comes always and soon, and in chapter twenty-one Job will answer that the wicked often grow old and rich. Psalm seventy-three agrees with Job. And look at what Eliphaz is really doing. Matthew Henry, in his concise commentary, names the logic. Eliphaz maintains that the wicked are certainly miserable: whence he would infer, that the miserable are certainly wicked, and therefore Job was so.

That is the trap. A true description of sin, turned backward into a diagnosis of suffering. When you see a man in ruins, do not run Eliphaz's argument in reverse.

John chapter eleven, verses forty-five through fifty-seven.

Lazarus is alive, and the crowd splits. Many believe. Some report to the Pharisees.

The chief priests and Pharisees call the council, and their worry is not whether the signs are real. They admit he performs many signs. Their worry is verse forty-eight. The Romans will come and take away both our place and our nation. A Ligonier devotional notes that our place probably means chiefly the temple. Hold onto that.

Then Caiaphas, high priest that year, speaks. You know nothing at all. It is better for you that one man should die for the people, not that the whole nation should perish. Cold political arithmetic.

And John says, he did not say this of his own accord, but being high priest that year he prophesied. How can a man plotting murder speak prophecy? Chrysostom answered it well. He prophesied, not knowing what he said; and the grace merely made use of his mouth, but touched not his accursed heart. Calvin said Caiaphas had two tongues that day. One spoke his own wicked plan. The other, which God was using, announced the gospel.

Then John adds what even Caiaphas never imagined. Not for the nation only, but also to gather into one the children of God who are scattered abroad. Gather and scattered. That is shepherd language, from Ezekiel thirty-four and from John ten, where the wolf scatters the flock and Jesus speaks of other sheep. Chrysostom saw the scale. The believer in Rome, he says, counts the believer in India a member of his own body.

And here is the irony. They killed Jesus to save their place. About forty years later Rome came anyway and the temple burned. Chrysostom says they did the very thing by which they hoped to escape, and did not escape.

Jesus withdraws to Ephraim near the wilderness, and pilgrims arriving for Passover ask each other in the temple courts, will he come to the feast?

First John chapter four, verses one through six.

Last time John gave us the letter's first plain mention of the Spirit. By this we know that he abides in us, by the Spirit whom he has given us. Now comes the warning that should follow. Beloved, do not believe every spirit, but test the spirits. The verb is dokimazo, the word for assaying metal, testing whether the coin is real.

Who does the testing? Calvin says John is speaking to the whole church and to every single believer. Not just pastors. You. And he gives an image. Gold is tried by fire or touchstone, but it can only be done by those who understand the art. The Word is the touchstone, but you need the Spirit to use it, and Calvin is sure God will not leave his people without that wisdom when they ask. The Westminster Confession says the same in chapter one: the supreme judge of every controversy is the Holy Spirit speaking in the Scripture.

The test itself, verse two. Every spirit that confesses that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh is from God. John's opponents said the divine Christ only seemed to have a body. A Ligonier devotional notes that John chose the incarnation because it was the doctrine his opponents denied.

Augustine pushed further. A man can say the words and deny them by how he lives, so tap the clay pot and listen whether it rings full or sounds cracked. John's test is first about doctrine, but Augustine is right that a life can deny what the mouth confesses.

Then verse four. Little children, you are from God and have overcome them, for he who is in you is greater than he who is in the world. Last time it was God greater than our heart. Now he is greater than the world. And Augustine warns us not to turn the victory into pride. Do not extol yourself; see who it is that in you has overcome.

Here is the thread that runs through today. Where does God dwell? Ezekiel sees the glory return to the place of my throne, to dwell in the midst of his people forever. In John, the high priest guards our place, the temple, by plotting to kill the man in whom that glory actually dwelt. And First John makes the dividing line simple. Do you confess that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh? God has come to live with his people, and he did it in a body.

Carry this question through the day. The whole mountain is holy now. What part of your ordinary day are you still treating as common ground?
