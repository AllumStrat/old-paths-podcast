Early in the last century, German archaeologists digging in the ruins of Babylon opened a vaulted storeroom near Nebuchadnezzar's palace. Inside were clay tablets, hundreds of them. They were not hymns or chronicles. They were ration lists. So much barley, so much oil, issued to the people the king was feeding.

And on a few of those tablets is a name. Yaukinu, king of the land of Yahudu. Jehoiachin, king of Judah. Oil for him, and oil for his five sons.

The book of Jeremiah ends with that man, eating a daily portion at the table of the king of Babylon. Fifty-two chapters of fire and tears, and the last scene is a meal.

Why would God end the book there?

The readings are Jeremiah fifty-two, the Song of Songs chapter five, John chapter six, verses forty-one through fifty-nine, and First Peter chapter three, verses thirteen through twenty-two.

Last time we watched Seraiah sink the scroll in the Euphrates, and the chapter closed with a line like a door shutting. Thus far are the words of Jeremiah. I told you that was an editor speaking, and that chapter fifty-two is an appendix. Today we read it, and we finish the book of Jeremiah.

Three threads come home today. First, the temple vessels. In chapter fifty-one God said he would make Bel, the god of Babylon, spit out what he had swallowed. Today you get the inventory, down to the pomegranates. Second, back in episode three I promised we would return to the almond branch of chapter one, shaqed, almond, and shoqed, watching. Today is the day. And third, the captive king set free. Is that a happy ending or a sad one? Good men have answered both ways.

The Song of Songs reaches its center, and then a knock in the night. In John, the Father draws, and Jesus says you must eat his flesh. And First Peter gives us the hardest verses in the letter.

Jeremiah fifty-two. Start with where it sits. Chapter fifty-one ended with the prophet's last words. This chapter follows the end of Second Kings almost word for word. Someone, perhaps Baruch, as Keil and Delitzsch suggest, set it here on purpose. Here is a fun fact. John Calvin lectured straight through Jeremiah, and when he got to fifty-two, he stopped. He said this chapter is not part of the prophecy but history, added because the prophecy could not be understood without it.

Matthew Henry says it in one line. "History is the best expositor of prophecy." For forty years Jeremiah said this would happen. Here is what happened.

And it is told with dates. Verse four. In the ninth year of Zedekiah, in the tenth month, on the tenth day, Nebuchadnezzar came against Jerusalem. That is January of 588 B.C. Verse six. On the ninth day of the fourth month the famine was so severe there was no food. That is July of 586. Eighteen months of siege. Then the wall was breached, and Zedekiah was caught, made to watch his sons die, and blinded. We walked through that in chapter thirty-nine. But notice one phrase Jeremiah adds that Second Kings does not have. Verse eleven. He put him in prison till the day of his death. Hold on to that phrase. It comes back.

Verse twelve. In the fifth month, on the tenth day of the month, Nebuzaradan entered Jerusalem and burned the house of the Lord. Second Kings says the seventh day. Matthew Poole thought Nebuzaradan arrived on the seventh and set the fire on the tenth. Keil and Delitzsch think a copyist confused two Hebrew numerals, and admit we cannot tell which is original. Either way, the Jewish people still keep a fast in that month. They call it Tisha B'Av.

Now here is the first of our threads. Go back to chapter one, verse three. The word of the Lord came to Jeremiah until the captivity of Jerusalem in the fifth month. The book opened by telling you where it would end. In the fifth month. And then in verses eleven and twelve God showed the young prophet an almond branch, shaqed, the tree that wakes first in spring, and said, You have seen well, for I am watching, shoqed, over my word to perform it.

Forty years later, in the fifth month, the word was performed. The almond was awake the whole time. And Calvin, on that vision, insisted that God's watching covers not only his threats but his promises. Keep that in your pocket.

Verses seventeen through twenty-three are the inventory. The bronze pillars, the stands, the bronze sea and its twelve bulls, the pots and shovels and snuffers and basins. And then a lingering description of the two pillars. Twenty-seven feet high, hollow, with bronze capitals wreathed in pomegranates, ninety-six on the sides and a hundred all around.

Why would a historian slow down to count pomegranates on the day the temple burned?

Because you would. If your father's house burned, you would remember the banister. These pillars had names. First Kings seven tells us Solomon called one Jachin, which means he will establish, and the other Boaz, which means in him is strength. Matthew Henry notices that they held nothing up. They were signs. When the Chaldeans broke them up, God was saying, I am no longer establishing this house, and its strength is gone.

And now the vessel thread. Remember Hananiah, who broke the yoke off Jeremiah's neck in 594 and promised the vessels already taken would be home within two years? Jeremiah answered in chapter twenty-seven with a list. The pillars, the sea, the stands, and the rest of the vessels that are left in this city. They shall be carried to Babylon and remain there until the day when I visit them, declares the Lord. Then I will bring them back and restore them to this place. Chapter fifty-two is the receipt for the first half of that sentence. And Ezra chapter one is the receipt for the second half, when Cyrus counted out more than five thousand gold and silver vessels to be carried home. That is Bel disgorging what he had swallowed. The great bronze never came back, but the vessels of worship did. Calvin, on chapter twenty-seven, makes the point that God never cared more for the vessels than for his people. The vessels came home because the people would.

Then verse twenty-four. The captain of the guard took Seraiah the chief priest, and Zephaniah the second priest, and the three keepers of the threshold, and about seventy officials, and the king struck them down at Riblah. Another Seraiah, and Matthew Poole is careful to say this is not Baruch's brother. This one is the high priest. And here is something remarkable. Turn to Ezra chapter seven, verse one, and you read, Ezra the son of Seraiah. Son there means descendant. Nebuchadnezzar executed the high priest at Riblah, and from his line came the scribe who would carry the law of Moses back to Jerusalem and read it to the people. The man was killed. The line was not.

Verses twenty-eight to thirty are found only in Jeremiah. The deportation register. In the seventh year, three thousand twenty-three. In the eighteenth year, eight hundred thirty-two. In the twenty-third year, seven hundred forty-five. Four thousand six hundred in all.

That does not match the ten thousand of Second Kings twenty-four. Most likely this register counts a particular group, perhaps adult men of Judah, while Kings gives the round total. But enjoy this. Older commentators wanted to correct seventh year to seventeenth, because Kings says the eighth. Then the Babylonian Chronicle, Babylon's own record, dated the capture of Jerusalem and its king to Nebuchadnezzar's seventh year, March of 597. Jeremiah's register was using Babylon's calendar, and it was right all along.

The third, in 582 B.C., is narrated nowhere else. Now do the arithmetic Matthew Henry does. A few thousand men go into exile, and Ezra two counts more than forty-two thousand coming home. As in Egypt, he says, the Lord made them fruitful in the land of their affliction.

And do not miss verse sixteen. Nebuzaradan left some of the poorest of the land to be vinedressers and plowmen. The people nobody thought worth the trouble of transporting were the people left on the land. The proud lost their vineyards. The poor were handed them.

Now the ending. Verse thirty-one. In the thirty-seventh year of the exile of Jehoiachin king of Judah, in the twelfth month, on the twenty-fifth day of the month, Evil-merodach king of Babylon, in the year that he began to reign, graciously freed Jehoiachin king of Judah and brought him out of prison.

That name is not a moral verdict. In Babylonian it is Amel-Marduk, man of Marduk, Nebuchadnezzar's son, who reigned about two years.

Behind graciously freed is a Hebrew idiom. He lifted up the head of Jehoiachin. You have heard that before. In Genesis forty, Joseph tells Pharaoh's cupbearer, In three days Pharaoh will lift up your head and restore you to your office. A prisoner, forgotten, and then on a king's birthday, lifted up. Matthew Poole says this idiom signifies the altering of one's estate that is in misery. Jehoiachin went into prison at eighteen. He came out at fifty-five.

Verse thirty-two. He spoke kindly to him and gave him a seat above the seats of the kings who were with him in Babylon. Verse thirty-three. So Jehoiachin put off his prison garments. And every day of his life he dined regularly at the king's table. And verse thirty-four, the last verse of the book. For his allowance, a regular allowance was given him by the king, according to his daily needs, until the day of his death, as long as he lived.

First, according to his daily needs is literally a day's matter in its day. That is the exact phrase from Exodus sixteen, verse four, when God rained bread from heaven and told Israel to gather a day's portion every day. The manna phrase. Jehoiachin lives on manna from a pagan king.

Second, the word for allowance is the one used in chapter forty, when Nebuzaradan released Jeremiah with an allowance of food. The book ends with both prophet and king living on Babylon's rations.

And now listen to that last verse again with verse eleven beside it. Zedekiah, in prison till the day of his death. Jehoiachin, at the king's table until the day of his death. The chapter that began with a king blinded in a cell ends with a king seated at a table.

So here is the hard question. Is this ending hope, or is it a sad little footnote?

Some read it as melancholy, and they have a case. Jeremiah twenty-two said Jehoiachin would die in a foreign land and never return, and he did die there. Joseph Parker, the London preacher, put the case sharply. "Jehoiachin at his best was only a decorated captive; he was still in Babylon. That is the sting. Not what have we, but where are we, is heaven's piercing inquiry." And the text says not one word about Jehoiachin repenting. A change of clothes, not of heart.

Feel that. A better seat in Babylon is still a seat in Babylon.

But I do not think that is why the editor ended here. Remember what God said about this man in chapter twenty-two. Though Coniah were the signet ring on my right hand, yet I would tear you off. Write this man down as childless, a man who shall not succeed in his days, for none of his offspring shall succeed in sitting on the throne of David. That sounds like the end of the line of David. The promise of Second Samuel seven, a son of David on the throne forever, looks dead and buried in Babylon.

And then the ration tablets tell you he had five sons. Childless as a king, not as a father. The line was alive, eating Babylonian oil. Calvin, commenting on that very curse, said this. "The grace of God, then, was hidden for a time, but never extinguished; for at length a rod did grow up from the stem of Jesse, as it is said by Isaiah." In the same place he says God's covenant was not dead but, as it were, for a time buried.

D. A. Carson, writing on the same ending in Second Kings, puts it this way. "The story of redemption is not yet done, the Davidic line not yet extinct. In the midst of crushing sin and slashing judgment, hope still beckons."

And here we need the New Testament, because it names this man. Matthew chapter one, verse twelve. After the deportation to Babylon, Jechoniah was the father of Shealtiel, and Shealtiel the father of Zerubbabel. Jehoiachin's grandson Zerubbabel led the exiles home, and in Haggai two, verse twenty-three, God says to him, I will make you like a signet ring, for I have chosen you. The ring that was torn off is put back on. The church has wrestled with how that curse relates to Christ. Irenaeus, back in the second century, used it to defend the virgin birth. "If indeed He had been the son of Joseph, He could not, according to Jeremiah, be either king or heir." Others in our own Reformed tradition, like Ra McLaughlin, answer that the curse was limited to Jehoiachin's own days, so that Jesus' legal claim to the throne runs right through him. Either way, Matthew sets this prisoner's name in the family tree of the King.

So is it hope or melancholy? I think the honest answer is that it is a small hope, deliberately small. A seed. Not the return, not the kingdom, not the Son of David on his throne. A captive king with a lifted head and a daily portion. Enough to tell the exiles that the almond is still awake. God was watching over his word to destroy, and he is watching over his word to build and to plant.

One more detail. The Cambridge commentators suggest the final phrase, as long as he lived, was placed there so the book would not end on the word death. Whatever the intention, Jeremiah does not close with death. It closes with life, a day at a time.

Spurgeon took the parallel verse in Second Kings for one of his morning readings. "Jehoiachin was not sent away from the king's palace with a store to last him for months, but his provision was given him as a daily pension. Herein he well pictures the happy position of all the Lord's people. A daily portion is all that a man really wants."

So here is where this lands. You probably have some promise of God that looks buried. A prayer for a child, unanswered for years. A sin you thought would be gone by now. Jeremiah fifty-two does not tell you everything will be fixed by Friday. It tells you God is still watching over his word, and that while you wait, he feeds you one day at a time. So tomorrow morning, when you open this book, do not ask for a store to last you for months. Ask for today's portion. And take it as evidence that the line is not dead.

One more line. After the return, the people asked Zechariah whether to keep fasting in the fifth month. God answered, The fast of the fourth month and the fast of the fifth and the fast of the seventh and the fast of the tenth shall be to the house of Judah seasons of joy and gladness and cheerful feasts. Every date in this chapter, turned into a feast. That is where this road goes, though next time it goes through Lamentations first.

The Song of Songs, chapter five. Verse one is the hinge of the whole book. Last time she said, Let my beloved come to his garden. Now he answers. I came to my garden, my sister, my bride, I gathered my myrrh with my spice, I ate my honeycomb with my honey, I drank my wine with my milk. Keil and Delitzsch read it plainly as the consummation of the marriage. That is the center of the Song, and it is holy.

Then another voice. Eat, friends, drink, and be drunk with love. Is it the groom to his guests, or the poet blessing the couple? Either way, at the very center of a book of Scripture, someone looks at a husband and wife on their wedding night and says, Enjoy it. Do not let anyone tell you the Bible is embarrassed by married love.

Then verse two, and the tone changes. I slept, but my heart was awake. Most commentators take that as a dream, and a familiar kind. A sound! My beloved is knocking. Open to me, my sister, my love, my dove, my perfect one, for my head is wet with dew. And she answers with excuses that are almost funny. I had put off my garment; how could I put it on? I had bathed my feet; how could I soil them? By the time she gets up and opens the latch, her hands dripping with myrrh, he is gone. I sought him, but found him not; I called him, but he gave no answer.

And then verse seven, which is the shock of the chapter. The watchmen found me, they beat me, they bruised me, they took away my veil. In chapter three the watchmen simply found her and she moved on. Now they wound her. On the plain level, a woman alone in the streets at night is taken for something she is not.

The old interpreters argued about those watchmen. Richard Sibbes said they are ministers who should have protected her and wounded her instead. Gregory of Nyssa, in the fourth century, said they are angels, and that their blows are a blessing that strips away a veil she did not need. Hudson Taylor, the missionary, took a middle road. He thought they were people who simply saw her lukewarmness more clearly than she did.

Samuel Rutherford, the Scottish Presbyterian, read the whole scene in a letter to a friend while the church in Scotland was suffering. "Because our Beloved was not let in by His spouse when He stood at the door, with His wet and frozen head, therefore He will have us to seek Him awhile; and while we are seeking, the watchmen who go about the walls have stricken the poor woman, and have taken away her veil from her. But yet a little while and our Lord will come again."

Then the daughters ask the question that turns the chapter around. What is your beloved more than another beloved? And she answers with the only head-to-toe portrait in the Song spoken by the woman about the man. Radiant and ruddy. Distinguished among ten thousand, and that word means bannered, lifted up like a flag over an army. Head of gold, eyes like doves, arms like rods of gold, legs like alabaster columns. Gold, ivory, sapphire, cedar. She describes him the way you would describe a temple.

And she ends, His mouth is most sweet, and he is altogether desirable. The Hebrew is one plural word, machamaddim. He is all desires. John Flavel, the Puritan, said of that word, "As all the rivers are gathered into the ocean, which is the congregation or meeting place of all the waters in the world: so Christ is that ocean in which all true delights and pleasures meet."

Then the line every married man should hear. This is my beloved and this is my friend. Lover and friend, both.

So make it practical. In marriage, love will knock at inconvenient times, when you are tired and have a perfectly good reason. The Song does not shame the bride for being human, but it shows you what a lazy no can cost. And notice what she did with the absence. She did not sulk. She talked about him. She praised him to anyone who would listen until they wanted to go find him too. If there is distance in your marriage this season, start there. Answer the knock, and when you cannot, rehearse what you love. And if the distance you feel is in your walk with the Lord, the same two things apply.

John six, verses forty-one through fifty-nine. Jesus has just said, I am the bread that came down from heaven, and verse forty-one says the Jews grumbled. It is the word the Greek Old Testament uses for Israel's grumbling in the wilderness. Same murmur, new wilderness. And what offends them is how ordinary he is. Is not this Jesus, the son of Joseph, whose father and mother we know?

Yesterday we heard the open door of verse thirty-seven. Whoever comes to me I will never cast out. Now Jesus tells you why anyone comes at all. Verse forty-four. No one can come to me unless the Father who sent me draws him. And I will raise him up on the last day.

The verb is helkuo, to draw. John uses it for hauling a net full of fish to shore in chapter twenty-one, and Luke uses it in Acts sixteen for Paul and Silas being dragged into the marketplace. R. C. Sproul once debated a professor who said it means to woo, since the Greeks used it for drawing water from a well. Sproul's answer. "But let me ask you, how do you get water out of a well? Do you look down and say, 'Here, water, water, water'? Do you woo it out? Of course not. That water is inert."

But here is the tension, and I like it. Spurgeon preached on this verse in 1858, as Calvinist as Sproul, and he said flatly that Christ does not drag people by the hair of their heads, and that in the Father's drawing there is no compulsion at all. Calvin sits in between. The drawing is not violent, he says, but it is a powerful work of the Spirit that makes the unwilling willing. They all mean the same thing. The drawing is necessary and it always works. They differ on the picture.

And the oldest picture may be the best. Augustine, preaching on this verse, said, "You hold out a green twig to a sheep, and you draw it. Nuts are shown to a child, and he is attracted; he is drawn by what he runs to, drawn by loving it, drawn without hurt to the body, drawn by a cord of the heart." God draws by making Christ delightful. Then verse forty-five tells you how. They will all be taught by God, quoting Isaiah fifty-four. The Father teaches the heart, and the taught come.

Then Jesus pushes harder. Your fathers ate the manna in the wilderness, and they died. Manna kept you alive for a day. And verse fifty-one. The bread that I will give for the life of the world is my flesh. That is the first time in John that the bread is tied to his death. When they argue, he does not soften it. Verse fifty-three. Unless you eat the flesh of the Son of Man and drink his blood, you have no life in you.

That is shocking. The law repeatedly forbids eating blood, and Leviticus seventeen gives the reason. The life is in the blood, and the blood makes atonement. That is the key. Jesus is talking about his blood as atonement, received.

Is this the Lord's Supper? Many of the fathers heard it that way. Calvin said no, not directly. Jesus had not yet instituted the Supper, and if this chapter were about the sacrament, everyone who took communion would have eternal life. He is talking about feeding on Christ by faith, which the Supper then signs and seals. And Calvin's line is unforgettable. "It is a mockery to dream of any way of eating the flesh of Christ without faith, since faith alone is the mouth, so to speak, and the stomach of the soul." Bishop Ryle added the penitent thief. He never took the Supper, yet who would say he had no life in him? Our Westminster Confession says that at the Table believers really, but spiritually, feed on Christ crucified. The same Christ, fed on by faith, in the Word and at the Table.

From verse fifty-four on, Jesus switches to a rougher verb for eating, trogo, to chew. Scholars debate how much to make of that, but he certainly did not reach for a gentler word. Verse fifty-seven. Whoever feeds on me, he also will live because of me. Present tense. A day's portion every day.

First Peter chapter three, verses thirteen through twenty-two. After Psalm thirty-four, Peter asks, verse thirteen, Who is there to harm you if you are zealous for what is good? And then he admits the answer. Even if you should suffer for righteousness' sake, you will be blessed.

Verses fourteen and fifteen quote Isaiah eight. Back in the 730s B.C., King Ahaz was terrified of the alliance between Syria and Israel, and God told Isaiah, Do not fear what they fear, but the Lord of hosts, him you shall honor as holy. Peter takes that verse and puts Christ where Isaiah had the Lord of hosts. In your hearts honor Christ the Lord as holy. That is how high Peter's view of Jesus is.

Then, always being prepared to make a defense, an apologia, to anyone who asks you for a reason for the hope that is in you. Sproul liked to point out that this is not apologizing for being a Christian. It is a defense, as in a courtroom. And notice the order. Fear goes when Christ is set apart as holy in the heart, and the answer flows from there, with gentleness, respect, and a good conscience. Calvin says Peter is not asking every believer to answer every question. He is asking you to be ready to say why you hope.

Then verse eighteen, one of the great gospel sentences. For Christ also suffered once for sins, the righteous for the unrighteous, that he might bring us to God. The verb bring is a word for being ushered into a king's presence. Robert Leighton, the Scottish archbishop, saw a Savior who does not just open the door. "And if the soul bear back still through distrust, he takes it by the hand, and draws it forward, leads it unto his Father."

Now the hard part. Verse nineteen. He went and proclaimed to the spirits in prison, because they formerly did not obey, when God's patience waited in the days of Noah. There are three main readings. Augustine argued that Christ, by his Spirit, preached through Noah to people who were alive then and are now in prison. The ark itself, he said, was a sermon. Many Protestant commentators followed him, from Poole and Gill to Wayne Grudem. A second view says Christ went to the realm of the dead between his death and resurrection. Calvin surprises people here. He denies this is about the descent into hell, and argues instead that Christ's grace was shown to the faithful who were waiting. A third view, held by John MacArthur and others, says these spirits are the fallen angels of Genesis six, and Christ announced his triumph to them. MacArthur says, "I believe He went to declare His victory over demons and that the spirits refer to demons."

I lean toward Augustine. Peter has already said the Spirit of Christ spoke in the prophets, and Second Peter calls Noah a herald of righteousness. But hold it humbly. Robert Leighton changed his mind on this verse in a later note. And notice what every one of these voices agrees on. No second chance after death, and a few were saved while many refused. Eight persons.

Then verse twenty-one. Baptism, which corresponds to this, now saves you, not as a removal of dirt from the body but as an appeal to God for a good conscience, through the resurrection of Jesus Christ. Peter guards it in both directions. Not the water. The resurrection. As a Presbyterian, I believe with the Westminster Confession that baptism is a sign and seal of the covenant, and that its grace is truly given by the Spirit in God's time, though not tied to the moment the water is applied. My Baptist brothers like MacArthur read that word appeal as the believer's pledge, and want the sign given to those who make it. We disagree, and we both say what Leighton said about the sacraments. "They are then, in a word, neither empty signs to those who believe, nor effectual causes of grace to those who believe not."

And verse twenty-two ends it where suffering Christians most need it. He has gone into heaven, and is at the right hand of God, with angels, authorities, and powers subjected to him.

Two connections before we go, and both are really in the text.

First, the bread. Jeremiah ends with a captive king receiving a day's matter in its day, the manna phrase, from the king of Babylon, until the day of his death. And in John, Jesus says, your fathers ate the manna and died, but whoever feeds on this bread will live forever. Babylon could feed a son of David until he died. God gives his people a Son of David to feed on, and they do not die. That is the difference between a decorated captive and a child at the Father's table.

And notice the seats. Evil-merodach gave a son of David a seat above the seats of the kings who were with him in Babylon. Babylon's best honor ended at his death. Peter ends today's passage with another Son of David, seated at the right hand of God, with angels, authorities, and powers subjected to him. That seat does not end.

Second, the few. Four thousand six hundred carried off and a remnant left among the vines. Eight persons in the ark. A line of David hidden in a Babylonian storeroom. God has never needed a crowd to keep his word. He watches over it.

So here is a question to carry through your day. What promise of God in your life looks buried right now, and will you ask him today only for today's portion?
