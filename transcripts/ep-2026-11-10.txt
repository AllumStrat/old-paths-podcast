Ezekiel thirty-one, verse two. Whom are you like in your greatness?

God shows Pharaoh a tree. A cedar on Lebanon, its top among the clouds, so beautiful that the trees in the garden of God envied it. Then you hear the axe. Then the crash, and the whole forest goes quiet.

So here is today's question. What are you growing tall on? And who has been watering it?

This is Old Paths. Today is day twelve of the eleventh month in the reading plan, day two hundred sixty-two of three hundred. The readings are Ezekiel chapters thirty-one and thirty-two, Job chapter eleven, John chapter ten, verses eleven through twenty-one, and First John chapter two, verses eighteen through twenty-three.

Last time Pharaoh lay in the Nile like a crocodile and said, My Nile is my own; I made it for myself. Today the crocodile comes back, and Ezekiel finishes the whole case against Egypt with a tree and a grave. In Job, the third friend finally opens his mouth. In John, the door becomes a shepherd. And in First John, the apostle who just told us that the world is passing away now tells us what time it is.

Ezekiel thirty-one.

These two chapters close the oracles against the nations. Chapter thirty-one is dated the eleventh year, the third month, the first day. That is June of 587 B.C., two months after the broken arm oracle we read last time. Jerusalem is still surrounded. Hophra's army has already come up and gone home.

And God asks Pharaoh a question. Whom are you like in your greatness? Then, instead of answering, he tells a story. Behold, Assyria was a cedar in Lebanon.

The Cambridge Bible tries to make this tree Egypt itself, reading the word for Assyria as a kind of cypress. But Keil notes that the cedar's fall is told as already finished, while Pharaoh's is still coming, and verse eighteen asks the question again. The cedar is Assyria. God is holding up a mirror.

And what a mirror. Verse four. The waters nourished it; the deep made it grow tall. Verse six. All the birds of the heavens made their nests in its boughs, and under its shadow lived all great nations. Verse eight. The cedars in the garden of God could not rival it. That is Eden language, the same garden Ezekiel used for the king of Tyre. And then verse nine, a phrase that is easy to skip. I made it beautiful.

The tree did not plant itself. The deep that fed its roots was not its own deep. Assyria's beauty was a gift.

And remember Sennacherib's boast in Isaiah thirty-seven, that he had cut down Lebanon's tallest cedars. The king who felled the cedars becomes the cedar that is felled.

Verse ten gives the reason. Because it towered high and set its top among the clouds, and its heart was proud of its height. Matthew Poole puts it simply: Assyria could not carry that much prosperity, and in its pride it forgot the God who had lifted it up.

So verse twelve. Foreigners, the most ruthless of nations, have cut it down. That happened. In 612 B.C. the Babylonians and the Medes besieged Nineveh for three months. A Babylonian chronicle tablet records that they hauled off the plunder of the city and its temple and left Nineveh a heap of ruins.

Now look at verse seventeen. Those who were its arm, who lived under its shadow among the nations, went down to Sheol with it. Who sat in Assyria's shade at the end? Egypt did. As Assyria was dying, Egypt marched north to hold it up. Second Kings twenty-three says Pharaoh Neco went up to the king of Assyria to the river Euphrates, and killed King Josiah at Megiddo on the way. The same Babylonian chronicle tells us that at Harran, fear of the enemy overcame the last Assyrian king and the army of Egypt that had come to help him, and they abandoned the city. Pharaoh had stood in that shade. He had heard the tree fall. And now God says, verse eighteen, This is Pharaoh and all his multitude.

Chapter thirty-two has two dates, and the dates matter. The first of the twelfth month of the twelfth year, then two weeks later. That is March of 585 B.C. Jerusalem has been in ashes for a year and a half. And as Ellicott notes, it is nearly two months since the fugitive arrived in chapter thirty-three. We have been tracking Ezekiel's silence for weeks. So these laments over Egypt are spoken after his mouth was opened. The prophet who was proved right about Jerusalem now sings Egypt's funeral.

Verse two. You consider yourself a lion of the nations, but you are like a dragon in the seas. That is our crocodile, tannim, back again. Ellicott catches the contrast. Pharaoh's courtiers called him a lion, the king of beasts. God says, no, you are a crocodile thrashing in your canals and fouling the very rivers that feed your people. Then the net, the open field, and verse seven, I will cover the sun with a cloud. That is day of the Lord language. When a power this large falls, it is as if the lights of the sky go out.

Then comes the strangest passage in these chapters. Verses seventeen through thirty-two. Ezekiel is told, wail over the multitude of Egypt, and send them down to the world below, to those who have gone down to the pit.

Two Hebrew words carry this whole section. The first is Sheol, the realm of the dead. The second is bor, which means the pit, a cistern or a grave shaft. Ezekiel walks us down into the pit and calls the roll. Assyria is there, with her graves all around her. Elam is there. Meshech and Tubal are there. Edom, the princes of the north, the Sidonians. And the same refrain tolls over each one: slain by the sword, uncircumcised, who spread terror in the land of the living.

Uncircumcised is a deliberate insult. The Egyptians actually practiced circumcision, so the Pulpit Commentary takes the word here as pure scorn. A king who spent his reign preparing an honored tomb is told he will lie among the people he despised most.

Then the last scene. Verse thirty-one. When Pharaoh sees them, he will be comforted for all his multitude. The word is nacham, to be comforted. What kind of comfort is that? Keil says Pharaoh will simply realize that no ruler before him got a better ending. John Gill, the Baptist, is blunter. Though such comfort as this must be poor comfort indeed! and yet this is all the comfort wicked men have in hell, that they have company with them there.

That raises the hard question. Is Ezekiel thirty-two a map of the afterlife, with the dead sorted into national cemeteries?

Notice that Keil and Gill do not quite agree. Gill reads straight to hell. Keil is more careful. Sheol, he says, is the nether world, the gathering-place of the dead; not the place of punishment for the damned. And R. C. Sproul is honest about it. The concept of life after death in the Old Testament, indicated often by references to Sheol, is somewhat vague and shadowy; death is depicted as a place beyond the grave where both good and bad people go. He adds that the saints did know of a future life. It simply is not as clear as in the New Testament.

So here is how I would read it. This is a dirge, a funeral song. Ezekiel is not drawing a floor plan. But a poem can still tell the truth. It tells you that the dead have not ceased to be. It tells you that death levels every empire. And it tells you that terror spread in the land of the living follows a man down into the grave as shame. The fuller light comes later. The Westminster Confession, in chapter thirty-two, says the souls of the wicked are cast into hell and the souls of the righteous are received into the highest heavens, and that Scripture knows no third place. Ezekiel's pit is the shadow. The confession states the substance.

Now bring it home. The cedar's sin was not its height. God made it tall. God watered it. God said, I made it beautiful. The sin was that its heart was proud of its height, as if the water came from inside the tree. Verse fourteen says the whole story was told so that no other tree would try it.

So what has God watered in your life? Your mind, your health, your work, your reputation. None of it is wrong. But if you start to believe the deep belongs to you, you are standing in Pharaoh's shade. And down in the pit no one asks your title.

Job chapter eleven.

Zophar the Naamathite finally speaks, and he is the bluntest of the three. Chapter eleven falls into three parts. Verses one through six, a rebuke. Should a man full of talk be judged right? Verses seven through twelve, the unsearchable God. Verses thirteen through twenty, a call to repent with a promise attached.

Verse six is the knife. Know then that God exacts of you less than your guilt deserves. Keil explains the Hebrew verb as giving to forgetfulness. God has let some of your guilt be forgotten. Say that to a man who has just buried ten children.

Then verses seven and eight. Can you find out the deep things of God? It is higher than heaven, deeper than Sheol. That is magnificent, and it is true. Then verse twelve calls Job, in effect, a wild donkey's colt.

So how do you read a friend who says true things? God himself says in chapter forty-two that these men did not speak of him what is right.

Spurgeon's advice is plain. Remember, dear friends, that whenever you read the words of these three men, you must take them with a good many grains of salt. They are not to be accepted as if they were God's Word, because they are not. He means that Scripture records their speeches truthfully, not that it endorses them.

Gregory the Great puts his finger on the real fault. Zophar delivered many forcible sayings, but he is not conscious that he is addressing them to a better than himself. Gregory is surprisingly gentle with him, though. He thinks great teachers often overshoot in correction out of zeal.

Calvin, preaching through Job in Geneva, praised the friends' theology and still condemned them, because they applied it without pity. They should have mixed some sugar with the medicine, he said.

Right doctrine at the wrong bedside can still be cruelty. Before you speak a true word to a suffering friend, ask whether you are speaking to him or at him.

John chapter ten, verses eleven through twenty-one.

Jesus is still speaking to the Pharisees who threw the blind man out. Behind every word stands Ezekiel thirty-four, which we read tomorrow, where God condemns the shepherds of Israel who fed themselves and promises to shepherd his flock himself.

I am the good shepherd. The word good is kalos, good in the sense of noble, beautiful, the real thing. And the mark of the real thing is in the same breath. The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep.

Then the hired hand, who sees the wolf coming and runs. Augustine asks who that is today, and his answer is uncomfortable. It is the pastor who will not rebuke a sheep the wolf has by the throat. If that man says, I have not fled, here I am, Augustine replies, You have fled, because you have been silent; you have been silent, because you have been afraid. The flight of the mind is fear.

Verse sixteen. I have other sheep that are not of this fold. Calvin is sure the fold is Israel and the other sheep are the Gentiles. Spurgeon, preaching in 1883, thought instead the fold was the small band of disciples standing around Jesus. Either way, Calvin draws out the comfort. We are already God's sheep, before we are aware that He is our shepherd. And in his own Latin, Calvin rendered the end of the verse one flock, not one fold. There are many pens. There is one flock.

Then verses seventeen and eighteen. No one takes it from me, but I lay it down of my own accord. Chrysostom points out that any man can end his life, but no mere man can lay it down so that no one could take it from him against his will. And Augustine adds that the Son also raised himself, rising from the tomb as a man gets up from his bed.

The crowd splits again. He has a demon. Others answer, can a demon open the eyes of the blind? This time Jesus says nothing. The healed man is his answer.

First John chapter two, verses eighteen through twenty-three.

Last time John said the world is passing away. Now verse eighteen. Children, it is the last hour.

How can it be the last hour two thousand years later? Augustine answered that sixteen centuries ago. This same last hour is long; yet it is the last. Calvin adds that John fixes no date. He simply means that nothing remains on God's calendar except the return of Christ.

Only John uses the word antichrist, and Augustine explains it simply: contrary to Christ. The coming antichrist already has forerunners. They went out from us, but they were not of us.

That verse hurt then, and it hurts now. Calvin says it disturbs the weak more when one of our own falls away than when a thousand outsiders attack. His answer is that they were chaff on the same threshing floor as the wheat, and that John is resting on God's faithfulness, not ours. The Westminster Confession, chapter seventeen, says that those God has effectually called can neither totally nor finally fall away. But a Ligonier devotion on this verse adds a wise caution. Repentance is possible until death, so pray for the ones who left.

Then verse twenty. You have been anointed. The Greek is chrisma, and John is surely playing on the words: the antichrists left, but you have the chrisma of the Christ. And the test is not vague. Who is the liar but he who denies that Jesus is the Christ? No one who denies the Son has the Father.

One connection today, and it is a real one.

Pharaoh goes down into the pit, and the most he finds there is company. In John ten, a shepherd goes down into death on purpose, because no one could take his life, and he comes back up with authority to take it again. Ezekiel's pit has no exit. The good shepherd made one.

So carry this question today. Whose shade are you sitting in, and will it still be standing when the axe comes?
