Ezekiel thirty-nine, verse fifteen. And when these travel through the land and anyone sees a human bone, then he shall set up a sign by it, till the buriers have buried it.

Picture the work. Men walking the hills of Israel for months, eyes on the ground, planting a marker beside every bone they find. Last time, bones rose and stood on their feet. Today, bones go into the ground and stay there.

What makes the difference?

This is Old Paths. Today is day fifteen of the eleventh month in the reading plan, day two hundred sixty-five of three hundred. The readings are Ezekiel chapters thirty-eight and thirty-nine, Job chapter fourteen, John chapter eleven, verses seventeen through thirty-seven, and First John chapter three, verses eleven through eighteen.

Yesterday we stood in the valley of dry bones and heard God ask, can these bones live? Matthew Henry noticed something about that valley that only pays off today. Israel's bones were lying out in the open. Gog's bones, in chapter thirty-nine, will be buried. Hold on to that. And remember where chapter thirty-seven ended. My dwelling place shall be with them. The glory of God is on its way back to a temple, and in four more chapters it arrives. But first there is a war.

Ezekiel thirty-eight and thirty-nine.

These chapters carry no date. They come after the news of Jerusalem's fall reached the exiles, in about January of 585 B.C., and before the temple vision, which is dated to 573 B.C. And notice where they sit in the book's flow. Chapters thirty-four through thirty-seven have promised a shepherd king, a new heart, a living people and a reunited nation. Then, before the temple, comes this. A restored people, living in peace, attacked one last time by the whole world.

Poole saw the pastoral reason. Exiles hearing those glorious promises might expect clear skies from the day they came home. God warns them now, so that when the storm comes they do not decide he has failed.

Look at the names in verses two through six. Gog, of the land of Magog. Magog was a son of Japheth in Genesis ten, and Jewish tradition, followed by Josephus and Jerome, linked his descendants with the Scythians, the horse nomads of the far north. Ellicott reminds us that Scythian raiders had swept through Asia Minor and down toward Egypt within living memory of Ezekiel. Meshech and Tubal were peoples of Asia Minor, south of the Black Sea. Gomer and Beth-togarmah, from the uttermost parts of the north. Then Persia to the east, Cush and Put far to the south. These are peoples from the edges of the known map. Count them, and there are seven. Ezekiel is assembling the whole world against the people of God.

And here is the first question most people ask. Is this Russia? Your ESV margin says, or, prince of Rosh. Rosh can mean chief, or it may be a place name, and because it sounds a little like Russia, some readers have built whole end times charts on it. A Ligonier Tabletalk devotional on this chapter answers plainly. Yet we must reject this interpretation because the nation of Russia did not exist in Ezekiel's day. If the passage had no meaning to its original audience, we could twist and shape its interpretation like a wax nose.

That is the right instinct. Start where Ezekiel's first hearers stood.

Now watch how the chapter is built. Verse four. I will turn you about and put hooks into your jaws, and I will bring you out. Before we ever see Gog's army, before the horses and the shields, God has a hook in the invader's mouth. Matthew Henry caught why that order matters. Thus tender is God of the comfort of his people, thus careful that they may not be frightened; even before the trouble begins he tells them it will end well.

Then verse ten. Thoughts will come into your mind, and you will devise an evil scheme. So who is behind this invasion? God draws Gog out, and Gog plots evil. Both are true, and Gog is fully guilty for his greed. He looks at the land of unwalled villages, the quiet people who dwell securely, and sees plunder. Verse twelve says they dwell at the center of the earth. The Hebrew is tabbur, the navel. Israel is the hub of the world, not on a map, but in God's purposes.

Then verse seventeen, a strange question. Are you he of whom I spoke in former days by my servants the prophets? No earlier prophet names Gog. Ellicott and the Cambridge Bible both note this, and they draw the same conclusion. Gog is the summing up of every attack the prophets had warned about. Every enemy Israel ever faced, gathered into one last army.

And the battle is not much of a battle. Earthquake, every man's sword against his brother, pestilence, hailstones, fire and sulfur. Israel never fights. God does it all.

Chapter thirty-nine retells the defeat and then lingers over the aftermath. The weapons are so many that Israel burns them as firewood for seven years. The dead are so many that burying them takes seven months, in order to cleanse the land, because in the law a corpse defiled whatever it touched. The burial ground gets a name. Hamon-gog, which means the multitude of Gog. The army that came to swallow the land is swallowed by it.

Now come back to Henry's observation, from his comments on the valley of dry bones. The bones of Gog and Magog shall be buried, he wrote, for their destruction is final; but the bones of Israel are in the open valley, under the eye of Heaven, for there is hope in their end. Israel's bones lay exposed, waiting for the breath. Gog's bones are buried with a marker, and nobody is coming to raise them. And notice, Henry adds that burying even enemies is a good work, because even they shall rise again.

Then verse seventeen of chapter thirty-nine. God invites the birds and beasts to a sacrificial feast on the mountains of Israel. The meal is the flesh of the mighty. It is a terrible picture, and John borrows it in Revelation nineteen, where an angel calls the birds to the great supper of God as the rider on the white horse rides out against the beast.

So here is the hardest question. Who is Gog, and when does this happen?

Honest commentators have struggled here. Henry admits that for want of a historical key, we are locked out of the meaning. Poole and Henry both wonder about Antiochus Epiphanes, who persecuted the Jews in the second century before Christ. Gill thought of the Turks. And Ambrose of Milan, writing in about 378, the year the Goths crushed a Roman army at Adrianople, was sure. That Gog is the Goth, whose coming forth we have already seen, and over whom victory in days to come is promised. Every generation has been tempted to find Gog in its own newspaper.

Augustine, a generation later, said no. Writing on Revelation twenty, he insisted that Gog and Magog are not some barbarian nation in one corner of the world. They are the enemies of God spread through every nation, and the camp of the saints they surround is the church of Christ throughout the world.

I think Augustine is right, and the text itself pushes us there. Seven years of firewood. A valley east of the Dead Sea receiving an army that invaded from the north. Birds called to a sacrifice. Ellicott says these details cannot be taken literally, and that they signal a meaning beyond any single earthly war. And the New Testament settles it. Revelation twenty, verses eight and nine. After the thousand years, Satan deceives the nations at the four corners of the earth, Gog and Magog, and they surround the camp of the saints and the beloved city, and fire comes down from heaven and consumes them. John takes Ezekiel's Gog and makes him the final rage of the world against the church.

So Gog is not a code to crack from the headlines. He is a portrait of the last assault, and its outcome is fixed.

Why does God allow it at all? The chapters keep answering. Thirty-eight, verse sixteen. That the nations may know me. Thirty-nine, verse twenty-three. The nations shall know that the house of Israel went into captivity for their iniquity. The exile was never God's weakness. It was his justice, and the defeat of Gog will prove it.

And then the last verse. I will not hide my face anymore from them, when I pour out my Spirit upon the house of Israel. The Spirit promised in chapter thirty-six and breathed into the bones in chapter thirty-seven is the guarantee that God's face will not turn away again.

Here is the application. God showed his people the hook in Gog's jaw before he showed them Gog's army. You know the end of the story before you face the battle. Whatever is rising against you or against Christ's church this year, the Lord has already named the valley where it will be buried. So do not read the headlines in fear, and do not read the Bible as a decoder ring. Read the ending, and then go to your work in peace.

Job chapter fourteen.

This chapter closes Job's long reply to Zophar and the first round of speeches. It moves in three steps. Verses one through six, how short life is. Man who is born of a woman is few of days and full of trouble. Verses seven through seventeen, the tree and a longing. Verses eighteen through twenty-two, the mountain worn down by water. So you destroy the hope of man.

The middle is where the poetry lives. For there is hope for a tree, if it be cut down, that it will sprout again. Keil thinks Job had the palm in mind, which sends up new shoots from a dead stump. But a man dies, and where is he?

Now catch a word. In verse seven, sprout again translates a Hebrew verb from the root chalaph, to change or to pass on. In verse fourteen, Job says he would wait till my renewal should come, and renewal is the same root. Job takes the tree's word and reaches for it himself. All the days of my service, my hard service like a soldier on duty, I would wait for my relief.

So does verse fourteen hope or despair? If a man dies, shall he live again? Keil says the honest answer of Job's mind at this moment is no, but that the wish itself rises from a craving of his heart. Cyril of Jerusalem, teaching new believers around the year 350, heard in the tree an argument for the resurrection. Since a tree falls and revives, shall not man, for whom all trees were made, himself revive?

Spurgeon gave the best answer. In 1862 a pit disaster at the Hartley Colliery killed some two hundred miners, and he preached on this verse. His answer was two words. No. Yes. No, a man does not come back to repeat this life, so use it now. And then, yes. He does not die like a dog; he shall live again; not here, but in another and a better or a more terrible land.

Job's question was honest. Bring yours to God the same way.

John chapter eleven, verses seventeen through thirty-seven.

Jesus arrives, and Lazarus has been in the tomb four days. There is no doubt left that he is dead. Martha meets him on the road with a grief that is also a confession. Lord, if you had been here, my brother would not have died. Jesus says, your brother will rise again. And Martha answers with good orthodox theology. I know that he will rise again in the resurrection on the last day.

She is right, and it is not enough. Jesus does not correct her doctrine. He puts himself inside it. I am the resurrection and the life. The last day has come walking up the road to Bethany.

Then Mary falls at his feet with the same words, and verse thirty-three says he was deeply moved in his spirit and greatly troubled. Look at your ESV margin. Or, was indignant. The Greek verb is embrimaomai, and outside this passage it describes a snort of anger or a stern rebuke. Benjamin Warfield, the great Princeton theologian, argued that Jesus came to the grave not in uncontrollable grief but in irrepressible anger. Angry at whom? It is death that is the object of his wrath, and behind death him who has the power of death, and whom he has come into the world to destroy.

Chrysostom read the word differently, as Jesus checking and restraining his own grief, and Calvin stressed that the Son of God took on true human feelings, sin only excepted. You do not have to choose. Anger at death and tears for his friends sit side by side, and then the shortest verse in your Bible. Jesus wept. He knew what he was about to do, and he wept anyway.

First John chapter three, verses eleven through eighteen.

Last time John said the children of God and the children of the devil are known by two marks, righteousness and love. Now he takes up love. This is the message that you have heard from the beginning, that we should love one another. And he gives the first counterexample in history, Cain, who murdered his brother because his own deeds were evil and his brother's righteous.

Then verse fourteen. We know that we have passed out of death into life, because we love the brothers. Calvin is careful here. John argues from the sign, not the cause. Love does not move you out of death. It shows that the Spirit already has.

Verse sixteen sets the measure. By this we know love, that he laid down his life for us. Then verse seventeen brings it down to the ground. If anyone has the world's goods and sees his brother in need, yet closes his heart against him, how does God's love abide in him? Augustine joined the two verses in one sentence. If you are not yet equal to the dying for your brother, be even now equal to the giving of your means to your brother. The road to laying down your life starts with opening your wallet and your heart.

Here is the connection, and it is real. Every reading today stares at death. Gog's army is buried in a valley with a name. Job asks whether a man shall live again. Jesus stands at a grave angry and weeping. And John says those who love have already passed out of death into life. In Christ, death is an enemy whose burial place is already named.

Carry this question through the day. Who is the brother in need you have seen this week, and what would it look like to love him in deed and in truth, before sundown?
