I am the man who has seen affliction under the rod of his wrath.

That is how this morning's poem begins. Darkness without any light. A bear lying in wait. Arrows in the kidneys. Teeth ground on gravel.

And twenty lines later, the same man, in the same ruins, says this. The steadfast love of the Lord never ceases. His mercies never come to an end. They are new every morning. Great is your faithfulness.

Nothing outside him has changed. The city is still ashes. So what changed?

He remembered something. And by the end of this episode I want you to know exactly what it was.

This is Old Paths. Today is day twenty-four of the tenth month in the reading plan, day two hundred forty-nine of three hundred. The readings are Lamentations chapter three, the Song of Songs chapter eight, verses one through seven, John chapter seven, verses fourteen through twenty-four, and First Peter chapter five, verses one through seven.

Last time we stood in Lamentations two and heard the poet ask a question he could not answer. What can I liken to you, that I may comfort you? Your ruin is vast as the sea; who can heal you? Chapter two ended with no answer. Zion was talking to God, and that was all.

Today the answer begins. Not from outside the book. From inside it, in the very middle of it.

I have also been following a little word with you. Like. Like a widow in chapter one. Like an enemy in chapter two. Today it is like a bear and like a lion. And yes, the mouth comes before the eye again.

In the Song, we reach the summit of the whole book, love strong as death. In John, Jesus stops hiding and teaches openly in the temple. And Peter, the man who once denied Jesus by a charcoal fire, turns to the elders of the churches and tells them how to shepherd.

Lamentations chapter three.

The setting has not moved. It is still the aftermath of the summer of 586 B.C. The temple is burned, the walls are down, King Zedekiah has been blinded and dragged to Babylon, and the survivors are sitting in the rubble. But the poem has changed shape.

Chapters one and two had twenty-two stanzas, one for each letter of the Hebrew alphabet. Chapter three has sixty-six lines, and it runs through the alphabet three lines at a time. Three lines start with aleph, then three with beth, and so on to the end. Matthew Henry notes that the earlier poems were single alphabets and this one is a triple alphabet. It is the most tightly built poem in the book, and it sits at the center, the third of five. Whatever is at the heart of this chapter is the heart of Lamentations.

And the speaker has changed. In chapters one and two Zion spoke as a woman, a widow, a daughter. Now we hear, I am the man. The Hebrew word is geber, a man in his strength, a grown man. The strong man is the one who has been broken.

Now read verses one through eighteen and notice something Calvin noticed. God is never named. He has driven me. He has made my flesh waste away. He has walled me about. Calvin points out that the prophet speaks of God only with a pronoun, as of one too well known to need naming. For seventeen lines it is only he, he, he. The man knows exactly who is doing this. He cannot bring himself to say the name.

And here is the next step in our like thread. Verse ten. He is a bear lying in wait for me, a lion in hiding. In chapter one Zion was like a widow. In chapter two the Lord became like an enemy. Now the man says God is a predator crouched in the brush. That is the darkest of the three, and it is meant to be. Matthew Henry puts the danger well. When God's hand is turned against us all day long, we are tempted to think his heart is turned against us too. Hold on to that word heart. It comes back in verse thirty-three.

Then the bottom. Verse eighteen. So I say, My endurance has perished; so has my hope from the Lord.

That is the first time the covenant name appears in the poem, at the moment he says his hope in it is gone. Even his despair is addressed to the right person.

Before we turn the corner, here is our first hard question. Who is this man?

Some say Jeremiah himself, mocked by his own people in verse fourteen and thrown into a pit in verse fifty-three. John Gill took that line. Others say the man is the nation, Israel personified, speaking with one voice. Calvin thought so, and Henry says it seems rather to be the voice of the church in captivity. And notice that in verse forty the I turns into we. Let us test and examine our ways.

D. A. Carson admits it is hard to decide. I will give you my answer. It is a real man, very likely Jeremiah, who has suffered so deeply with his people that he can speak for all of them. And that is the genius of the poem. Anyone in the ruins could pray it. You can pray it. I am the man. Then, verse twenty-one, you can pray the turn.

Here it is. Remember my affliction and my wanderings, the wormwood and the gall! My soul continually remembers it and is bowed down within me. But this I call to mind, and therefore I have hope.

Same faculty, two directions. In verse twenty memory is crushing him. In verse twenty-one memory saves him. Spurgeon preached this verse in October 1865 and called memory the handmaid of hope. Despair uses memory like a whip. Faith trains the same memory to bring the promises. The man gets no new facts. He calls old ones to mind.

And what does he call to mind? Verse twenty-two. The steadfast love of the Lord never ceases; his mercies never come to an end.

Three Hebrew words carry the whole turn. Steadfast love is hesed, God's covenant loyalty, love that has bound itself by promise. Mercies is rachamim, compassions, and it comes from the word for womb. It is a mother's kind of feeling. And in verse twenty-three, great is your faithfulness, the word is emunah, firmness, reliability, the thing you can lean your weight on.

Look at your footnote on verse twenty-two. The Hebrew text as we have it reads, because of the steadfast love of the Lord we are not cut off, or in the older translation, it is of the Lord's mercies that we are not consumed. The Syriac and the Aramaic Targum read it as, the mercies do not cease. The difference in Hebrew is one letter. Gill mentions that the medieval rabbi Rashi already treated the two forms as the same, and Calvin preferred the reading our English Standard Version follows. Either way it is true. We are not consumed, because his mercies are not consumed.

Then, they are new every morning. Calvin was lecturing on this verse in Geneva when the bell rang, and the old lecture notes record what he said. "I am surprised at the hour striking so soon; I hardly think that I have lectured a whole hour." The great Reformer lost track of time in verse twenty-three.

New every morning. John Piper draws the lesson from the manna in the wilderness, given one day at a time, with no storing up. "Today's mercies are for today's troubles. Tomorrow's mercies are for tomorrow's troubles." You cannot carry tomorrow on today's mercy. Tomorrow's is not late. It is not due yet.

Verse twenty-four. The Lord is my portion, says my soul, therefore I will hope in him.

Portion is cheleq, an allotted share. In Numbers eighteen God told the priests of Aaron's house they would have no portion in the land. I am your portion. Now think where this man is sitting. Every family's allotment from the days of Joshua has been lost. And he says, the Lord is my portion. They took the land. They could not take the Lord.

Spurgeon told a story in that same sermon. "One of our kings, high and haughty in temper, had a quarrel with the citizens of London, and thought to alarm them by a dreadful threat that would cow the spirits of the bold burghers, for if they did not mind what they were at he would remove his Court from Westminster. Whereupon, the doughty Lord Mayor begged to enquire whether His Majesty meant to take the Thames away, for so long as the river remained, his majesty might take himself wherever he pleased." And then Spurgeon's point. "We reply, so long as you cannot take our Lord away we will not complain."

Now look at a lovely bit of craft. The three lines for the letter teth, verses twenty-five through twenty-seven, all begin with the same word. Tov. Good. Good is the Lord to those who wait for him. Good it is that one should wait quietly for the salvation of the Lord. Good it is for a man that he bear the yoke in his youth. A poet in the ashes chose to write the word good three times in a row.

And then verses twenty-eight and twenty-nine. Let him sit alone in silence when it is laid on him; let him put his mouth in the dust, there may yet be hope. Remember that posture. Peter is going to describe it too.

Then verse thirty-one, the Lord will not cast off forever. Verse thirty-two, though he cause grief, he will have compassion according to the abundance of his steadfast love. And verse thirty-three, for he does not afflict from his heart or grieve the children of men.

From his heart. Henry warned us that when the hand is against us we fear the heart is too. Here the poet answers him. The hand is heavy. The heart is not in it.

But that brings us to the hardest question in the chapter. Five verses later, verses thirty-seven and thirty-eight. Who has spoken and it came to pass, unless the Lord has commanded it? Is it not from the mouth of the Most High that good and bad come?

So which is it? Does God not afflict from his heart, or does every disaster come from his mouth? The poet does not think he is contradicting himself.

Calvin answers with a picture from a courtroom. God is like a judge, "who, when he ascends his throne and condemns the guilty to death, does not do this from his heart, because he wishes all to be innocent, and thus to have a reason for acquitting them." And yet, Calvin adds, "he willingly condemns the guilty, because this is his duty." The judge truly passes the sentence. He does not enjoy it.

Calvin is just as firm that nothing happens by chance, because nobody prays to a God who does not rule. Our own Confession puts it in chapter five. God upholds, directs, disposes, and governs all creatures, actions, and things, and yet he is not the author of sin.

A Ligonier devotional on this passage frames it in a way I have never forgotten. "God's wrath is the necessary response of His holiness to sin, but His love of us is wholly unnecessary because there is nothing lovely in us." His judgment is required by who he is. His love is free. And verse thirty-three tells you which way his heart runs.

Verse thirty-nine. Why should a living man complain, a man, about the punishment of his sins? If you are still breathing in the ruins, you have received more than the sentence required.

And then verse forty, and here is the application I want you to take. Let us test and examine our ways, and return to the Lord!

Matthew Henry saw what we all do in a crisis. "We are apt, in times of public calamity, to reflect upon other people's ways, and lay blame upon them; whereas our business is to search and try our own ways. We have work enough to do at home; we must each of us say, "What have I done? What have I contributed to the public flames?" that we may each of us mend one, and then we should all be mended."

What have I contributed to the public flames? When your church or your family is troubled, your first move is to find the guilty party. Lamentations says the first move is a mirror. Ask what part of the fire you brought.

Then the poem sags again, honestly. Verse forty-four, You have wrapped yourself with a cloud so that no prayer can pass through. And the mouth comes before the eye again. The stanza for pe, which means mouth, says, All our enemies open their mouths against us. Then the stanza for ayin, which means eye. My eyes will flow without ceasing.

Verse forty-five gives us our first trip into the New Testament, because Paul himself takes it. You have made us scum and garbage among the peoples. The older versions say offscouring and refuse, the dirt you sweep off the floor. Calvin points out that the Greek word for this is the one Paul uses in First Corinthians four, when he says the apostles have become the scum of the world, the refuse of all things. Henry makes the same connection. Paul takes the shame of the ruined nation and wears it as the badge of an apostle. What Jerusalem suffered for its sin, the servants of Christ endure for his name.

Then the pit. Verses fifty-two through fifty-four. They flung me alive into the pit and cast stones on me; water closed over my head; I said, I am lost.

Calvin thinks this is a picture, not a literal dungeon, a man sealed in a pit until the pit becomes his grave. And the early church heard something more in it. Rufinus of Aquileia, writing on the Apostles' Creed around the year 400, comes to the clause, he was buried, and quotes this very line. They have cut off my life in the pit, and have laid a stone upon me. And he says, "These words of the Prophet point most plainly to His burial."

That is our second and last New Testament move, and I think it is a fair one. The righteous sufferer of Lamentations goes down to the pit with a stone over it, calls on the name of the Lord, and is heard. And in the fullness of time a righteous sufferer was laid in a tomb with a stone rolled against the door. He too was heard.

Because listen to verses fifty-five through fifty-seven. I called on your name, O Lord, from the depths of the pit; you heard my plea. You came near when I called on you; you said, Do not fear!

Do not fear. In the whole book of Lamentations, these are the only words of God that anyone quotes. God speaks once, and it is that.

Then verse fifty-eight. You have taken up my cause, O Lord; you have redeemed my life. Redeemed is ga'al, the verb of the kinsman redeemer. The poem closes by asking God to repay the enemies, and Carson is right that this is not personal spite. A God just enough to chasten his own people will deal with the cruelty of those he used to do it.

So what changed between verse eighteen and verse twenty-two? Nothing in the city. He called something to mind. Here is what I want you to do this week. Write down three times the Lord has been faithful to you. Specific ones. Dates if you can. And on the morning when the poem of your life sounds like verses one through eighteen, read them back to yourself. Memory can be trained to serve hope.

The Song of Songs, chapter eight, verses one through seven. We are at the summit of the book.

She begins with an odd wish. Oh that you were like a brother to me, who nursed at my mother's breasts! If I found you outside, I would kiss you, and none would despise me. In her world a woman could show affection to a brother in public. A lover, no. She wants to love him out loud. Henry reads it as the wish of every believer for a more open profession of Christ.

Then, for the third and last time, the refrain. His left hand is under my head, and his right hand embraces me! I adjure you, O daughters of Jerusalem, that you not stir up or awaken love until it pleases. We heard it in chapters two and three. Do not force love. Let it come in its time.

Verse five. Who is that coming up from the wilderness, leaning on her beloved? Back in chapter three almost the same question, what is that coming up from the wilderness, was asked of Solomon's royal procession with sixty warriors around it. Now the procession is two people. She is leaning on him.

Spurgeon once complained, half joking, that people always misquote this verse as leaning upon the arm of her beloved. There is no arm in the text, he said. She leans on the whole person. Not on his strength alone, but on him.

Then verses six and seven, the peak of the book.

Set me as a seal upon your heart, as a seal upon your arm. A seal, chotam, was a carved stone worn on a cord around the neck or as a ring. Judah handed his seal and cord to Tamar in Genesis thirty-eight. It was his identity, his signature. She is asking to be that to him. Worn over the heart. Never taken off.

For love is strong as death, jealousy is fierce as the grave. The grave there is Sheol. Death and Sheol always win. No one bargains with them. Love, she says, is that strong.

And jealousy is qin'ah. Do not hear it as suspicion. Ellicott says the word comes from being red with flame, and that the parallel lines show it is meant in a good sense. It is the ardor that will not share the beloved with a rival. God calls himself jealous in exactly this sense.

Augustine, preaching on the psalms, stopped over this verse. "A great saying that, brethren, Love is strong as death. The strength of charity could not be expressed in grander terms than these, Love is strong as death. For who resists death, my brethren? Consider, my brethren. Fire, waves, the sword, are resisted: we resist principalities, we resist kings; death comes alone, who resists it?"

Next line. Its flashes are flashes of fire, the very flame of the Lord. In Hebrew that last phrase is one word, shalhevetyah. Most translators take the yah at the end as a way of saying the greatest of flames. But Ellicott notes it is literally a flame of Yah, the short form of the divine name, and the only place in the whole Song where a sacred name appears. The book that never mentions God mentions him once, right here, at the hottest point of love.

Verse seven. Many waters cannot quench love, neither can floods drown it. If a man offered for love all the wealth of his house, he would be utterly despised. Love cannot be drowned and it cannot be bought. Remember that last line tomorrow, when Solomon's vineyard comes up.

So make it practical, starting with your marriage. Married love is meant to be sealed, exclusive, worn over the heart and not taken off when it is inconvenient. Ask yourself what, in your schedule or your screen or your friendships, competes with your wife for the place over your heart.

And then the deeper reading the church has always heard here. Matthew Henry, reading it of Christ and his people, puts it well. "If we desire and expect that Christ should set us as a seal on his heart, surely we cannot do less than set him as a seal on ours." He wears your name over his heart, the way the high priest wore the names of the tribes on his breastplate. Where do you wear his?

John chapter seven, verses fourteen through twenty-four.

Last time Jesus told his brothers he was not going up to the feast with them, and then went up privately. Now the Feast of Booths is half over, and verse fourteen says, About the middle of the feast Jesus went up into the temple and began teaching. The quiet arrival becomes a public classroom.

Verse fifteen. The Jews therefore marveled, saying, How is it that this man has learning, when he has never studied? Literally, how does this man know letters? He never sat at a rabbi's feet. Yet he handles the Scriptures better than the professionals.

R. C. Sproul put it in modern terms. The scholars "looked askance at Jesus, asking where His sheepskin could be found." And Sproul, who did doctoral work in the Netherlands, confessed with a smile that plenty of his classmates had been educated beyond their intelligence. Credentials are not the same thing as knowing God.

The same charge is made in Acts four about Peter and John, uneducated, common men. The teacher without credentials trained fishermen without credentials.

Verse sixteen. My teaching is not mine, but his who sent me. Augustine loved the paradox. How can it be my teaching and not mine at the same time? His answer is that Jesus means, I am not from myself. The Son speaks what he has from the Father. Calvin adds why the Father arranged things this way. He brought his Son out of a carpenter's workshop rather than the schools of the scribes, so that no one could imagine the gospel was invented on earth.

Then verse seventeen, one of the most important sentences in this Gospel. If anyone's will is to do God's will, he will know whether the teaching is from God or whether I am speaking on my own authority.

Here is the hard question. Is Jesus saying you must obey before you can know? That seems backward. Augustine answered it with a line that became famous. "For understanding is the reward of faith. Therefore do not seek to understand in order to believe, but believe that you may understand."

The problem with Jesus' hearers was not information. It was the will. A man who has already decided he will not bow will never find the evidence sufficient. A man whose heart says, Lord, whatever you want, will find the light grows as he walks. Be honest with yourself. Are your doubts mostly about the evidence, or mostly about what it would cost you if the evidence were true?

Verse eighteen gives the test for any teacher. The one who speaks on his own authority seeks his own glory. Calvin calls this the touchstone. Whose glory is the preacher after? Run every voice through that question, mine included.

Then Jesus goes on offense. Verse nineteen, none of you keeps the law. Why do you seek to kill me? The crowd says, You have a demon, but John has already told us in chapter five that the leaders were plotting his death. The one work in verse twenty-one is the healing at the pool of Bethesda, a man ill thirty-eight years, healed on a Sabbath.

So Jesus argues from their own practice. The law says circumcise a boy on the eighth day. If the eighth day falls on a Sabbath, they circumcise anyway. Their own teachers had a saying for it, which Ellicott records. Circumcision vacates the Sabbath. Fine, says Jesus. You will perform a work on one part of a man's body to keep the law, and you are angry that I made a man's whole body well?

And verse twenty-four. Do not judge by appearances, but judge with right judgment. Isaiah eleven said the Messiah would not judge by what his eyes see. Jesus is not forbidding judgment. He is forbidding a double standard. They let their own Sabbath work slide and condemned his. So let me ask you. Where do you grade yourself on intentions and everyone else on appearances?

First Peter chapter five, verses one through seven.

Yesterday it was judgment beginning at the household of God. Now Peter says, So I exhort the elders among you. If judgment begins at God's house, look first to the men who keep the house.

And look how he introduces himself. Not as the chief apostle. As a fellow elder, and a witness of the sufferings of Christ. Calvin notices that if Peter had held a primacy over the other pastors, this was the place to claim it, and he does not. Ellicott notes that witness here means not only one who saw, but one who testifies. Peter saw the sufferings, and on the night they began, he denied he knew the man. The witness was once a coward.

Verse two. Shepherd the flock of God that is among you, exercising oversight. Shepherd is poimaino, and Ellicott points out it is the very verb Jesus used to Peter by the lake in John twenty-one. Tend my sheep. Peter is passing on the commission he received at a charcoal fire, the morning after a night of failure.

Exercising oversight is episkopeo, from which we get the word bishop. Elder and overseer here are the same men. Calvin says plainly the names bishop and presbyter are synonymous. That is one reason we Presbyterians govern the church by elders, and I am glad to say our Baptist brothers read this verse the same way.

Then three contrasts, which Calvin says aim at three vices. Not under compulsion but willingly, against sloth. Not for shameful gain but eagerly, against greed. Not domineering but being examples, against the lust for power. Those are still the three ways pastors fall.

Polycarp, bishop of Smyrna, who as a young man knew the apostle John, wrote to the church at Philippi a few decades after this letter, and he sounds like Peter. "And let the presbyters be compassionate and merciful to all, bringing back those that wander, visiting all the sick, and not neglecting the widow, the orphan, or the poor." That is the job description. Not a platform. A flock.

Verse four. When the chief Shepherd appears, you will receive the unfading crown of glory. Chief Shepherd, archipoimen, appears only here in the New Testament. Pastors are under-shepherds. And unfading, amarantinos, comes from the amaranth, a flower the ancients thought never wilted.

Verse five. You who are younger, be subject to the elders. Clothe yourselves, all of you, with humility toward one another. Clothe yourselves is a rare verb, egkombosasthe. Ellicott says it describes tying on a kind of cape worn by slaves and by no others. Tie on the slave's apron. It is hard not to think of the night Jesus tied a towel around his waist and washed Peter's feet, and Peter said, You shall never wash my feet. Peter learned. Then he quotes Proverbs three. God opposes the proud but gives grace to the humble.

Verse six. Humble yourselves, therefore, under the mighty hand of God so that at the proper time he may exalt you.

Calvin has a picture for that mighty hand. "God has two hands; the one, which like a hammer beats down and breaks in pieces those who raise up themselves; and the other, which raises up the humble who willingly let down themselves, and is like a firm prop to sustain them." The same hand that breaks the proud holds up the humble. The question is never whether you will be under God's hand. It is which way you will meet it.

And verse seven. Casting all your anxieties on him, because he cares for you. Peter is echoing Psalm fifty-five. Cast your burden on the Lord, and he will sustain you.

Why does this follow humility? Because pride is what makes us keep our worries. Spurgeon preached on this verse in January 1862 and said, "the very essence of anxious care is the imagining that we are wiser than God, and the thrusting of ourselves into his place, to do for him that which we dream he either cannot or will not do." Anxiety, at bottom, is me doing God's job.

He told a story in that sermon. Picture a porter you have hired to carry your goods. He shows up with your load, but he is also staggering under a heavy piece of his own household furniture. You would tell him, I did not hire you to carry your own load. I hired you to carry mine. Put yours down. Some of us are failing at the work God gave us because we will not put down the load he never asked us to carry.

Two connections before we go, and both of them are in the text.

First, the hand. In Lamentations three, verse three, the man says, Surely against me he turns his hand again and again the whole day long. And Peter, writing to suffering churches, says, Humble yourselves under the mighty hand of God. Lamentations tells you what to do under that hand. Sit alone in silence. Put your mouth in the dust. There may yet be hope. Peter tells you how it ends. At the proper time he will exalt you. And the two books give you the same reason. Lamentations, he does not afflict from his heart. Peter, he cares for you. The hand may be heavy. The heart is for you.

Second, the water. In Lamentations three, verse fifty-four, the man in the pit says, Water closed over my head; I said, I am lost. And in the Song, the bride says, Many waters cannot quench love, neither can floods drown it. The man under the water called on the name of the Lord, and the Lord came near and said, Do not fear. The floods were real. They were not stronger than the love.

So here is your question to carry through the day. What do you need to call to mind this morning, so that you have hope?

Next time we finish three books at once. Lamentations four and five, where the poems run down to a final prayer, Restore us to yourself, O Lord. The last verses of the Song of Songs, eight, verses eight through fourteen. John seven, verses twenty-five through thirty-six, where the crowd asks whether this could be the Christ. And First Peter five, verses eight through fourteen, the roaring lion and Peter's farewell from Babylon.
