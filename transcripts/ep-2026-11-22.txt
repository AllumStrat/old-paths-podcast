Daniel chapter five, verse twenty-two. And you his son, Belshazzar, have not humbled your heart, though you knew all this.

Four words decide that chapter. Though you knew. Belshazzar was not ignorant. He had the whole story of his grandfather's madness and his grandfather's praise, and he threw a party anyway. So before we begin, a question. What do you already know that you are still living as though you did not?

This is Old Paths. Today is day twenty-two of the eleventh month in the reading plan, day two hundred seventy-two of three hundred. The readings are Daniel five and six, Job twenty, John thirteen, verses one through eleven, and Third John, a whole book in one sitting.

Last time we stood with three young men at the furnace who said, but if not. Today Daniel himself, now an old man, goes down into the lions' den. Matthew Henry set the two scenes side by side in one sentence. The three children were cast into the fiery furnace for not committing a known sin, Daniel was cast into the lions' den for not omitting a known duty. They would not do what was forbidden. He would not stop doing what was commanded. And last time Nebuchadnezzar closed his letter with the words, those who walk in pride he is able to humble. Today we meet his heir, who read that letter and learned nothing.

Daniel five and six.

First, the history. Nebuchadnezzar died in 562 B.C., and his empire lasted only about twenty-three more years. A son, a son-in-law, a boy king who was murdered, and then a man named Nabonidus, who was not of Nebuchadnezzar's line at all. A Babylonian tablet called the Nabonidus Chronicle says, year after year, that the king was in Tayma, out in the Arabian desert, while the prince and the army were in Akkad. That prince was his eldest son, Bel-shar-usur. Belshazzar.

Critics once said Daniel invented Belshazzar, because the Greek historians never named him. Then the tablets came out of the ground. That explains a detail you may have read past. Belshazzar promises the man who reads the writing the place of third ruler in the kingdom. Not second. Second was taken, by Belshazzar himself. And when Nebuchadnezzar is called his father, read ancestor. Your footnote says, or predecessor.

Now the scene. October of 539 B.C. The armies of Cyrus have already won at Opis and taken Sippar without a fight. And inside the walls Belshazzar throws a feast for a thousand lords. The Greek historian Herodotus says that when the Persians came in, people in the heart of the city were still dancing at a festival, until they learned the truth too late.

Then verse two. Warmed by the wine, the king sends for the gold and silver vessels from the temple in Jerusalem. Remember chapter one, verse two, where this whole book began. The Lord gave Jehoiakim into his hand, with some of the vessels of the house of God. Those cups have sat in a Babylonian treasury for sixty-six years. Now they go around a drinking table while the guests praise gods of gold and silver, bronze, iron, wood, and stone. It is a toast against the God of Israel.

And the answer is a hand. Not thunder, not an army. Fingers writing on the plaster, opposite the lampstand, lit by the king's own lamp. Henry asks, if that is only the finger of God, what must his arm be? The king's knees knock together before he has read a word. His conscience reads it first.

Daniel is brought in, past eighty now, and he begins with a refusal. Let your gifts be for yourself. Then he retells chapter four to Nebuchadnezzar's own heir, and ends with our verse. You have not humbled your heart, though you knew all this.

Here is the hard question in chapter five. Nebuchadnezzar got a dream, a prophet's counsel, and twelve full months to repent. Belshazzar gets one sentence and dies that night. Why no grace period?

Verse twenty-two is the answer. The grace period was the story of his grandfather. Calvin puts it sharply. Men are accustomed to shield themselves under their ignorance with the view of extenuating the guilt of their crimes, but those who sin knowingly and willfully are without the slightest excuse. Belshazzar did not lack information. He lacked humility. God is not obliged to repeat a lesson to a man who has turned it into a joke.

Then the writing. Mene, mene, tekel, parsin. On their face these Aramaic words are weights. A mina, a mina, a shekel, and half-minas. The Cambridge Bible reads them just that way. Then Daniel reads each weight as a verb. Mene, numbered. God has counted your days and finished them. Tekel, weighed. You have been weighed in the balances and found wanting. Keil hears a play on the word for light. You were too light. And peres, divided, with a pun on Paras, Persia. Your kingdom is given to the Medes and Persians.

Babylon weighed silver for the whole world. Tonight Babylon is on the scale. That very night Belshazzar was killed, and the Nabonidus Chronicle says the army of Cyrus entered Babylon without battle.

Chapter six. Darius the Mede receives the kingdom. Who he is remains debated. A Ligonier devotional takes the name as a throne name for Cyrus's general Gubaru. Either way, the exile Daniel rises to the top again.

Verse four. They could find no ground for complaint or any fault, because he was faithful. Jerome wrote on the next verse, Blessed indeed is a life so led that even enemies can find no cause for accusation, except perhaps in matter pertaining to God's law. So they write a law to make his faithfulness a crime. And they lie to do it. All the high officials are agreed, they tell the king. All of them? Daniel was one of the three. Nobody asked him.

Then verse ten. When Daniel knew that the document had been signed, he went to his house where he had windows in his upper chamber open toward Jerusalem. He got down on his knees three times a day and prayed and gave thanks before his God, as he had done previously.

Why toward Jerusalem? Because Solomon, dedicating the temple in First Kings eight, asked that if Israel were ever carried captive and prayed toward the land, the city, and the house, God would hear from heaven. Daniel is praying Solomon's prayer toward a city still in ruins.

And here is the hardest question in chapter six. Was this wise? Jesus told us to go into our room and shut the door. Daniel could have closed the shutters for thirty days and prayed just as truly. Was he daring the lions?

The voices take that seriously. Jerome is cautious. He warns against rushing into danger, and notes that Daniel prayed in his own house, not in the public square. Spurgeon, preaching on this verse in 1868, agreed it was no stunt. He thought the windows probably faced an inner courtyard, and that Daniel had no eye on publicity. But Spurgeon would not let him close them either. If Daniel had changed his habit, the weak would say he was cowed, and the wicked would say he served God only when it was safe. Then this line. He did not believe in opposing outward falsehood by an inward truth. Calvin goes further. To alter his habit at all, he says, would have been a partial denial of God, and he rebukes those who think faith can survive completely buried.

Here is how I put it together. The key words are the last ones in the verse. As he had done previously. Daniel did not open the windows that day. They were already open. He did not add a protest, and he did not subtract a habit. The law demanded a visible change, and he refused to make one.

The king hunts until sunset for a loophole, and finds none. A stone is sealed over the den. The king spends the night without food or sleep, and at dawn he runs to the den and calls, O Daniel, servant of the living God. From the dark comes the old man's voice. My God sent his angel and shut the lions' mouths. The king had the palace and no rest. Daniel had the lions and peace. When the letter to the Hebrews lists the faithful, it remembers this night in one phrase, those who stopped the mouths of lions.

So the application is about the ordinary habit. Daniel's courage on the worst day of his life was simply his schedule. He did that day what he had done on ten thousand quiet days before it. You will not invent faithfulness under pressure. You will only keep or drop what you already practice. What are your open windows? Keep them open.

Job twenty.

This is Zophar's second speech, and his last. He never speaks again. He is stung by Job's warning at the end of chapter nineteen, that there is a judgment. So he answers with one long poem with a single thesis, verse five. The exulting of the wicked is short, and the joy of the godless but for a moment.

The poem moves in three waves. First, the wicked man vanishes like a dream, and people say, where is he? Second, the most vivid picture in the chapter. Evil is sweet in his mouth. He hides it under his tongue like candy and will not let it go. But in his stomach it turns into the venom of cobras. He swallows riches and vomits them up again. Third, God's arsenal. Flee the iron weapon and a bronze arrow finds you. This is the wicked man's portion from God.

Is that false? Not as a principle. Sin really is sweet going down and bitter later. But the commentators agree on two faults. First, Zophar measures everything inside this short life, as Barnes puts it, he looks at one small slice of existence. Chapter twenty-one will answer that the wicked often live long and die rich. Second, he has stopped listening. Job has just said, I know that my Redeemer lives. And Zophar does not mention it. The Expositor's Bible says, When Job affirmed his sublime confidence in a Divine Vindicator, Zophar caught only at the idea of an avenger. John Gill even suspects that verse five is a sneer, that Job's triumph of faith is the short joy of a hypocrite.

Here is the practical wisdom. You can say true things about God and still be a liar about your friend. Before you apply a true doctrine to someone in pain, listen to what they have just told you. Zophar answered the man he had decided Job was, not the man in front of him.

John thirteen, verses one through eleven.

John's Gospel turns here. Twelve chapters of public signs are over, and five chapters in the upper room begin. And the first verse is a doorway. Having loved his own who were in the world, he loved them to the end. The Greek is eis telos. It can mean to the end of his life, or to the uttermost. Both are true. Augustine said, God forbid that He, whose death could not end, should have ended His love at death.

Then verse three, and watch the logic. Jesus, knowing that the Father had given all things into his hands, and that he had come from God and was going back to God, rose from supper and took a towel. Knowing all things are his, he kneels. His greatness is not the obstacle to the towel. It is the reason for it. R. C. Sproul points out that foot washing was so low a task that, by Jewish custom, even a Jewish slave could not be required to do it. Only a Gentile slave could. And Luke tells us the disciples had been arguing that evening about which of them was greatest.

Peter objects, you shall never wash my feet. Calvin calls that pride wearing the costume of humility. Jesus replies, if I do not wash you, you have no share with me. So Peter swings the other way. Then wash all of me.

Verse ten has two different Greek words. The one who has bathed, louo, the whole body, needs only to wash, nipto, his feet. Augustine took the bath as baptism and the feet as daily sins confessed. Calvin read the feet as ongoing renewal against the remains of the flesh. I think both are in it. You are clean once, in union with Christ, and you still need him every day for the dust of the road.

And then, not all of you are clean. Judas was in that room, and Jesus washed his feet too. Calvin observes that Jesus did not name him, so that the door of repentance stayed open.

Third John.

Yesterday John told the elect lady not to welcome false teachers into her house. Today, in the mirror letter, he thanks a man for welcoming true ones. The elder writes to the beloved Gaius, and verse four is the heartbeat. I have no greater joy than to hear that my children are walking in the truth.

Traveling missionaries, strangers to Gaius, had come through, and he housed them. Send them on their journey in a manner worthy of God, John says. For they have gone out for the sake of the name, accepting nothing from the Gentiles. The name is Jesus. They took no money from pagans, so no one could call the gospel a sales pitch. And verse eight makes Gaius their partner. Therefore we ought to support people like these, that we may be fellow workers for the truth. If you cannot go, you can still be a fellow worker. A spare room and a gift make you part of the mission.

Then Diotrephes, who likes to put himself first. That is one Greek word, philoproteuon, and it appears nowhere else in the New Testament. Notice what John does not accuse him of. No false Christ, no strange doctrine. He wants control, so he rejects John's letter, slanders the apostle, turns away the brothers, and throws out anyone who welcomes them. John MacArthur says, Diotrephes illustrates the truth that self-love is the mother of all heresies. Wanting to be first does damage enough on its own.

So John gives the simplest rule in the letter, verse eleven. Do not imitate evil but imitate good. Then he commends Demetrius, and closes, we will talk face to face.

Here is the connection, and it is about bent knees. Belshazzar would not humble his heart, and he was weighed and found wanting. Daniel bent his knees three times a day, with the windows open, and the lions' mouths were shut. Diotrephes loved to be first. And on the last night, knowing that all things had been given into his hands, the Lord of glory knelt on the floor with a towel and washed the feet of fishermen, and of his betrayer.

So carry this question through the day. Where is God asking you to kneel?
