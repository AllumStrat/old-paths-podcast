Ezekiel forty-seven, verses five and six. It was a river that I could not pass through, for the water had risen. It was deep enough to swim in. And he said to me, Son of man, have you seen this?

A trickle under a doorway. No streams flowing into it. And about a mile downhill it is over a grown man's head.

This is Old Paths. Today is day eighteen of the eleventh month in the reading plan, day two hundred sixty-eight of three hundred. The readings are Ezekiel forty-five through forty-seven, Job sixteen, John twelve, verses one through eleven, and First John four, verses seven through twenty-one.

Last time the glory came home, and God said of his house, this is the place of my throne. I also promised that we would meet the prince properly, the nasi, the man who alone may sit in the shut east gate. Today we meet him. And then we watch what comes out of a house where God lives. In John, we left the council plotting and the pilgrims asking whether Jesus would come to the feast. Today he comes as far as Bethany, to a dinner.

Ezekiel forty-five through forty-seven.

We are still inside the vision dated in chapter forty, about 573 B.C., the twenty-fifth year of the exile. Nebuchadnezzar rules Babylon, and Jerusalem has been rubble for fourteen years. The glory has returned to the house. Now God orders everything around it, the land, the rulers, the calendar, and then he opens a spring.

Chapter forty-five begins with a map. A holy district sits at the center for the sanctuary, the priests, and the Levites. Next to it is a strip of land for the city that belongs to the whole house of Israel. And on both sides lies the prince's land. Verse eight gives the reason. It is to be his property in Israel. And my princes shall no more oppress my people.

Remember Ahab, who wanted Naboth's vineyard and took it by murder. So God gives the prince his own land first, so he has no excuse to take anyone else's.

Then verse nine, and the voice gets sharp. Thus says the Lord God: Enough, O princes of Israel! Put away violence and oppression, and execute justice and righteousness. Cease your evictions of my people. Ellicott notes that the word translated evictions literally means expulsions. Driving families off their land. Then come honest scales, an honest ephah for grain, an honest bath for oil.

From there the prince leads worship, supplying the sacrifices for the feasts, the new moons, and the Sabbaths.

Matthew Henry has a line on this verse. Even princes and great men that have long done amiss must at length think it time, high time, to reform and amend; for no prescription will justify a wrong. Prescription is an old legal word for a right claimed by long custom. Years of habit never turn a wrong into a right.

And now the hard question. Who is this prince? Look at verse twenty-two of chapter forty-five. At Passover the prince shall provide for himself and all the people of the land a young bull for a sin offering. For himself. And in chapter forty-six he has sons, and he passes land to them.

John Gill says plainly that Christ is this prince. But a sin offering for himself is hard to fit to Jesus, and the older Jamieson, Fausset, and Brown commentary says the mention of the prince's sons is another argument against reading him as the Messiah. Matthew Poole says simply that he offers it to expiate his own sins. Patrick Fairbairn argues that the prince is neither a committee of rulers nor simply the Messiah. He is the royal office as it ought to be, drawn in the shapes of the old covenant. And notice that God keeps the throne for himself. The earthly ruler only gets the title prince.

Here is where I land. Ezekiel is showing you the best king Israel could imagine. He has his own land, so he does not steal. He keeps honest scales. He leads the people in worship. And even this good king has to bring a bull for his own sin. The vision is honest about every earthly ruler, even the best of them. It leaves an empty place that only a King who needs no sin offering can fill.

Then chapter forty-seven. The guide brings Ezekiel back to the temple door, and water is coming out from under the threshold. A Ligonier devotional notes that the Hebrew pictures a trickle, not a gush. Jerusalem never had a river. It had the little spring of Gihon and the waters of Shiloah that, Isaiah says, go gently. Now a stream comes from the place of God's throne.

The man with the measuring line walks east. A thousand cubits, about a quarter of a mile, and it is ankle deep. Another thousand, knee deep. Another thousand, waist deep. Another thousand, and it is a river that I could not pass through. Keil points out that there are no tributaries. A natural river cannot grow like that. All of it comes from the source.

Then the guide asks, Son of man, have you seen this? Do not walk past it.

The river runs down into the Arabah and into the Dead Sea, the saltiest water Ezekiel's hearers knew, where nothing lives. And the sea is healed. Fishermen spread nets from En-gedi, which means the fountain of the goat, a spring on the western shore. In verse nine the Hebrew word for river is in the dual form, nachalayim, literally two rivers. Keil and others take it as a way of saying a double river, a river of great force. Trees line the banks, with fruit every month and leaves for healing.

Here I will make my two New Testament moves, and both are earned, because the New Testament reaches back to this chapter itself. On the last day of the Feast of Booths, Jesus stood up and cried out, Whoever believes in me, as the Scripture has said, out of his heart will flow rivers of living water. John adds that he said this about the Spirit. And in Revelation twenty-two, John sees the river of the water of life flowing from the throne of God and of the Lamb, with the tree on either side, its leaves for the healing of the nations. Ezekiel's river has a source, and the New Testament names it.

But look at verse eleven. Its swamps and marshes will not become fresh; they are to be left for salt. Even this river leaves some places unhealed. Ellicott says the world will always have its unhealed places, because a man is still free to hear or to refuse. Matthew Henry names two kinds of marsh. One is so settled in the mire of its own sin that it will not be healed. The other is so pleased with its own righteousness that it thinks it needs no healing. Both end up as salt flats. The water runs right past them.

Matthew Henry also says it has often been said that Scripture is like these waters, shallow enough for a lamb and deep enough for an elephant. The saying goes back to Gregory the Great, writing to his friend Leander of Seville around 595. It is, as it were, a kind of river, if I may so liken it, which is both shallow and deep, wherein both the lamb may find a footing, and the elephant float at large.

Here is the application. Spurgeon preached on four words, waters to swim in, on a Thursday evening in April 1872. He said the gospel at first is a rivulet to us, then ankle deep, then knee deep, and God means it to become a river you swim in. Then he said this about Christ. We are never straitened in him, if stinted at all, we stint ourselves. Straitened means hemmed in or kept short. The water is not short.

So be honest. How long have you been standing ankle deep? Same verses, same prayers, same small idea of grace, year after year. The river is not the problem. Walk in.

Job sixteen.

Eliphaz finished his lecture on the wicked. Now Job answers, and the chapter moves in three steps. First, to the friends. I have heard many such things; miserable comforters are you all. Eliphaz had accused Job of windy knowledge. Job throws it back at him. Shall windy words have an end? Then the sting in verse four. I also could speak as you do, if you were in my place. Matthew Henry says a sufferer is in a sad state when his medicine turns out to be poison and his doctors are his worst disease.

Second, Job describes God himself as his attacker. He has torn me in his wrath. He seized me by the neck. He set me up as his target; his archers surround me. He runs upon me like a warrior. This is as raw as anything in the book.

Then the third step, verse nineteen, and it is stunning. Even now, behold, my witness is in heaven, and he who testifies for me is on high. The Cambridge Bible notes that the second word is Aramaic, sahadi, the same root as Jegar-sahadutha, the heap of witness Laban raised in Genesis thirty-one. It means more than a bystander. It means an advocate, someone who will testify for him and see that justice is done.

But who is in heaven? The same God Job just called his attacker. Keil faces that head on. It is an inward irresistible demand made by his faith which here brings together two opposite principles, principles which the understanding cannot unite, with bewildering boldness. Job appeals to God against God. And in verse twenty-one he asks that someone would argue the case of a man with God, as a man does for his neighbor.

Hold on to that verse. In three chapters Job will say, I know that my Redeemer lives.

John twelve, verses one through eleven.

Six days before Passover, Jesus comes back to Bethany, less than two miles from the city that wants him dead. They give a dinner. Martha serves. And Lazarus is reclining at the table. Chrysostom says that this was proof the resurrection was real. A ghost does not eat dinner.

Then Mary. Last time we saw her in John, she fell at his feet. Here she is at his feet again. She breaks open a pound of pure nard. Judas values it at three hundred denarii, and a Ligonier devotional notes that this was a year's wages. She anoints his feet and wipes them with her hair, and the house is filled with the fragrance.

Judas objects, and it sounds so responsible. Why not sell it and give to the poor? John tells us why he really said it. He was a thief, and he helped himself from the moneybag. Augustine says that the fragrance which delighted everyone else killed Judas. Alas for you, wretched man! The sweet savor has slain you.

Jesus says, Leave her alone, so that she may keep it for the day of my burial. She is anointing a body for the grave while he is still sitting at the table.

Now here is a real disagreement. Calvin insists that this was a one-time act for Christ's burial, and not a rule for costly worship. He says people who imitate it with expensive ceremony are apes rather than followers. Sproul, preaching on this text, presses the other way. There are times for extravagance, and it is always time for an extravagant manifestation of our adoration of Christ.

I think they are guarding different gates. Calvin is guarding against expensive religious show. Sproul is guarding against the cold arithmetic of Judas, who never gave Jesus anything he could not account for. What made Mary's gift extravagant was not the price. It was a whole heart, poured out on the person.

Then verse ten. The chief priests plan to kill Lazarus too, because people are believing because of him. Chrysostom asks the obvious question. Is it a crime to have received a kindness?

First John four, verses seven through twenty-one.

Last time John told us to test the spirits. Now he says, Beloved, let us love one another, for love is from God. Then the famous words. God is love.

Sproul warns that this is not an equation you can turn around to say love is God. And when people tell him, my God is a God of love, he asks where they got that idea. From a cancer ward? They got it from Scripture, the same Scripture that says God is holy and just. And John defines this love immediately, in verse ten. In this is love, not that we have loved God but that he loved us and sent his Son to be the propitiation for our sins. The word is hilasmos, the sacrifice that turns away God's wrath. God's love does not ignore his justice. It pays what justice requires.

Then verse eighteen, and another question. There is no fear in love, but perfect love casts out fear. Whose love? Augustine says our love, growing. He compares fear to the bristle used in sewing to draw the thread. Fear goes in first to make a way, then the thread of love follows and the bristle comes out. Calvin reads it as God's love, known by faith. He admits fear never completely leaves us in this life. But when we flee to God as to a quiet harbor, safe and free from all danger of shipwreck and of tempests, fear is really expelled, for it gives way to faith.

I lean toward Calvin, because verse sixteen speaks of the love that God has for us and verse seventeen of confidence on the day of judgment. But Augustine describes what that confidence does in a life. Then verse nineteen joins them. We love because he first loved us. And verse twenty does not let you hide. A man who says he loves God and hates his brother is a liar.

Here is the connection today, and it is a real one. Each reading has something flowing out from a source. A river runs out from under the threshold of God's house. In Bethany, a fragrance fills the house. In Job, tears pour out to a God he cannot yet see clearly. And John says love is from God. Calvin, on verse eight, calls God the fountain of love, and says its effect flows out wherever God is truly known. The same God who healed the Dead Sea in Ezekiel's vision pours his love into his people, and it does not stop with them.

So carry this question through the day. Where are you still standing ankle deep, when God has given you waters to swim in?
