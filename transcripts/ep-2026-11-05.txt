Ezekiel twenty-two, verse thirty. And I sought for a man among them who should build up the wall and stand in the breach before me for the land, that I should not destroy it, but I found none.

I found none.

Now hold that next to a simple fact. When God said those words, Jeremiah was standing in Jerusalem. He had been preaching there for more than thirty years. He wept for that city. He prayed for it until God told him to stop.

So what does God mean, I found none? Was Jeremiah not enough? Was nobody enough?

Keep that question. We will answer it.

I also promised you two things. First, that Ezekiel twenty-three would retell the story of chapter sixteen, the foundling who became a harlot, which we read back in episode eighteen. Ezekiel twenty-two and twenty-three.

Neither chapter carries a date. The last date was chapter twenty, verse one, the summer of 591 B.C. The next is chapter twenty-four, verse one, January of 588 B.C., when Nebuchadnezzar's army closes around Jerusalem. These oracles fall in between. Zedekiah is on the throne. He has sworn loyalty to Babylon and is quietly sending messages to Egypt. Ezekiel is in Tel-abib, still mute except when God opens his mouth. And the glory of God, which left the temple in chapter eleven, is still gone.

Chapter twenty-one gave us the sword. Chapter twenty-two, as Keil says, describes the sins that brought it. It has three oracles, each opening with, the word of the Lord came to me.

The first is verses one through sixteen, and it begins with a title. Verse two. Will you judge the bloody city? In Hebrew that is ir haddamim, literally, the city of bloods. The plural means bloodshed, again and again. And here is what makes it sting. The prophet Nahum had already used that exact title for another city. Woe to the bloody city, all full of lies and plunder. That city was Nineveh, the capital of Assyria. Ezekiel takes the name of Israel's most hated enemy and hangs it over the gate of Jerusalem.

Then comes the charge sheet. Princes shedding blood. Parents treated with contempt. The sojourner, the fatherless, and the widow, the three people the law told Israel to protect, are the three Jerusalem oppresses. Slander, incest, bribes, interest charged to the poor. Keil notices that the phrase to shed blood comes back three times, in verses six, nine, and twelve, and it divides the list into three groups. Slander sheds blood. Bribes shed blood. Ezekiel wants you to see that sins we call small, sins of the tongue and the purse, end up with bodies in the street.

And at the bottom of the list, verse twelve. But me you have forgotten, declares the Lord God. Every other sin on the list grows out of that one. Matthew Henry says sinners provoke God because they forget him.

The second oracle is short, verses seventeen through twenty-two, and it is a picture from the refinery. Son of man, the house of Israel has become dross to me. The Hebrew word is sig, the scum that floats to the top when you melt silver. Then come the cheap metals. Bronze and tin and iron and lead. God says, I will gather you into the midst of Jerusalem, as one gathers metal into a furnace, and I will blow the fire on it.

When the Babylonians came, people from all over Judah would flee into Jerusalem, the safest place in the country. God says, your refuge is my crucible. Henry has a sharp way of putting it. They gathered into Jerusalem as a place of defense, and God gathered them there as a place of execution.

The old commentators did not agree about those four metals. Henry thought brass meant brazen faces that cannot blush. Gregory the Great, in his book on pastoral care around the year 590, read them better, I think, as people under affliction. God says in effect, "I would have purified them by the fire of tribulation, and I sought that they should become silver or gold; but they have been turned before me in the furnace into brass, tin, iron, and lead, because even in tribulation they have broken forth, not to virtue but to vices. For indeed brass, when it is struck, returns a sound more than all other metals. He, therefore, who, when subjected to strokes, breaks out into a sound of murmuring is turned into brass in the midst of the furnace." Tin, he says, looks like silver, so tin is the man who keeps pretending even in trouble. Iron is the one who still plots against his neighbor. And lead is the heaviest metal, the man whose desires never rise off the ground, no matter how hot it gets.

That is a searching question for anyone in a hard season. When you are struck, what sound do you make?

Is this furnace meant to purify or to destroy? Malachi pictures God as one who will sit as a refiner and purifier of silver. Ezekiel does not say the refiner sits by this furnace. Henry draws the contrast. "When God brings his own people into the furnace he sits by them, as the refiner by his gold, to see that they be not continued there any longer than is fitting and needful; but he will bring these people into the furnace, as men throw dross into it, which they design shall be consumed, and therefore are in no care about it, but leave it there."

For the nation as a whole, this furnace is judgment. For a remnant, God will use the same fire to refine. Verse fifteen already promised that. I will consume your uncleanness out of you.

The third oracle, verses twenty-three through thirty-one, goes class by class. Prophets like a roaring lion. Priests who make no distinction between the holy and the common. Princes like wolves. And the people of the land, robbing like everyone above them. Verse twenty-eight should sound familiar. Her prophets have smeared whitewash for them. That is the flimsy wall from chapter thirteen, from episode seventeen. The false prophets are still painting over cracks.

Which brings us to verse thirty, and our question.

Two Hebrew words. To build up the wall is gader, a stone wall. The breach is perets, a break in it. Ezekiel used both in chapter thirteen, when he told the false prophets, you have not gone up into the breaches. Picture a city wall with a hole smashed through it, and somebody standing in the gap with his body. But the enemy pouring through this breach is not Babylon. Look at the verse again. Stand in the breach before me. God himself is the one coming through.

Who can stand there? Psalm one hundred six says Moses did. Keil hears Abraham in the background, pleading for Sodom. For the sake of ten, I will not destroy it.

So what about Jeremiah? The commentators give three answers, and they fit together.

John Gill says there were godly people in the land, Jeremiah, Baruch, and others, but not among the classes God just named. The prophets, priests, princes, and people had produced nobody.

Ellicott says God does not mean there was not one godly man. He means there was no one of such strength and standing that his prayers could turn back the judgment.

And the third answer comes from Jeremiah's own book. God had told him three times to stop praying for this people. Jeremiah fifteen, verse one, is the hardest. Though Moses and Samuel stood before me, yet my heart would not turn toward this people. And Ezekiel himself said something like it in chapter fourteen. Even if Noah, Daniel, and Job were in the land, they would save only themselves. The door for that kind of intercession had been closed.

D. A. Carson wrote that his own call to the ministry was bound up with this verse. And he answers our question directly. "Of course, God had Jeremiah in Jerusalem and Ezekiel among the exiles. But these men were to declare God’s word in declining times. Theirs was not the task of rebuilding the wall, of standing in the gap, of averting the wrath of God."

That is the honest answer. Jeremiah was faithful. He was not enough. No sinner can stand in that breach for a city of sinners. And Isaiah had already said what God would do about it. Isaiah fifty-nine, verse sixteen. He saw that there was no man, and wondered that there was no one to intercede; then his own arm brought him salvation.

God searched for a man and found none. So God supplied the man. Carson's next line says that, in the end, only one Mediator would do. That is Paul in First Timothy two. There is one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus. The breach Ezekiel saw stayed open in 586 B.C. It was closed, finally, by a man who stood in it and took the full force of what was coming through.

Now chapter twenty-three. Two sisters.

Their names are Oholah and Oholibah. Both come from the Hebrew word ohel, tent, the word used for the tabernacle. Oholah means her tent. Oholibah means my tent is in her. Oholah is Samaria, the northern kingdom. Oholibah is Jerusalem. Keil explains why those names matter. "The “tent” is the sanctuary: both tabernacle and temple. These names characterize the two kingdoms according to their attitude toward the Lord. Jerusalem had the sanctuary of Jehovah; Samaria, on the other hand, had her own sanctuary". One she invented herself. Matthew Poole agrees: after the northern tribes broke away, all their worship was of their own making. Remember Jeroboam's golden calves at Dan and Bethel.

So the names carry the whole tragedy. Samaria had a religion she made up. Jerusalem had the real thing, God's own tent in her midst. And she did worse.

How does this differ from chapter sixteen? Keil notes that sixteen told the whole history, past, present, and future. Twenty-three keeps its eyes on guilt and punishment, and the lovers are named. They are nations. This is foreign policy described as adultery.

Verses five through ten. Oholah lusted after the Assyrians, all of them desirable young men, horsemen riding on horses. The kings of Samaria paid tribute and took Assyria's gods along with its protection. And in 722 B.C. the lover became the executioner. Samaria fell to Assyria and the ten tribes were carried away.

Then verse eleven. Her sister Oholibah saw this, and she became more corrupt than her sister. She watched the northern kingdom deported and drew no lesson from it. Ahaz bought Assyria's help and copied the altar he saw in Damascus. Manasseh filled Jerusalem with idols. Henry puts his finger on it. "Those may justly expect God's judgments upon themselves who do not take warning by his judgments upon others, who see in others what is the end of sin and yet continue to make a light matter of it. But it is bad indeed with those who are made worse by that which should make them better".

Verse fourteen is one of the most vivid details in the book. She saw men portrayed on the wall, the images of the Chaldeans portrayed in vermilion, with belts at their waists and flowing turbans on their heads. Judah fell in love with a picture. Keil says the description fits the carved walls of the Assyrian palaces exactly, and we can still see them. In the 1840s Austen Henry Layard dug up Sennacherib's palace at Nineveh, and its panels of the siege of Lachish in Judah, with Judean families walking into exile, are in the British Museum now. Kings covered their walls with their own power, and it worked. Judah looked and wanted to be on their side.

That is how it often starts. Not with a decision but with a look. Verse sixteen. She sent messengers to them in Chaldea. And then, verse seventeen, after she was defiled by them, she turned from them in disgust, and ran back to Egypt, the lover of her youth.

And so verse twenty-two. Behold, I will stir up against you your lovers from whom you turned in disgust. The ones she courted become the army at her gates. Henry adds a dry observation about human nature. Sinful love, he says, very often turns into hatred.

The language of this chapter is graphic, more than chapter sixteen. Carson does not apologize for it. "It is shocking, forceful, and, in some ways, quite horrible. It is meant to be horrible." His point is that God wants us to feel what he feels. You would not shrug if you found out your spouse had been unfaithful for years. So, Carson says, you cannot flirt with sin and tell yourself that God is big enough to absorb it. That is not how he sees it. And to the charge that it demeans women, he answers that Ezekiel is addressing the whole nation, men and women alike, and no metaphor walks on all fours.

Then, at verse thirty-one, the image changes. You have gone the way of your sister; therefore I will give her cup into your hand. And verse thirty-four. You shall drink it and drain it out, and gnaw its shards. So bitter you chew the broken pottery afterward. The cup of God's wrath runs through the prophets, Jeremiah twenty-five, Isaiah fifty-one. So when Jesus fell on his face in Gethsemane and prayed, My Father, if it be possible, let this cup pass from me, he was not talking about fear in general. He was looking at Oholibah's cup. He drank it so that his people would not have to drain it.

One more detail before we leave. Verses thirty-eight and thirty-nine. When they had slaughtered their children in sacrifice to their idols, on the same day they came into my sanctuary. In the morning, a child given to Molech. In the afternoon, the temple courts. The tent was still standing, and they treated it as one more shrine on the circuit.

So here is the application, and it comes straight out of verse eleven. Oholibah saw. She watched her sister go into exile and decided it would not happen to her. Most of us have watched somebody wreck a life. A marriage that slowly died. A friend who drifted from church until he was gone. We shook our heads. Did we learn anything? Ask yourself honestly. What have you watched destroy someone else that you are now walking toward yourself? Henry's warning is not for Jerusalem only. It is bad indeed to be made worse by what should have made you better.

Job chapter eight.

Bildad the Shuhite now speaks. Eliphaz was the polished one, who began by asking permission to speak. Bildad starts with an insult. How long will you say these things, and the words of your mouth be a great wind? Matthew Henry, passing on a suggestion from Bishop Patrick, thinks the name Shuhite may come from Shuah, one of Abraham's sons by Keturah. If so, Bildad is a man of tradition, and that is exactly how he argues.

The speech has four parts. Verses two through seven, God's justice and Job's children. Verses eight through ten, an appeal to the fathers. Verses eleven through nineteen, three pictures from nature. And verses twenty through twenty-two, a closing promise.

Verse three is Bildad's whole theology in one line. Does God pervert justice? Or does the Almighty pervert the right? The answer is no, of course not. Job has never said otherwise. But watch what Bildad builds on it. Verse four. If your children have sinned against him, he has delivered them into the hand of their transgression.

He is saying this to a father who buried ten children at once. They must have had it coming. Henry asks the right question: could Bildad not be an advocate for God without becoming an accuser of the brethren? And then he gives one of the most useful sentences in his whole commentary on Job. He says that "extraordinary afflictions are not always the punishment of extraordinary sins, but sometimes the trial of extraordinary graces". Bildad had only one category. Job's suffering fits in the other one.

Verses eight through ten are the appeal to tradition. Inquire, please, of bygone ages. For we are but of yesterday and know nothing. Respect for earlier generations is good. But tradition is only as good as its application, and Bildad's tradition cannot account for a righteous man in agony.

Then come the three pictures. First, verse eleven. Can papyrus grow where there is no marsh? The Hebrew word, gome, is the papyrus reed of the Nile. Outside this verse it shows up only in connection with Egypt, including the basket of papyrus that held baby Moses. Papyrus shoots up fast and green, but cut off the water and it withers before any other plant. Such are the paths of all who forget God. Second, verse fourteen. His trust is a spider's web, literally a spider's house. He leans against it and it does not hold. Third, a lush plant spreading over a garden and wrapping its roots around the stones, ripped out so completely that the ground itself says, I have never seen you.

All three pictures are true. Gregory the Great, in his huge commentary on Job, puts it perfectly. "Bildad subjoins words of wondrous truth against hypocrites, but he is running himself through with the point of his discourse." Subjoins just means adds. And Gregory goes on to say that Bildad's words should have been spoken to fools, not to a wise man, to the wicked, not to a good one. Preaching them to Job, he says, is like pouring water into the river while the gardens dry up.

The truth was right. The man was wrong. And that is a practical warning for anybody who gives counsel. You can say true things about God and still be cruel, because you said them to the wrong person at the wrong time.

But the text still speaks to us, whatever Bildad meant by it. Spurgeon preached on the papyrus in 1865 in a sermon called A Sermon from a Rush, and he turned it into a call to examine yourself. The hypocrite, he said, grows up quickly and easily, with no inner struggle at all. And then this: "It is an ill sign, dear friend, if you never have to search your heart with deep anxiety lest you should be deceived. To have such strong faith that you never waver is one thing, but to be filled with such strong presumption that you never examine yourself is quite another."

Last, look at verse seven. Though your beginning was small, your latter days will be very great. Bildad meant it as a test Job would fail. Spurgeon preached on that verse too, in 1860, and he noticed that Bildad had prophesied without knowing it. Chapter forty-two. The Lord blessed the latter days of Job more than his beginning. Henry says God took Bildad at his word, and proved his servant Job an honest man by Bildad's own argument.

Hold on to that for the close.

John nine, verses one through twelve.

Chapter eight ended with stones in their hands and Jesus slipping out of the temple. Chapter nine begins, as he passed by. Chrysostom notices that it is Jesus who sees the blind man, not the other way around, and looks at him so intently that the disciples notice.

Then the disciples ask a question that could have come straight out of Bildad's mouth. Rabbi, who sinned, this man or his parents, that he was born blind?

R. C. Sproul wrote about this text for Ligonier, and he asks the obvious thing. "Had they not read the book of Job, which deals with a man who was innocent and yet was severely afflicted by God?" Sproul says the disciples made the same error as the friends. They took a general truth, that suffering entered the world through sin, and made it particular. And they set it up as a false choice. Either him or his parents.

Chrysostom adds something you will appreciate after the last few weeks in Ezekiel. To answer the idea that the child is punished for the parents' sin, he goes to Ezekiel eighteen, the sour grapes. The fathers have eaten sour grapes, and the children's teeth are set on edge. That proverb shall not be used. We spent a whole episode on that chapter, and here is a fourth-century preacher using it to explain John nine.

Jesus' answer, verse three. It was not that this man sinned, or his parents, but that the works of God might be displayed in him. Augustine and Chrysostom both point out that Jesus is not saying the man and his parents were sinless. He is saying their sin is not the explanation for this blindness. The real question is not where it came from but what God is going to do with it. Sproul draws the pastoral line. If there was another reason in this man's case, there may always be another reason in ours.

Spurgeon preached on this verse in 1889, and he opened with a warning that goes straight back to Bildad. "And it is very unkind, ungenerous and almost inhuman, to sit down, like the friends of Job, and suppose that because Job is greatly afflicted, he must therefore be greatly sinful." Some afflictions, he said, are not punishments at all. They are sent to refine, and to dig out more room in the heart for Christ.

Verse five. As long as I am in the world, I am the light of the world. In chapter eight he said it and they picked up stones. In chapter nine he proves it on a man who has never seen light.

And now the method, verse six. He spit on the ground and made mud and anointed the man's eyes. Why mud? Chrysostom has an answer I love. "For since they had heard that God made man, taking the dust of the earth, so also Christ made clay." In Genesis two, God formed the man from the dust of the ground. Here the same hand takes the same dust and finishes the job. The man was born with something missing, and his Maker supplies it.

Then, go, wash in the pool of Siloam, which means Sent. John translates the name because Jesus is the Sent One, over and over in this Gospel. Siloam was the pool fed by Hezekiah's tunnel. Second Kings twenty remembers how he made the pool and the conduit and brought water into the city. In 2004, workers repairing a sewer line in Jerusalem uncovered the steps of the pool as it was in Jesus' day. And you may remember from our time in John seven that during the Feast of Booths, a priest drew water from this very pool each morning and poured it out at the altar. That was the ceremony behind Jesus' cry, if anyone thirsts, let him come to me. Now the blind man walks down to that same pool, washes, and comes back seeing.

The neighbors cannot agree whether it is even the same man. He keeps saying, I am the man. Where is Jesus? I do not know. He has been healed by someone he has never seen.

J. C. Ryle draws a lesson from the spit and the mud that I want you to keep. Jesus healed people in different ways. A word, a touch, mud. So, Ryle says, be careful about measuring other people's experience of Christ by your own. The question is not whether your friend came to Christ the same way you did. The question is whether he can see.

First John, chapter one, verses one through four.

Today we start a new book, so here is a brief introduction. The letter does not name its author, but the church has always received it as written by John the apostle, the son of Zebedee, the same John who wrote the Gospel. Ellicott counts thirty-five passages that the Gospel and this letter share. The tradition places him at Ephesus late in the first century. Irenaeus, who as a young man heard Polycarp, who had known John, says the church at Ephesus had John living among them until the reign of Trajan, which began in the year 98. So this is an old man writing, probably the last of the apostles still alive.

Why did he write? Because false teachers had left the church and were unsettling those who stayed. They had what scholars call a docetic tendency, from the Greek word for seeming. They taught that the Son of God only seemed to have a real body. One teacher from that time, Cerinthus, said that the divine Christ came down on the man Jesus at his baptism and left him before the cross. Irenaeus tells a story about John and Cerinthus that he heard from Polycarp's circle. John went to the public baths at Ephesus, saw Cerinthus inside, and ran out without bathing, shouting, "Let us fly, lest even the bath-house fall down, because Cerinthus, the enemy of the truth, is within."

You may smile, but it shows how seriously John took the question of whether God really came in the flesh. That is where this letter starts.

In Greek, verses one through three are one long sentence. John piles up the objects before he even reaches his main verb. That which was from the beginning, which we have heard, which we have seen with our eyes, which we looked upon and have touched with our hands, concerning the word of life. Then a parenthesis in verse two, the life was made manifest, and finally, in verse three, the main verb. That which we have seen and heard we proclaim also to you.

Hear the opening of John's Gospel in it. In the beginning was the Word. In him was life. The Gospel starts in eternity and moves down to the flesh. The letter starts with the flesh and points back to eternity.

Notice the progression of the verbs. Heard. Seen. Looked upon, which means to gaze at, to study. And touched. The Greek word is the one Jesus used after his resurrection. Touch me, and see. A spirit does not have flesh and bones as you see that I have. John is answering Cerinthus with his own hands.

Augustine preached on this letter, and he went straight to that verb. Who could handle the Word with his hands, he asks, unless the Word had been made flesh and dwelt among us? Then he thinks of Thomas putting his fingers in the wounds, and he says this: "Because he touched the Man, he confessed the God."

Augustine adds that the Greek word behind we testify gave us the word martyr. For John's generation, to testify often meant to die.

Then come the two purposes. First, verse three, so that you too may have fellowship with us. The word is koinonia, a shared life, a partnership. And John immediately lifts it higher. Indeed our fellowship is with the Father and with his Son Jesus Christ. You and I did not see and touch. But through the apostles' testimony we are brought into the same fellowship they had. Spurgeon noticed the tense. Not was, but is. John had lost the sight of his Lord, but not his fellowship with him.

And second, verse four. We are writing these things so that our joy may be complete. An apostle's joy is not complete until you share it. Before John gives a single test of whether you know God, he tells you who God has shown himself to be. A Word you could touch.

Two connections today, and both are in the text.

The first is the question. Bildad says, your children must have sinned. The disciples ask, who sinned, this man or his parents? It is the same theology, and Sproul drew the line from one to the other. Bildad's God runs on a formula. Suffering in, sin out. In John nine, Jesus answers Bildad. Not this man, not his parents, but that the works of God might be displayed in him. That does not explain every sorrow. It forbids us to explain them all Bildad's way.

The second is the man in the breach. In Ezekiel twenty-two, God searches Jerusalem for a man to stand in the gap and finds none. But back in chapter fourteen, Ezekiel named the kind of man who might have stood there. Noah, Daniel, and Job. And at the end of Job's book, God says to Bildad and his friends, my servant Job shall pray for you, for I will accept his prayer. The man Bildad accused becomes the man who stands between Bildad and the wrath of God. Job is only a shadow of the true intercessor. But it is a beautiful shadow.

Here is your question to carry today. When someone you know is suffering, are you more like Bildad, explaining it, or like Job at the end, praying for the very people who hurt you?
