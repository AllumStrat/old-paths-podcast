This is Old Paths. Today is day ten of the eleventh month in the reading plan, day two hundred sixty of three hundred. The readings are Ezekiel chapters twenty-seven and twenty-eight, Job chapter nine, verses twenty-one through thirty-five, John chapter nine, verses twenty-six through forty-one, and First John chapter two, verses one through eleven.

Last time we met Tyre, the Phoenician trading city on its rock in the sea, saying Aha over the ruins of Jerusalem. I shall be replenished. She just counted the profit. Today Ezekiel sings her funeral song, first over the city, pictured as a great ship, then over her king. And we will face one of the most debated passages in the prophets: the king in Eden, the guardian cherub, cast down from the mountain of God. Is that a man, or the devil?

I also made two promises last time. In Job, the cry for an arbiter, someone to stand in the case between Job and God. And in First John, the reason God is just to forgive. Both come today. And in John, the man born blind is about to lose his place in the synagogue, and find something far better.

Ezekiel twenty-seven.

First, where are we? The Tyre oracles began in chapter twenty-six with a date, the eleventh year, the year Jerusalem fell, 587 or 586 B.C. Chapters twenty-seven and twenty-eight belong to the same cycle. The siege began in chapter twenty-four, and the news of the city's fall will not reach Ezekiel until chapter thirty-three. In between, God turns to the nations, and Tyre gets more space than any of them except Egypt.

Verse two. Now you, son of man, raise a lamentation over Tyre. The word is qinah, a funeral song. But Tyre is not dead. She is at the height of her power. Matthew Henry points out that there was not the least sign of decline, and yet the prophet must lament, because her prosperity was her snare. Ezekiel is singing at the funeral of a city that is still throwing parties.

Then verse three. O Tyre, you have said, I am perfect in beauty.

That phrase belongs to Zion. Psalm fifty, verse two. Out of Zion, the perfection of beauty, God shines forth. And in Lamentations two, passersby mock the ruined city: Is this the city that was called the perfection of beauty? John Gill puts it sharply. Jerusalem is destroyed, and Tyre assumes her character. The holy city lies in ashes, and the commercial city puts on her crown.

Then the poem turns Tyre into a ship. Planks of fir from Mount Hermon. A mast of cedar from Lebanon. Oars of oak from Bashan. A deck of Cyprus pine inlaid with ivory. A sail of embroidered Egyptian linen. Rowers from Sidon and Arvad, caulkers from Byblos, hired soldiers from Persia, Lud, and Put. Every nation contributed something. She was a floating world.

Then come verses twelve through twenty-five, the longest list of trading partners anywhere in Scripture. Tarshish in far-off Spain brings silver, iron, tin, and lead. The Greeks, with Tubal and Meshech, bring bronze and, verse thirteen, human beings. Tyre traded in slaves; Joel accuses Tyre and Sidon of selling the people of Judah to the Greeks. Then horses, ivory, coral, spices, gold. And in verse seventeen, Judah herself, selling wheat, honey, oil, and balm.

The Pulpit Commentary compares it to Homer's catalogue of ships. It is meant to dazzle. Then Matthew Henry notices what is missing. In a funeral song you praise the dead, and the prophet praises everything Tyre had. But Henry says he has nothing to say of her religion, her piety, or her charity, nothing about her being a refuge to the distressed. Only that she lived great and traded great. That is the eulogy. She was very, very good at business.

Want to see one of these ships? In 1997 a United States Navy research submarine found two small merchant ships on the floor of the Mediterranean off Ashkelon, more than four hundred meters down. In 1999 Robert Ballard, the man who found the Titanic, went back with the archaeologist Lawrence Stager. They were Phoenician ships from the eighth century B.C., sitting upright with nearly four hundred clay jars still in each hold. Traces in the jars pointed to wine. Ships from Tyre's own world, sunk with their cargo, sitting in the dark for almost three thousand years. That is the picture Ezekiel paints next.

Verse twenty-six. Your rowers have brought you out into the high seas. The east wind has wrecked you in the heart of the seas.

Watch that phrase, the heart of the seas. In Hebrew, belev yammim. It is the hinge of these two chapters. Verse four, your borders are in the heart of the seas. Verse twenty-five, you were filled and heavily laden in the heart of the seas. Verse twenty-six, the east wind has wrecked you in the heart of the seas. Verse twenty-seven, everything sinks into the heart of the seas. And then in chapter twenty-eight, the king says, I sit in the seat of the gods in the heart of the seas, and God answers in verse eight, you shall die the death of the slain in the heart of the seas.

The place that made Tyre feel safe is the place where she drowns. And the east wind is Nebuchadnezzar from the east, but it is also the wind God sends, the wind that dried the Red Sea. Tyre's sailors steered her into deep water, and God sent the weather.

Then sailors climb down from their ships onto the shore, throw dust on their heads, and sing their own lament. Verse thirty-two. Who is like Tyre, like one destroyed in the midst of the sea? And the merchants hiss.

If that sounds familiar, it should. Revelation eighteen takes this very chapter and lays it over Babylon the great. The merchants stand far off and weep, there is a cargo list ending with slaves, that is, human souls, and the sailors cry, What city was like the great city? Tyre is a type of every proud economy that treats people as cargo and calls itself perfect in beauty. Every one of them ends with merchants standing on the shore.

Now chapter twenty-eight, and the camera moves from the city to the man.

Verse two. Say to the prince of Tyre, you have said, I am a god. Historically this is Ithobaal, the king Josephus names as reigning during Nebuchadnezzar's thirteen-year siege.

Verse three is sarcastic. You are indeed wiser than Daniel. That is the young exile we met in chapter fourteen, already famous at Nebuchadnezzar's court. The great king of Tyre is measured against a Jewish prisoner of war. And Henry gives a sentence I cannot improve on. "Note, Knowledge puffeth up; it is hard to know much and not to know it too well and to be elevated with it. He that was wiser than Daniel was prouder than Lucifer."

Then verses four and five show where his wisdom went. By your wisdom and your understanding you have made wealth for yourself. By your great wisdom in your trade you have increased your wealth, and your heart has become proud in your wealth.

D. A. Carson argues that the king is not really claiming to be a god the way a philosopher makes a claim about his own nature. He is the embodiment of a whole city's self-confidence. Carson says, "There is no sense of personal weakness or need, still less any lingering sense of dependence upon the God who made them and who providentially rules over them."

That is the sin. Not a shrine to Baal. A balance sheet that made him feel he needed no one. And God answers with the question from verse nine. Will you still say, I am a god, in the presence of those who kill you?

Then verse eleven begins a lament over the king of Tyre, and the language goes somewhere no one expects.

Verse twelve. You were the signet of perfection, full of wisdom and perfect in beauty. Verse thirteen. You were in Eden, the garden of God; every precious stone was your covering. Nine stones are named, and every one is also on the high priest's breastplate in Exodus twenty-eight. Verse fourteen. You were an anointed guardian cherub. I placed you; you were on the holy mountain of God; in the midst of the stones of fire you walked. Verse fifteen. You were blameless in your ways from the day you were created, till unrighteousness was found in you. Verse sixteen. In the abundance of your trade you were filled with violence, and you sinned; so I cast you as a profane thing from the mountain of God.

The English Standard Version footnotes admit that several phrases here are uncertain. But the big question is, who is this? A king who was in Eden? A cherub? Blameless from the day he was created? The voices divide.

The early church read it, almost with one voice, as the fall of Satan. Tertullian, writing around the year 207 against the heretic Marcion, who blamed the Creator for evil, said this. "If you turn to the prophecy of Ezekiel, you will at once perceive that this angel was both by creation good and by choice corrupt. For in the person of the prince of Tyre it is said in reference to the devil". His argument is that no human being was created in the paradise of God, not even Adam, who was placed there. So the words must reach beyond the king.

Origen agreed. Augustine, in the City of God, used this verse to answer whether the devil was created evil. No, says Augustine. Ezekiel says he was perfect in his ways. And he adds a sentence every Christian should know about evil. "for if sin be natural, it is not sin at all."

Gregory the Great went further. In his commentary on Job he says, "He mentioned nine kinds of stones, doubtless because there are nine; orders of angels." Then he borrows from the jeweler. Stones were drilled so that gold could bind them into a royal ornament. This angel, Gregory says, was made capable of love, but "This stone then had holes, but, through the sin of pride, they were not filled with the gold of charity." The other angels let love bind them to the King's crown. He refused, and fell out of the setting.

That is gorgeous. But is it what Ezekiel meant?

Later commentators mostly say, not first. Matthew Henry grants that many see the angels' fall here, but if there is anything mystical in it, he would rather refer it to the fall of Adam. Keil, the great nineteenth century Old Testament scholar, says Ezekiel compares the prince of Tyre with the first man in Paradise, and his fall with Adam's fall. On the cherub, Keil notices that the word for guardian, sokek, covering, is the word Exodus uses for the cherubim whose wings covered the mercy seat. The king saw himself as the sacred guardian of a sacred island. John Gill, surprisingly, reads the prince as a picture of Antichrist. And Carson says Tyre's sin is of a piece with Adam and Eve's. They wanted to be like God, with no one to tell them what is good.

So where do I land? Look at what the text insists on. This is a lamentation over the king of Tyre. Twice God says, you are but a man, and no god. And the sin that brought him down is commercial. In the abundance of your trade. In the unrighteousness of your trade. That is not an angel's fall before the world began. That is a merchant king.

So I read the lament as addressed to a real king, painted in the colors of Eden. He was placed in a paradise of wealth and wisdom. He was given a guardian's place. And he did exactly what Adam did. He reached for godhood, and he was driven out. The garden is the measure of how far he fell.

But the fathers were right about something. When a man says, I am a god, he is speaking the devil's language. Paul warns in First Timothy three that a proud new convert may become puffed up with conceit and fall into the condemnation of the devil. Pride was the devil's sin first. So behind every proud king stands a darker shadow, and the fathers saw it. Just start with the man, because the man is who God is addressing. And he is a lot more like you and me than a fallen archangel is.

The chapter ends with two short oracles. The first is against Sidon, Tyre's sister city and Jezebel's home. Verse twenty-two. Behold, I am against you, O Sidon, and I will manifest my glory in your midst.

Remember the glory. It left the temple and paused over the Mount of Olives in chapter eleven, and it has not returned. Jerusalem has no glory. But God does. He will be glorified even in Sidon. The God who left his house has not left his throne.

Then verses twenty-four through twenty-six turn to Israel. No more a brier to prick or a thorn to hurt them, the words Numbers thirty-three used for the Canaanites. They shall build houses and plant vineyards and dwell securely. The proud ship sinks. The broken people come home.

So here is the application, and it comes straight out of verse five. Your heart has become proud in your wealth.

Ezekiel does not despise ships or trade. Hiram of Tyre helped build Solomon's temple. Tyre was condemned because her success taught her she did not need God, and it was her wisdom that did it. The smarter she got, the more self-made she felt.

So, what is your island? Maybe your competence has quietly become the reason you sleep at night. Maybe it is your savings, or your reputation, or the fact that you have never really failed. Good gifts. But has any of them started to say, in the heart of the seas, I am a god? Matthew Henry has a line for anyone who measures himself by his achievements. "Let none reckon themselves beautified any further than they are sanctified, nor say that they are of perfect beauty till they come to heaven."

Job chapter nine, verses twenty-one through thirty-five.

Last time Job asked, how can a man be in the right before God? He pictured a courtroom and saw he could not win. Now he follows that thought into the dark, in four movements.

First, verses twenty-one through twenty-four. I am blameless; I regard not myself; I loathe my life. Keil reads it, I do not know myself. His suffering contradicts his conscience, and he has become a stranger to himself. Then comes his most daring line yet. It is all one; he destroys both the blameless and the wicked. When disaster brings sudden death, he mocks at the calamity of the innocent. The Pulpit Commentary grants that in one sense this is true. Ecclesiastes nine says the same event happens to the righteous and the wicked. But Job is leaving out the forgiveness of God and the life to come, and in his pain he is accusing God of laughing at the innocent.

Second, verses twenty-five and twenty-six. My days are swifter than a runner. They go by like skiffs of reed, like an eagle swooping on the prey. The skiffs are light papyrus boats from the Nile. Land, water, and air, all saying one thing. My life is disappearing.

Third, verses twenty-seven through thirty-one. Job tries to cheer up. If I say, I will forget my complaint, I will put off my sad face, I become afraid of all my suffering. Matthew Henry knew sickbeds. It is easy, he says, to preach patience to those in trouble, but it is not so soon done as said. Then, if I wash myself with snow and cleanse my hands with lye, you will plunge me into a pit. Whatever I do to clean myself, you will throw me back in the ditch.

Here Henry draws a line I think every suffering believer needs. "When we are in trouble we are allowed to complain to God, as the Psalmist often, but must by no means complain of God, as Job here." The psalms are full of complaint to God. How long, O Lord? But Job has begun talking about God as if God were the enemy. Henry is gentle. Good men, he says, do not always speak like themselves, and God remembers their frame.

Then the fourth movement, verses thirty-two and thirty-three, the turning point of the chapter. For he is not a man, as I am, that I might answer him, that we should come to trial together. There is no arbiter between us, who might lay his hand on us both.

The word for arbiter is mokiach, from a verb meaning to argue a case and decide it. Jacob used it with Laban: let our kinsmen decide between us two. Job wants an umpire who can stand between the parties and put a hand on each shoulder.

The footnote gives another reading. Would that there were an arbiter. The ancient Greek reads it as a wish, O that there were a mediator. Henry says the complaint that there was none is really a wish that there were.

Now, is Job prophesying Christ here? The voices genuinely disagree. The Cambridge Bible says flatly that there is no prophecy of the incarnation in these verses, only a cry of the human heart that it might meet and see God as a man. Gregory the Great, on the other hand, heard Job calling for the Mediator who is God and man in one. And Spurgeon preached a whole sermon on this verse, which he called The Great Arbitration Case. He described Job this way. "In his mournful plight, he sighed for an arbitrator who, while dealing justly for God, would at the same time deal kindly with poor flesh and blood, being able to lay his hand upon both."

I think Cambridge is right that Job is not predicting anything. But he is asking exactly the right question, the one the rest of the Bible answers, and we will hear the answer before we are done.

Here is the practical word. When you suffer, there is a way of talking that slowly turns God into the accused. Watch for it. And when you cannot see how you and God could be reconciled, do what Job does in verse thirty-three. Say what you need, and say it to God.

John chapter nine, verses twenty-six through forty-one.

Questioned yet again, the healed man runs out of patience. Verse twenty-seven. I have told you already, and you would not listen. Why do you want to hear it again? Do you also want to become his disciples?

A beggar is mocking the most powerful court in Jerusalem. Augustine catches the little word also. It means, I am one already.

They answer with contempt. You are his disciple, but we are disciples of Moses. We know that God has spoken to Moses, but as for this man, we do not know where he comes from. Calvin says they lie when they claim Moses, for they have turned aside from the goal of the law. Jesus told them in chapter five, if you believed Moses, you would believe me.

Then the man preaches. Why, this is an amazing thing! You do not know where he comes from, and yet he opened my eyes. We know that God does not listen to sinners. Never since the world began has it been heard that anyone opened the eyes of a man born blind. If this man were not from God, he could do nothing.

Is it true that God does not listen to sinners? Here a father and a Reformer disagree. Calvin says the man reasons correctly; by sinner he means the ungodly, and God does not answer the ungodly with signs like this. Augustine pushes back. The man, he says, is still only half taught. "For God hears even sinners. For if God heard not sinners, in vain would the publican, casting his eyes on the ground, and smiting on his breast, have said, Lord, be merciful to me a sinner."

Both are right. Calvin about the argument: God does not hand miracles like this to a fraud. Augustine about the soul: if you think your sins mean God will not hear you, the publican is proof that he will.

Verse thirty-four. You were born in utter sin, and would you teach us? At the start of the chapter the disciples asked, who sinned, this man or his parents? Jesus said neither. The Pharisees give the old answer anyway. And they cast him out.

Then comes the tenderest verse in the chapter. Jesus heard that they had cast him out, and having found him. John Chrysostom puts it in a single line. "The Jews cast him out from the Temple, and the Lord of the Temple found him".

J. C. Ryle adds that the time when men forsake us is often the very time when Christ draws near.

Jesus asks him, Do you believe in the Son of Man? Some manuscripts read Son of God. But Son of Man fits, because in Daniel seven the Son of Man receives the kingdom, and judgment is the next word Jesus speaks. The man answers, And who is he, sir? He has never seen Jesus. Jesus says, You have seen him, and it is he who is speaking to you. The voice he heard in the dark is the face he now sees in the light.

Lord, I believe, he says, and he worshiped him. How much did he understand? Calvin doubts he yet knew Christ was God in the flesh. Chrysostom says that by this act, which few healed people did, he declared Christ's divine power. I lean toward Chrysostom; in John that word is used for worshiping God. The man may not have had the theology yet. But his knees were ahead of his head.

Then verse thirty-nine. For judgment I came into this world, that those who do not see may see, and those who see may become blind. Some Pharisees overhear and say, Are we also blind? And Jesus answers, If you were blind, you would have no guilt; but now that you say, We see, your guilt remains.

Augustine explains it with a doctor. If you admitted you were blind, you would go to the physician. Because you say, we see, you never seek him. Spurgeon, preaching this verse in 1884, said the gospel always has an effect, antidote or poison. The most dangerous sentence in religion is, We see.

And there is no break in the story. Next time Jesus keeps talking to these same men about sheep, a door, and a shepherd. The man cast out of the fold is about to hear who the real shepherd is.

First John, chapter two, verses one through eleven.

Last time John said that if we say we have no sin we deceive ourselves, and that if we confess our sins, God is faithful and just to forgive. Now, verse one. My little children, I am writing these things to you so that you may not sin.

John has just spent several verses on forgiveness, and he says the point of all that grace is that you will not sin. Calvin says John joins two parts of the gospel that unreasonable men separate. Spurgeon preached this verse in 1863 against the charge that free grace makes people careless. Gratitude, he said, has bands stronger than iron, though softer than silk. And he described sin in a believer this way. "Sin is dejected in the Christian's heart, though it is not ejected." It is still there. But it is no longer on the throne.

But if anyone does sin, we have an advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ the righteous.

Advocate is parakletos, one called alongside, a defense counsel. It is the same word Jesus used in John fourteen for the Holy Spirit, another Helper. So we have one advocate in us, the Spirit, and one for us, with the Father, the Son. And notice his name: Jesus Christ the righteous. Matthew Henry points out that in this court the clients are guilty, so the advocate cannot plead their innocence. He must plead his own righteousness for them.

Then verse two. He is the propitiation for our sins.

Here is the promise I made last time. We heard Calvin say God is just to forgive because he is faithful to his promise, and Spurgeon say he is just because the debt has been paid. Verse two is why both are right. Propitiation is hilasmos, the sacrifice that turns away God's righteous wrath. John Owen connects it with the mercy seat, the lid of the ark, where the blood was sprinkled under the wings of the cherubim. The promise of forgiveness rests on a payment. God is not being lenient when he forgives you. He is being just to his Son.

And then the clause everyone argues about. And not for ours only but also for the sins of the whole world.

Does that mean Christ made atonement for every person who ever lived? The Westminster Confession, chapter eight, says that to all those for whom Christ purchased redemption, he certainly and effectually applies it, making intercession for them. John ties the two together too. The same Christ is advocate and propitiation. He does not die for any he will not plead for.

So what is the whole world? Calvin takes up the famous formula that Christ suffered sufficiently for the whole world but efficiently only for the elect. And his answer surprises people. "Though then I allow that what has been said is true, yet I deny that it is suitable to this passage; for the design of John was no other than to make this benefit common to the whole Church." Augustine, centuries earlier, read the verse against the Donatists, who thought the true church existed only in their corner of North Africa. Not for ours only, Augustine says. The church fills the whole world. And Owen says if John meant that Christ died equally for those who perish, it would comfort no one. "Is not this rather a pumice-stone than a breast of consolation?"

So the whole world means there is no other propitiation for anyone anywhere, and this one reaches people from every nation. And remember, John is consoling believers who have sinned. Do not make this verse only an argument. Make it a refuge.

Then verses three through six. By this we know that we have come to know him, if we keep his commandments. Whoever says I know him but does not keep his commandments is a liar. Calvin says that where there is no pure conscience, there is only an empty phantom of knowledge. And verse six. Whoever says he abides in him ought to walk in the same way in which he walked.

Augustine has a wonderful moment here. Walk as he walked? Does that mean, he asks, that we should walk on the sea? That be far from us! Last time Job said that God alone tramples the waves of the sea. You are not asked to do that. Augustine says the way Christ walked was the way of the cross, praying, Father, forgive them. If you have learned to pray for your enemy, he says, you walk in the way of the Lord.

Finally, verses seven through eleven. No new commandment, but an old one. And yet a new one. Old, Calvin says, because they heard it from the beginning of the gospel. New, because God renews it every day and it never grows stale. And the reason it is new is in verse eight. The darkness is passing away and the true light is already shining. Whoever says he is in the light and hates his brother is still in darkness. He does not know where he is going, because the darkness has blinded his eyes.

There are two real connections in today's readings, and I want you to hear both.

The first is the one I promised. In Job nine, verse thirty-three, a suffering man cried, There is no arbiter between us, who might lay his hand on us both. He needed someone who could stand with God and stand with him. Spurgeon begins his sermon on that verse by saying that what Job desired, the Lord has provided in his own dear Son. And today in First John two, verse one, the answer comes in plain words. If anyone does sin, we have an advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ the righteous. One hand on the Father, because he is the righteous Son. One hand on you, because he became the propitiation for your sins. Job's wish is your possession.

The second connection is blindness, and it runs through all three of our other readings. The king of Tyre was wiser than Daniel and could not see death coming. The Pharisees said, We see, and their guilt remained. And John, who wrote both the Gospel and the letter, says that the man who hates his brother walks in darkness because the darkness has blinded his eyes. In every case the blindness belongs to the confident.

So here is your question for today. Where are you saying, We see? Is there a place where your competence, your knowledge, or your success has made you sure you need no one? Tell God, I am blind there. That is where the light gets in.
