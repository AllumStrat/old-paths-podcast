How the hammer of the whole earth is cut down and broken! How Babylon has become a horror among the nations!

Now picture when those words were written. Not after Babylon fell. Before. Jeremiah wrote them while Nebuchadnezzar was at the height of his power, his armies undefeated, Jerusalem still standing but already under his thumb. The hammer had not even finished its work on Judah. And Jeremiah, the man everyone in Jerusalem called a Babylonian sympathizer, sat down and wrote Babylon's funeral song.

What does it take to see the end of the strongest thing in the world while it is still swinging?

This is Old Paths. Today is day nineteen of the tenth month in the reading plan. The readings are Jeremiah fifty, the Song of Songs chapter four, verses one through seven, John chapter six, verses sixteen through twenty-four, and First Peter chapter three, verses one through seven.

For several episodes now we have been walking through Jeremiah's oracles against the nations, and we have been watching for a few patterns. One was a little Hebrew word for quiet. The sword of the Lord could not be quiet. Moab had been quiet too long, settled on his dregs. Damascus was like a sea that cannot be quiet. Today that thread reaches Babylon, and it ends in a promise of rest for the whole earth.

The second pattern was restoration. Moab, Ammon, and Elam each heard, yet I will restore your fortunes. Edom heard nothing. Last time, Jeremiah's oracle against Edom ended in silence, and today you will hear the same silence over Babylon, only louder.

And then there is the cup. Back in Jeremiah twenty-five, God sent the cup of his wrath around the table of the nations, and the last line said, after them the king of Babylon shall drink. Today the cup reaches the last seat.

In the Song of Songs, the wedding procession of chapter three gives way to the groom's first full song of praise for his bride. In John, the disciples row into the dark without Jesus. And in First Peter, the word likewise carries Peter's teaching on submission into the most intimate institution of all, the home.

Jeremiah fifty first. Start with the date, because it changes how you hear the chapter. Chapters fifty and fifty-one belong together as one long oracle against Babylon, a hundred and ten verses, the longest in the book. And at the very end of chapter fifty-one, Jeremiah tells us when he sent it. It went to Babylon with a man named Seraiah, the son of Neriah, in the fourth year of Zedekiah. That is 594 or 593 B.C. Jerusalem would not fall for another seven years. Nebuchadnezzar would reign for another thirty. Babylon was the capital of the world.

And remember who is writing. This is the prophet who told the exiles to build houses in Babylon and to seek the welfare of the city, and pray to the Lord on its behalf. This is the man arrested as a deserter for saying, surrender to the Chaldeans and live. Jeremiah was no Babylonian patriot. He simply knew that Babylon was God's tool for a season, and that the season had an end date. Back in chapter twenty-five he even gave the date: when seventy years are completed, I will punish the king of Babylon.

Chapter twenty-five also gave Babylon a code name. The king of Sheshach shall drink. Sheshach is a cipher. Take the Hebrew letters of Babel, swap each one for its mirror in the alphabet, the last letter for the first, the second-to-last for the second, and you get Sheshach. For twenty-five chapters Babylon's doom was written in code. Now Jeremiah writes it in plain letters.

The chapter does not march in a straight line. It swings back and forth like a pendulum. Babylon falls, Israel comes home. Babylon falls again, Israel is pardoned. Babylon falls again, Israel's Redeemer pleads their case. Judgment and redemption take turns, and that is the point. Babylon's ruin is Israel's release.

Verse two opens with a headline. Babylon is taken, Bel is put to shame, Merodach is dismayed. Merodach is the Hebrew spelling of Marduk, the patron god of Babylon. Bel means lord, and it was Marduk's title. Nebuchadnezzar called himself Marduk's worshipper and named his own son Evil-merodach.

Here is where archaeology gives us something remarkable. When Cyrus the Persian took Babylon in October of 539 B.C., he had a clay cylinder inscribed to celebrate it. You can read it today in translation. Cyrus says it was Marduk who chose him, Marduk who made him enter his city Babylon without fighting or battle. The conqueror gave the credit to Babylon's own god. Jeremiah, fifty-five years earlier, had already answered him. Bel is put to shame. Merodach is dismayed. Same event, two theologies. One says the god of Babylon handed the city over. The other says the God of Israel judged the god of Babylon.

Verses four and five. In those days and in that time, the people of Israel and the people of Judah shall come together, weeping as they come, and they shall seek the Lord their God. They shall ask the way to Zion, with faces turned toward it, saying, Come, let us join ourselves to the Lord in an everlasting covenant that will never be forgotten.

Notice the word together. Israel and Judah had been two rival kingdoms for over three hundred years. The north had been in exile since 722. Now they come home side by side, and they come weeping. John Trapp, the Puritan, saw a pattern there. "In better times they could not agree; but when they were both in a weeping condition, misery bred unity, as it did also between Hooper and Ridley, when they were both in prison for the truth." John Hooper and Nicholas Ridley were English Reformers who had quarreled bitterly over clerical vestments. In Queen Mary's prisons, both waiting to be burned, they were reconciled. Shared repentance does what argument never could.

And watch one Hebrew verb, shakach, to forget. In verse five the covenant will never be forgotten. In verse six, My people have been lost sheep. Their shepherds have led them astray. They have forgotten their fold. The word fold is rebets, a place of lying down, a resting place. Here is the tragedy and the grace in two verses. A people that forgot its resting place is brought into a covenant that will never be forgotten. Their memory failed. God's will not.

Now verse seven, and the first hard question in the chapter. All who found them have devoured them, and their enemies have said, We are not guilty, for they have sinned against the Lord.

That is Babylon's defense, and it is not a stupid one. Jeremiah himself had called Nebuchadnezzar my servant. God had said, I am sending the Chaldeans against you. So the Babylonians say, we were only doing God's work. Your God was angry with you. We were the instrument. How can God then turn around and punish his own instrument?

John Gill, the Baptist, answers directly. "This they said, not that they feared the Lord, or had any regard to his honour and glory, but to excuse themselves, which would not do; for though they sinned against the Lord, they had not sinned against them, and they had no right to destroy them."

The chapter itself gives three reasons. First, motive. Verse eleven: though you rejoice, though you exult, O plunderers of my heritage. Babylon did not march on Jerusalem to uphold God's holiness. It marched for plunder, and it enjoyed it. Calvin says the Chaldeans were driven only by avarice and ambition, and it was never their purpose to punish the Jews as they deserved. Second, excess. Verse seventeen: first the king of Assyria devoured him, and now at last Nebuchadnezzar has gnawed his bones. God sent a rod. Babylon brought teeth. Third, and deepest, pride against God himself. Verse twenty-nine: she has proudly defied the Lord, the Holy One of Israel.

Isaiah faced the same question with Assyria and put it in one line. Shall the axe boast over him who hews with it? An axe can be in God's hand and still be an axe that hates the tree. Our own Westminster Confession, in chapter five, says that God's providence reaches even to the sinful acts of men, yet the sinfulness proceeds only from the creature, and not from God. Babylon did what God purposed. Babylon also did what Babylon wanted. God will judge what Babylon wanted.

Verse twenty is the heart of the chapter for me. In those days and in that time, declares the Lord, iniquity shall be sought in Israel, and there shall be none, and sin in Judah, and none shall be found, for I will pardon those whom I leave as a remnant.

Picture a search. Somebody goes through Israel's record with a lamp, looking for guilt, and finds an empty file. Not because Israel never sinned. Chapter fifty has just said they did. The reason is in the last line. I will pardon. The verb is salach, and in the Old Testament only God is ever its subject. Human beings forgive one another with other words. This word belongs to God. It is the same verb as in the new covenant promise of chapter thirty-one: I will forgive their iniquity, and I will remember their sin no more.

Spurgeon preached on this verse in 1860, and he reached back to Genesis for an image. "You remember that Rachel hid the image under the seat on which she sat, so that, when her father searched for it, he could not find it; yet it was there all the while. But it is not to be so with our sins; they are to be searched for, but there shall be none; they are to be sought for, but so effectually shall they have been put away, that they shall not be found." Our sins are not hidden under a saddle where a sharper search might find them. They are gone.

Thomas Watson, the Puritan, actually cites this verse in his book on the Beatitudes, and he uses a debtor's ledger. "The debt book is cancelled in Christ's blood, and if the debtor owe never so much, yet if the creditor cross the book, it is as if he had never owed anything." Owe never so much means no matter how much he owes. Watson is right to name the blood. In Jeremiah's own book, forgiveness is promised in the new covenant, and the new covenant is what Jesus said he was making when he lifted the cup on the night he was betrayed. That is the one place in this chapter where I think the cross is not an add-on but the answer to the question the verse raises. How can a holy God search and find nothing? Because someone else paid.

Calvin adds a warning here that I find searching. He says many people want relief from punishment without wanting reconciliation with God. They want the exile over, the pain gone. But sin is the disease, and the exile is only the symptom. Cure the disease and the symptoms go with it. Do you want the consequences of your sin removed, or do you want God?

Verse twenty-one has a pair of names worth a moment. Go up against the land of Merathaim, and against the inhabitants of Pekod. The ESV footnotes tell you Merathaim means double rebellion, and Pekod means punishment. Pekod was a real Babylonian tribe. Merathaim may be a pun on Aram-naharaim, the land of the two rivers. Ellicott suggests Jeremiah turned it into the land of the two rebellions. It is as if God renamed Babylon's provinces: Rebellion County and Punishment County.

Then verse twenty-three. How the hammer of the whole earth is cut down and broken! Matthew Henry gives it the perfect line. "The God of the whole earth will break the hammer of the whole earth." Babylon was the hammer that flattened Nineveh, Jerusalem, Tyre, and Egypt. But a hammer is a tool, and every tool belongs to someone.

Jerome, the great translator of the Latin Bible, borrowed this verse in a letter to a Roman mother named Laeta about raising her little daughter. He used Babylon as a name for the world's power to shape a child's heart. "If you take precautions to save your daughter from the bite of a viper, why are you not equally careful to shield her from the hammer of the whole earth? to prevent her from drinking of the golden cup of Babylon?" That is a fair use of the image. Babylon in Scripture becomes the name for the world organized against God, and parents still have to decide what their children will drink.

Now verses thirty-three and thirty-four, and our thread. The people of Israel are oppressed, and the people of Judah with them. All who took them captive have held them fast; they refuse to let them go.

If that sounds familiar, it should. Held fast, refuse to let them go: those are the very words of Exodus nine, where God says to Pharaoh, if you refuse to let them go and still hold them. Babylon has become Pharaoh. And so the answer comes in Exodus language too. Their Redeemer is strong; the Lord of hosts is his name.

Redeemer is go-el, the kinsman-redeemer. In Israel, when a man fell into debt or slavery or was murdered, the duty to buy him back or avenge him fell on his nearest relative. Boaz was Ruth's go-el. Here God calls himself Israel's next of kin. And there is a wordplay you can only see in Hebrew. The captors held them fast. The verb is chazaq, to be strong. Their Redeemer is strong, the same root, chazaq. The grip of Babylon is strong. The grip of the Redeemer is stronger.

He will surely plead their cause, that he may give rest to the earth, but unrest to the inhabitants of Babylon. Two Hebrew verbs that sound almost the same, hirgiya and hirgiz. Give rest, give unrest. Keil and Delitzsch point out that for decades Babylon had kept the whole earth in turmoil, and now the turmoil comes home to Babylon while the earth finally breathes.

So here is where our thread lands. In chapter forty-seven, the sword of the Lord was told, rest and be still, and that word rest is the same verb God uses here. Moab had been quiet too long. Damascus could not be quiet. Babylon, which had robbed the whole world of its quiet, now loses its own. Isaiah fourteen describes the same moment: the whole earth is at rest and quiet; they break forth into singing. Babylon's fall is the world's exhale.

Now the second hard question. Did this happen? Verse thirty-nine says Babylon shall never again have people, nor be inhabited for all generations. Verse forty compares it to Sodom. But when Cyrus took Babylon in 539, the Babylonian chronicle of that year says his army entered without battle. Cyrus kept Babylon as a royal capital. Alexander the Great died there two centuries later. So was Jeremiah wrong?

Calvin faced that question openly, and I respect him for it. "We hence, then, conclude, that what Jeremiah declares here, was not immediately fulfilled. But as the light or moderate punishments which the unbelieving suffer now are certain preludes of final and eternal destruction; so the Prophets, when speaking of God's vengeance, ever extend what they say to the last overthrow." In other words, the prophet sees the whole mountain range at once, and from far away the peaks look close together.

And history did climb the whole range. In 539 the empire ended in a single night, exactly as verse twenty-four says: you were taken, O Babylon, and you did not know it. The exiles went home. Then the city declined, revolt after revolt under the Persians, then the Greeks built a new capital nearby and drained its population away. When the Roman emperor Trajan came through in A.D. 116, he found ruins. Today Babylon is an archaeological site. It took six centuries, but the word stood.

And the New Testament picks up the far end. When John writes Revelation eighteen, he builds it out of this chapter and the next. Come out of her, my people. Pay her back as she herself has paid back others. A great millstone thrown into the sea. Babylon becomes the name of every proud human city that defies God, and its fall is still coming.

One more detail. The last section, verses forty-one through forty-six, recycles earlier oracles. Verses forty-one to forty-three are almost word for word from chapter six, where a people from the north came against the daughter of Zion. Now it says, against you, O daughter of Babylon. And verses forty-four to forty-six repeat what was said of Edom yesterday. Babylon hears the very words it once fulfilled against others. Verse fifteen spells out the principle: do to her as she has done.

So bring it home. Go back to verse seven. We are not guilty, for they have sinned. That is the oldest excuse in the world for cruelty. The person I hurt deserved it. My harshness was just the consequences catching up with them. A father says it about the child he humiliates. A boss says it about the employee he crushes. You and I say it in our heads about the people who wronged us first.

And it may even be true that they sinned. Israel had sinned. But God's answer to Babylon still stands. Their guilt before God never gave you a license. When God uses you in someone else's life, as a parent, a manager, a pastor, a husband, he is not only watching what you do. He is watching whether you enjoy it. Where in your life are you saying, they deserved it, as cover for something in you that wanted it?

Now the Song of Songs, chapter four, verses one through seven. Last time we watched Solomon's wedding procession come up from the wilderness. Now the groom speaks, and in verse eight he will call her bride for the first time. This is wedding-day praise.

The form has a name. Scholars call it a wasf, an Arabic word for description, from the wedding songs of Syrian villages where the groom would praise his bride feature by feature. The Song has several. Here he starts with her eyes and moves downward, and he gives seven images: eyes, hair, teeth, lips, cheeks, neck, breasts. Then he wraps the whole poem in a frame. It opens, Behold, you are beautiful, my love. It closes, You are altogether beautiful, my love; there is no flaw in you.

Now let us be honest. Some of these images sound odd to us. Your teeth are like a flock of shorn ewes that have come up from the washing, all of which bear twins. Your neck is like the tower of David. If you drew this literally, you would get a cartoon.

The early church noticed that too. Gregory of Nyssa, preaching on this passage in the fourth century, said plainly, "I do not understand how anyone could say that praise is being given her teeth by comparing them with prolific flocks." Gregory concluded the passage must be purely spiritual. Modern readers notice the same oddity and draw a different conclusion: this poetry is evocative, not photographic.

Think it through. Black goats streaming down a hillside in Gilead look, from a distance, like dark hair falling in waves. Washed sheep are brilliant white. Twins, with none missing, means a full matched set of teeth, which in the ancient world was no small thing. The tower hung with a thousand shields is a neck held high and decked with layers of necklaces. Delitzsch noticed that the first images come from a shepherd's world of flocks and hills, and then they turn royal, with David's tower and warriors' shields. He is saying, you have the bearing of a queen.

Then verse seven. There is no flaw in you. The Hebrew word for flaw is mum, pronounced moom. It is the word Leviticus uses for a blemish that disqualified a priest from the altar or an animal from sacrifice. And when the Greek Old Testament translated this verse, it used momos, the root of the word Paul uses in Ephesians five, where Christ presents the church to himself without spot or wrinkle, holy and without blemish. Paul is not quoting the Song, but he is using its vocabulary, and he says the mystery of marriage refers to Christ and the church. So here we may step carefully across.

Is it true that the church has no flaw? Plainly not, in herself. The bride in this Song confesses her own failures. James Durham, the Scottish Puritan, wrote a full commentary on the Song in the sixteen hundreds and was careful here. He refused to say the church is sinless, and he refused to say her sins do not matter. Then he gave Christ's words this sense. "'Thou art all fair,' that is, in my account thou art so; I reckon not thy spots, but esteem of thee as if thou had no spot: Christ is no severe interpreter of his people's actions."

That is the gospel, and it is also a pattern for a marriage. A husband who loves his wife is not blind to her faults. But love decides what to dwell on and what to say out loud. So here is the practical wisdom. Husbands, notice how this man praises. Not, you look nice. He is specific. He takes time. He uses images from the world the two of them share. And he does it before chapter four, verse sixteen, before the wedding night. Unhurried words come before intimacy. When did your wife last hear you describe, specifically, what you see in her?

John chapter six, verses sixteen through twenty-four. Last time the crowd wanted to make Jesus king by force, and he withdrew to the mountain alone. Matthew and Mark tell us he made the disciples get into the boat. He sent them away from the crowd's fever before it could infect them.

When evening came, his disciples went down to the sea, got into a boat, and started across the sea to Capernaum. It was now dark, and Jesus had not yet come to them. That last line is almost haunting. John uses darkness all through his Gospel for life without the light. Then the wind rises, and they row three or four miles, about halfway across, in heavy seas.

They saw Jesus walking on the sea and coming near the boat, and they were frightened. Every Jew who knew the Scriptures had a category for this. Job nine says God alone stretched out the heavens and trampled the waves of the sea. Psalm seventy-seven says of the Exodus, your way was through the sea, yet your footprints were unseen. Only God walks on water.

Then verse twenty. But he said to them, It is I; do not be afraid. In Greek, ego eimi. Literally, I am. Here the voices genuinely divide. J. C. Ryle, the Anglican bishop, doubted that it means anything more than, it's me. He called the divine-name reading a pious thought but not suited to the context. R. C. Sproul disagreed. Preaching on this passage, he said, "Ego eimi, I am. I am who I am is here." Sproul noted that this one is usually left off the list of Jesus's I am sayings, even though the words are the same.

I think both men see something true. On the surface, yes, he is identifying himself to frightened friends. But consider who is speaking, and where he is standing. He does not need the words to make the divine claim. His feet are already making it. And a few verses later he will start saying, I am the bread of life.

Augustine read the scene as a picture of the church's life in the world. "Tribulations increase, calamities increase, sorrows increase, all these swell and mount up: Jesus passes on treading upon the waves." The waves that are over your head are under his feet.

Alistair Begg put the cure for fear simply. "What cancels unbelief? Belief. What cancels fear? The one in whom we believe." Notice that Jesus does not explain the storm. He does not even say, the wind will stop. He says, It is I. The answer to fear is a person.

Then they were glad to take him into the boat, and immediately the boat was at the land to which they were going. Was that a second miracle? John Gill thought so, and so did Chrysostom. Ryle thought the storm simply stopped and the rest of the trip went fast. Either way, it echoes Psalm one hundred seven: then they were glad that the waters were quiet, and he brought them to their desired haven.

Verses twenty-two to twenty-four tell us the crowd worked it out. There was one boat, Jesus did not get in it, and yet he was gone. So they borrow boats from Tiberias and go hunting. But tomorrow Jesus will tell them why they came. Not for him. For more bread. The disciples rowed through a storm and got Jesus. The crowd sailed calm water and got exposed.

First Peter three, verses one through seven. Peter has been telling his churches how to live as exiles under pressure: be subject to every human institution, servants to masters, even crooked ones. Then he set Christ before them, who when reviled did not revile in return but entrusted himself to God. Now chapter three opens with likewise. In the same way. The wife in a hard marriage is to walk the path Christ walked.

Peter has a particular case in mind. Likewise, wives, be subject to your own husbands, so that even if some do not obey the word, they may be won without a word by the conduct of their wives. In the Roman world, a wife was expected to take her husband's religion. Plutarch, the Greek moralist, advised a new bride to worship only the gods her husband believes in and to shut the door on strange rituals. So a Christian wife married to a pagan had already defied her husband on the deepest question there is. Peter does not tell her to go back. He tells her that her conduct can do what arguments could not.

Tertullian, writing around A.D. 200 to his own wife, described what that marriage looked like. On her days of prayer, the pagan husband makes an appointment to meet her at the baths, and on her fast days he throws a banquet. But he also says what a faithful wife can do to such a man. "He has felt mighty works; he has seen experimental evidences; he knows her changed for the better: thus even he himself is, by his fear, a candidate for God." Experimental evidences means proof he has seen with his own eyes.

Verses three and four. Do not let your adorning be external, the braiding of hair and the putting on of gold jewelry, or the clothing you wear. That is not a ban. If it were, it would forbid clothing. It is a contrast. Let your adorning be the hidden person of the heart with the imperishable beauty of a gentle and quiet spirit. That word imperishable takes us back to chapter one, where we were ransomed not with perishable things such as silver or gold. Gold perishes. A gentle and quiet spirit does not. And the word for very precious in God's sight is the word for luxury goods, costly attire. Peter is saying, here is what heaven calls high fashion.

Then Sarah, who obeyed Abraham, calling him lord. Here is something wonderful. The only place Sarah calls Abraham lord in Genesis is chapter eighteen, verse twelve, and she says it to herself, while laughing in disbelief at God's promise. Calvin says Peter means this was how she habitually spoke of him, even privately. Matthew Henry sees grace in it: God remembers her virtues and passes over the laugh. Peter adds, do not fear anything that is frightening. This submission is not weakness. It takes courage.

And verse seven. Likewise, husbands. Likewise again, the same Christ-shaped path. Live with your wives in an understanding way, literally according to knowledge. Study her. Showing honor to the woman as the weaker vessel. Both are vessels, fragile jars. Hers is weaker in physical strength, and that weakness is a reason for more honor, never less. Since they are heirs with you of the grace of life. In the things of God, she is your equal. So that your prayers may not be hindered. Treat your wife carelessly and you will find heaven's door sticking.

Chrysostom, preaching on marriage, gave husbands a very practical word. "Show her too, that you set a high value on her company, and that you are more desirous to be at home for her sake, than in the market-place." For us, the market-place is the office, the phone, the garage, the game.

Two connections before we go, and both of them are in the text.

First, today's Song and today's epistle both speak to husbands. The groom in the Song looks at his bride and says, you are altogether beautiful. Peter tells the husband to live with his wife according to knowledge and to honor her. Put them together and you have a picture. A godly husband studies his wife, and then he tells her what he sees.

Second, fear. On the lake, Jesus says, It is I; do not be afraid. In First Peter, the wife who hopes in God is told, do not fear anything that is frightening. And behind both stands Jeremiah, writing Babylon's funeral song while Babylon was still the terror of the world. Fear shrinks when you know who holds the hammer, who walks on the water, and who has pardoned you so completely that a search finds nothing.

So here is a question to carry through your day. Whom are you treating as if their guilt gave you permission, and what would it look like to answer them the way God has answered you?

Next time we read Jeremiah fifty-one, as Babylon's oracle concludes and Seraiah sinks the scroll in the Euphrates, the Song of Songs chapter four, verses eight through sixteen, John six, verses twenty-five through forty, where Jesus says I am the bread of life, and First Peter three, verses eight through twelve.

This has been Old Paths. Grace and peace.
