Daniel chapter three, verse eighteen. But if not, be it known to you, O king, that we will not serve your gods or worship the golden image that you have set up.

Three men, a furnace, and a king who has never heard the word no. And the strongest words in their answer are the three smallest. But if not. What would your faith sound like if you took away the guarantee?

This is Old Paths. Today is day twenty-one of the eleventh month in the reading plan, day two hundred seventy-one of three hundred. The readings are Daniel three and four, Job nineteen, John twelve, verses thirty-seven through fifty, and Second John, which begins and ends today.

Last time, Nebuchadnezzar fell on his face before Daniel and confessed that his God is God of gods. Then I said that in the very next chapter he would build a statue of gold, all of it gold this time. He heard that his kingdom would end, and he built one that would not. Today he builds it. And twice now I have promised you Job nineteen. Today Job answers the question he asked two chapters ago, where then is my hope?

Daniel three and four.

First, where we are. Nebuchadnezzar took the throne in 605 B.C. and reigned until 562, more than forty years. Chapter three carries no date in the Aramaic text, but an old tradition, found in the Greek translation, sets it in his eighteenth year. That is around 587 B.C., when Jerusalem fell. If that is right, the king raised his golden image in the same season his armies were tearing down the temple of the Lord. Chapter four comes near the end of his reign, decades later.

The image is sixty cubits high and six wide, about ninety feet by nine, probably gold plated. It may have been a column with a figure on top, which would explain the strange proportions. And whatever it represented, the point is plain. In chapter two God showed the king a statue with a head of gold that would be broken. Here the king answers with a statue that is gold from head to foot.

Now listen to how Daniel tells it. The satraps, the prefects, the governors, the counselors, the treasurers, the justices, the magistrates. He lists them in verse two, and again in verse three. The horn, pipe, lyre, trigon, harp, bagpipe. He lists the instruments four times. The image that King Nebuchadnezzar has set up, over and over. This is the liturgy of the state, every official in his place, every instrument on cue, the whole world bending at the same moment. And against all that machinery Daniel sets three names. Spurgeon counted them. Their three names come thirteen times in this one chapter, like the refrain of a song.

Certain Chaldeans bring the charge, and the king says the line the whole chapter turns on, verse fifteen. Who is the god who will deliver you out of my hands?

Their answer is verses sixteen through eighteen. We have no need to answer you in this matter. That is not rudeness. They mean, there is nothing to deliberate. Then, our God whom we serve is able to deliver us. And he will deliver us. But if not.

Is that doubt? Jerome saw the care in the wording. They do not say, if he is not able. They say, in effect, if he does not will it. If they die, it will be God's choice, not God's weakness. They are certain of God's power. They are not presuming on God's plan. And their obedience does not hang on the outcome.

Spurgeon preached on these verses in a sermon published in 1891, and he put his finger on the heart of it. They did not base their loyalty to God upon their deliverance. They did not say, as some do, I will serve God if it pays me to do so. I will serve God if He helps me at such and such a time. No, they would serve Him for nothing! Theirs was not cupboard love.

Cupboard love is the love of a cat for whoever opens the pantry. These men loved God himself, not his provisions.

The furnace is heated seven times hotter, so hot it kills the soldiers who throw them in. And then the king leaps up. Did we not cast three men bound into the fire? But I see four men unbound, walking in the midst of the fire, and the appearance of the fourth is like a son of the gods.

So here is the hardest question in the chapter. Who is the fourth man?

The voices divide. Hippolytus, a church leader in Rome around the year 200, wrote one of the oldest Christian commentaries we have, and it is on Daniel. He believed the king saw the Son of God. And he noticed something in verse twenty-six. The three youths he thus called by name. But he found no name by which to call the fourth. For He was not yet that Jesus born of the Virgin. Spurgeon agreed. In that same sermon he pictures the Son of God leaving heaven to stand beside them in the coals.

Calvin disagrees. He says God sent one of his angels, and that a pagan king who called the figure a son of the gods was speaking the common language of his world about divine beings. He could not have recognized the only begotten Son. Jerome sits in between. The Greek translation says an angel, he notes, and most in his day said the Lord our Savior, but he wonders how a godless king could have been given a vision of the Son of God. And Nebuchadnezzar himself, two verses later, says God sent his angel.

Here is where I land. Nebuchadnezzar did not know what he was looking at, and his words cannot carry the doctrine by themselves. Many in our tradition, Matthew Henry and John Gill among them, believe this was the Son before his incarnation, and that may well be right. But the text's own point does not depend on settling it. God did what he promised in Isaiah forty-three, verses two and three. When you walk through fire you shall not be burned, and the flame shall not consume you. For I am the Lord your God. The fire burned exactly one thing, the ropes. And the book of Hebrews remembers these men without naming them, among those who by faith quenched the power of fire. That is my one New Testament move today, and it is Hebrews pointing back to this furnace.

There is nothing quite like chapter four in the Old Testament. It is a letter from a pagan emperor, in his own voice, to all peoples, nations, and languages. The same phrase he used to command worship of his image, now used to confess the God of Israel.

He dreams of a tree that reaches heaven and shelters all flesh. A watcher, a holy one from heaven, orders it cut down to a stump, bound with iron and bronze, and the man's mind changed to a beast's for seven periods of time. And the refrain, three times in the chapter, in verses seventeen, twenty-five, and thirty-two. The Most High rules the kingdom of men and gives it to whom he will.

Daniel urges him to break off his sins by showing mercy to the oppressed. Twelve months later, on the palace roof, verse thirty. Is not this great Babylon, which I have built by my mighty power? That boast fits the man. His own inscriptions, like one now held in the British Museum, are mostly records of what he built. While the words were still in the king's mouth, the sentence falls. Matthew Henry says it in one line. Nebuchadnezzar would be more than a man, but God justly makes him less than a man.

Then verse thirty-four. At the end of the days I, Nebuchadnezzar, lifted my eyes to heaven, and my reason returned to me. Jerome caught the order of events. Had he not raised his eyes towards heaven, he would not have regained his former intelligence.

Look up, then think straight. Not the other way around. And notice what the king confesses. None can stay his hand. Keil says that Aramaic idiom comes from striking a child's hand to stop him. No one slaps God's hand away. Was the king truly converted? Matthew Henry thought there could be no reasonable doubt. I am hopeful. Few Gentiles anywhere in the Old Testament speak this truly about God, and his last sentence is about pride. Those who walk in pride he is able to humble.

So two applications from two chapters. From chapter three: settle now that your obedience does not depend on rescue. And from chapter four: when your thinking goes wrong, the cure begins with lifting your eyes.

Job nineteen.

Job answers Bildad, and the chapter moves in a long descent before it climbs. These ten times you have cast reproach upon me, he says, meaning over and over. Then God is pictured as an army, walling up his road and building a siege ramp against one small tent. Then the slow loss of every person. Brothers, guests, servants, his wife, children in the street. I have escaped by the skin of my teeth. Then, have mercy on me, have mercy on me, O you my friends. A suffering man does not need your theory. He needs your mercy.

And then verse twenty-five. For I know that my Redeemer lives, and at the last he will stand upon the earth.

The Hebrew word is go'el. In Israel the go'el was your next of kin, the one who bought back the family land when you lost it, who freed you if you were sold, who stood up for your blood. Who is Job's go'el? Not his family, who have abandoned him. It is God, the very God he just described as his enemy. The Cambridge Bible points out that Job sets the word lives against himself. Job will die. His redeemer goes on living. Job is appealing from God to God, and calling God his kinsman.

Then verse twenty-six. In my flesh I shall see God. The Hebrew can be read from my flesh or without my flesh, and the commentators split. But verse twenty-seven settles it for me. My eyes shall behold, and not another. The church read it that way from the start. Clement of Rome, writing to the Corinthians near the end of the first century, asked, Do we then deem it any great and wonderful thing for the Maker of all things to raise up again those that have piously served Him in the assurance of a good faith? And he cited Job.

Job still has no answers about his suffering. He has something better. I know.

John twelve, verses thirty-seven through fifty.

This is John's verdict on the public ministry. Jesus has hidden himself, and the next chapter moves to the upper room. So John stops and asks why. Though he had done so many signs before them, they still did not believe in him. Then two texts from Isaiah. Lord, who has believed what he heard from us? And then the hard one. Therefore they could not believe. He has blinded their eyes and hardened their heart.

Could not, or would not? Augustine faced it squarely. But if I am asked why they could not, I reply at once, because they would not. And he goes on to say that God hardens by withdrawing his help, which is just, while every gift of help is pure mercy.

Then verse forty-one. Isaiah said these things because he saw his glory and spoke of him. Isaiah six, the throne, the seraphim, holy, holy, holy. Whose glory? R. C. Sproul, preaching this text, said it plainly. Isaiah saw Christ on the throne in His glory before Christ was even born. I think that is what John is declaring here.

And then a sad wordplay. Many of the authorities believed, but would not confess him, for they loved the glory that comes from man more than the glory that comes from God. Isaiah saw the glory of God. These men preferred the glory of the synagogue's approval. Chrysostom said they were not really rulers but slaves.

The section ends with Jesus crying out his last public words. I have come into the world as light. The word that I have spoken will judge him on the last day. The light does not wait forever.

Second John.

Today a whole book in one sitting. The writer calls himself the elder, and the church has always understood him to be the apostle John, writing late in the first century, likely from Ephesus. It is a single page. In the first four verses the word truth appears five times.

To the elect lady and her children. Is she a woman or a church? Clement of Alexandria thought both, a lady named Electa whose name signals the elect church. A Ligonier devotional reads her as a real woman and her household. I lean toward a church, because the letter closes with the children of your elect sister. Sister churches greeting one another.

John asks for love, and defines it. This is love, that we walk according to his commandments. Then the danger. Deceivers who do not confess the coming of Jesus Christ in the flesh. One of these was a teacher named Cerinthus, who taught that the divine Christ came upon a mere man named Jesus and left before the cross. Irenaeus passes on a story he traced back through Polycarp. John went to the baths at Ephesus, saw Cerinthus inside, and ran out without bathing, exclaiming, Let us fly, lest even the bath-house fall down, because Cerinthus, the enemy of the truth, is within.

Then verse ten. Do not receive him into your house or give him any greeting. Remember the churches met in houses, and traveling teachers lived on the hospitality of believers. To house such a teacher was to give him a pulpit and a meal ticket. The Pulpit Commentary warns that this is a rule for a special case, not a general principle. John is not banning kindness to unbelievers. He is banning partnership with false teachers.

Here is the connection, and it runs through the flesh. Job said, in my flesh I shall see God. In the furnace stood a fourth figure, and Hippolytus said the king had no name for him because he was not yet born of the Virgin. John tells us Isaiah saw his glory. And Second John draws the line of fellowship at one confession, that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh. Job wanted to see God with his own eyes, and God took flesh so he could be seen.

So carry this question through the day. What would you still refuse to bow to, even if God does not deliver you?
