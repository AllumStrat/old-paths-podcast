Ezekiel forty, verse four. Son of man, look with your eyes, and hear with your ears, and set your heart upon all that I shall show you.

And then what does God show him? A wall. A gate. Six side rooms. Thresholds, jambs, vestibules, and measurement after measurement for two full chapters.

Why would God ask a man to set his heart on that?

This is Old Paths. Today is day sixteen of the eleventh month in the reading plan, day two hundred sixty-six of three hundred. The readings are Ezekiel chapters forty and forty-one, Job chapter fifteen, verses one through sixteen, John chapter eleven, verses thirty-eight through forty-four, and First John chapter three, verses nineteen through twenty-four.

Last time we watched the armies of Gog buried in a valley with a name, and the war chapters ended with a promise. I will not hide my face anymore from them. Two chapters before that, God had said, my dwelling place shall be with them. So the question hanging over the exiles is simple. Where? The temple is ash. The glory left in chapter eleven. Today Ezekiel is shown the house. And in John, where we left Jesus weeping at a grave, today he speaks to it.

Ezekiel forty and forty-one.

Start with the date in verse one. The twenty-fifth year of our exile, the fourteenth year after the city was struck down. That is about 573 B.C. The last date we heard was the fugitive arriving in chapter thirty-three. More than a decade has passed. Jerusalem has been a ruin for fourteen years.

Then notice the day. At the beginning of the year, on the tenth day of the month. Ellicott and Keil both take this as the first month, and the tenth day was the day each household chose its Passover lamb, in Exodus twelve. It was also the day, in Joshua four, that Israel came up out of the Jordan into the land. The vision of the new house comes on the day the old exodus began.

Now listen to the shape of the whole book. In chapter one Ezekiel saw God by a canal in Babylon, and the vision was terrifying. In chapter eight he was carried to Jerusalem in visions of God, and he saw idols in the temple courts and the glory departing. Now, verse two, in visions of God he is brought back again, and set on a very high mountain. The Scottish Presbyterian Patrick Fairbairn caught the contrast. The vision on the Chebar was fitted to awaken terror and awe, he wrote, while this one was meant to produce the liveliest confidence and the most exalted hopes.

Waiting at the gate is a man whose appearance is like bronze, with a linen cord and a measuring reed. Look at the reed. In Hebrew it is qaneh hammiddah, the reed of measuring. The Pulpit Commentary points out that the word passed into Greek as kanon and into Latin as canna. That is where we get our word canon, a measuring rule. Before a single wall is described, the house has a rule, and God holds it.

Then the walk begins, and here is where you need to see it. The reed is about ten feet long. The outer wall is one reed thick and one reed high. You enter by the east gate, and inside the gateway are three side rooms on each side, guardrooms. The Reformation Study Bible notes that this exact plan, three chambers on either side of the passage, resembles gates from Solomon's day dug up at Hazor, Megiddo, and Gezer, the very cities First Kings nine says Solomon fortified. This is architecture an Israelite would recognize.

And keep count of the steps. Seven steps up into the outer court. Eight steps up into the inner court. Ten steps up to the porch of the temple itself. Every move toward God is a move upward.

Chapter forty-one takes us inside. The nave, forty cubits by twenty. Then verse three. He went into the inner room. He, not they. The guide measures it, twenty cubits square, the same as Solomon's, and says, this is the Most Holy Place. Ezekiel is a priest by birth, and he stands outside. The way into that room is still not open to him.

Look at the walls. Cherubim and palm trees carved all around, a palm between every pair of cherubim. Cherubim once guarded the way back to the garden, and Poole calls palms emblems of victory. The whole house is decorated like Eden, with the guards turned into ornaments. And in verse twenty-two, one piece of furniture, a wooden altar that the guide calls the table that is before the Lord.

Now notice what is missing. Fairbairn saw it, and it is striking. The temple itself, in its two important divisions, is hastily sketched, and nothing scarcely said of its sacred furniture, not even the slightest notice taken of the ark of the covenant, the heart and centre of the whole in former times. No ark. No lampstand. No gold. Pages on gates and side rooms, a single line on the holiest place.

Which brings us to the hard question. What kind of temple is this?

Fairbairn sorted the answers into four. Some said Ezekiel was simply remembering Solomon's temple. Others said this was a blueprint the returning exiles should have built and failed to. A third view, held by some Jewish teachers and by many Christians today, says it will be built literally in the future, sacrifices and all. And the fourth view, Fairbairn says, from the fathers downwards, was the prevailing view of the church, held by Luther and Calvin. The vision is a God-given picture, drawn in the shapes the exiles knew, of what God will do when he comes to dwell with his people.

I hold that fourth view, and the text pushes us there. First, Ellicott notes that in visions of God is the same phrase used for chapters one and eight. Nobody reads those as floor plans. Second, a Ligonier Tabletalk devotional makes a sharp point. When the exiles came home, Haggai rebuked them for dragging their feet on God's house, but no prophet ever rebuked them for not building Ezekiel's temple. And in the coming chapters the measurements grow so large that Matthew Henry, borrowing from John Lightfoot, says the temple would be larger than all of old Jerusalem. Gregory the Great, preaching on this chapter, said flatly that the building cannot be understood according to the letter, and he pointed out that Ezekiel says there was a structure like a city. Not a building. Something like one.

But does that make the vision less real? No. It makes it more certain. A symbol from God is a promise from God.

The New Testament tells us where this leads. In Revelation twenty-one, John is carried in the Spirit to a great, high mountain. An angel holds a measuring rod. The city is measured. And then John says, I saw no temple in the city, for its temple is the Lord God the Almighty and the Lamb. Ezekiel's house is fulfilled not in more stone but in God himself dwelling with his people. And Jesus told the woman at the well that the hour was coming when worship would belong to neither that mountain nor Jerusalem.

So why is the inner room empty? Fairbairn quotes the German scholar Havernick, and it is the line I want you to carry into next time. The interior of the temple there stands empty, waiting for the entrance of the Lord, that he may come and fill it with his glory.

Waiting. The glory left by the east gate in chapter eleven. In chapter forty-three it comes back. Ezekiel is walking through a beautiful house with no one home yet.

So why set your heart on measurements? Because they say this house is real, ordered, and his, and because attention is how anyone learns God. Spurgeon preached on verse four in 1881, and he was not talking to scholars. Some people like to read so many chapters every day. I would not dissuade them from the practice, but I would rather lay my soul asoak in half a dozen verses all day than I would, as it were, rinse my hand in several chapters.

You are on a reading plan. Good. But do not just rinse your hands in Ezekiel forty. Pick one verse. Ten steps up to the porch. A palm between the cherubim. He went into the inner room. Let it soak.

Job chapter fifteen, verses one through sixteen.

Round one is over. Every friend has spoken, and Job has answered each. Now round two begins, and Eliphaz, the oldest and most respected of the three, goes first again. His tone has changed. In chapter four he started gently. Now he starts with a slap.

Should a wise man answer with windy knowledge, and fill his belly with the east wind? The Hebrew is da'at ruach, knowledge of wind. And the east wind, qadim, is the scorching desert wind that withers crops. Eliphaz is saying, you are full of hot air, and it is the destructive kind.

Verse seven mocks him. Are you the first man who was born, brought forth before the hills? That is how Proverbs eight speaks of Wisdom herself.

Then verse eleven. Are the comforts of God too small for you, or the word that deals gently with you? The Cambridge Bible observes that the word that deals gently is Eliphaz describing his own first speech. He has labeled his own advice the comforts of God.

And in verses fourteen through sixteen he repeats the oracle from his night vision in chapter four, and throws Job's own words back at him. What is man, that he can be pure? Or he who is born of a woman. Job said that in chapter fourteen.

Everything Eliphaz says about human sin is true. Matthew Henry says his doctrine is sound but misapplied. Henry also names Eliphaz's real error. He has charged Job with conclusions Job never drew and would abhor.

Gregory the Great, in his Morals on Job, saw why the same words can be wrong from one mouth and right from another. Wherefore then was this, but that Almighty God estimates the words of each by the thoughts within, and in His ear those are not high, which are uttered with a lowly heart?

So here is the wisdom for today. When you correct someone, check two things. Is it true? And are you sure it is God's comfort, and not just yours with his name on it?

John chapter eleven, verses thirty-eight through forty-four.

Jesus, deeply moved again, comes to the tomb. A cave, with a stone across it. And he gives an order that sounds almost rude at a funeral. Take away the stone.

Martha, who has just confessed him as the Christ, objects. Lord, by this time there will be an odor, for he has been dead four days. Calvin, commenting on this verse, sees all of us in her. Distracted in various ways, we fight with ourselves, and while we stretch out the one hand to ask assistance from God, we repel, with the other hand, that very assistance, as soon as it is offered.

Jesus answers, did I not tell you that if you believed you would see the glory of God? That reaches back to verse four, where he said this illness is for the glory of God.

Then he prays, and the prayer is thanks. Father, I thank you that you have heard me. Spurgeon noticed the timing. Lazarus is still dead when Jesus says thank you. The answer is on the way, and he gives thanks first.

He cries with a loud voice, Lazarus, come out. Back in chapter five Jesus said an hour is coming when all who are in the tombs will hear his voice and come out. Bethany is a preview.

And the dead man comes out, bound hand and foot. Augustine asks the right question. Do you marvel that he could walk with his feet bound, and not marvel that he walked out at all? Either way, he says, it was the Lord's power, not the dead man's strength.

Then, unbind him, and let him go. Why have others do it? Chrysostom says it was so the crowd would be witnesses, their hands by lifting the stone, and loosing the grave-clothes, and their smell also by perceiving the ill-odor. Calvin agrees. Augustine, though, read the unbinding as a picture of the church loosing sins, and Calvin will have none of it. I stand with Chrysostom and Calvin here.

First John chapter three, verses nineteen through twenty-four.

Last time John said, let us not love in word or talk but in deed and in truth. Now he says, by this we shall know that we are of the truth and reassure our heart before him. The heart here means the conscience. And John assumes your conscience will sometimes accuse you. Whenever our heart condemns us, God is greater than our heart, and he knows everything.

Is that a comfort or a warning? Here the voices split. Calvin reads it as a warning. God is greater with reference to judgment, he says. If your own dim conscience condemns you, God sees more. Spurgeon's exposition reads it the same way. But a Ligonier devotional on this verse reads it as comfort. John expects even mature believers to struggle with doubt, and when they do, God himself is the ultimate basis for assurance. He knows every sin we have missed, and he still promises to receive the humble for Christ's sake.

I think both are right, and which one you hear depends on which way you are running. Augustine put it perfectly. If you would flee from Him, flee to Him. Flee to Him by confessing, not from Him by hiding: hide you can not, but confess you can.

Then verse twenty-two. Whatever we ask we receive from him. Augustine faced the obvious objection. Paul asked three times about his thorn and did not receive it. Augustine's answer was that Paul was heard for his salvation, even when he was not heard according to his wish.

Verse twenty-three gives the first use of the word believe in this letter, and the commandment is singular. Believe in the name of his Son and love one another. One command, two sides. And verse twenty-four gives the first plain mention of the Spirit. Next time John tells us to test the spirits.

Here is the connection. Each reading has a human verdict that God overrules. The exiles said, our bones are dried up and our hope is lost, and God answered with the plans of a house. Eliphaz pronounced his verdict on Job and called it the comforts of God, and the end of the book will overturn it. Martha said, four days, there will be an odor, and Jesus said, take away the stone. And when your own heart condemns you, God is greater than your heart.

Carry this question through the day. What verdict is your heart pronouncing right now that God is greater than?
