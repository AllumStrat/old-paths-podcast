A field outside a city. Bare ground, a hot wind, and a newborn girl lying where someone left her. The cord has not been cut. Nobody has washed her. Nobody has rubbed her with salt or wrapped her in cloth. Nobody is coming back. People walk past on the road and do not turn their heads.

Then one traveler stops. He looks at her, still lying in her own blood, and he says one word to her. Live.

He says it twice.

Here is the question underneath today's reading. What do you do with a word like that, once you have grown up on it?

This is Old Paths. Today is day five of the eleventh month in the reading plan, day two hundred fifty-five of three hundred. The readings are Ezekiel chapter sixteen, Job chapter five, John chapter eight, verses twenty-one through thirty, and Second Peter chapter two, verses seventeen through twenty-two.

Last time, Ezekiel fifteen ended with a vine that was good for nothing but the fire, and the last line of the chapter gave the reason. Because they have acted faithlessly, declares the Lord God. That word faithless is the seed. Today God takes it and grows it into a whole life story, from a birth in a field to a marriage to a betrayal to a covenant that will not die.

Yesterday I called this a long and shocking parable, and it is. It is the longest chapter in Ezekiel, and it uses the language of sexual betrayal openly. I will be frank about what it says, because God was. I will not be graphic, because there is no need.

There is also a puzzle waiting in Job. In Job five, Eliphaz says that God catches the wise in their own craftiness, and the apostle Paul quotes that line in First Corinthians as Scripture. But at the end of the book God says that Eliphaz did not speak of him what is right. So how can Paul quote him? We will answer that today.

In John, Jesus tells his enemies, when you have lifted up the Son of Man, then you will know that I am he. And Peter finishes his portrait of the false teachers with two of the least flattering animals in the Bible.

Ezekiel chapter sixteen.

Start with where we are. There is no date at the top of this chapter, so it belongs to the block that began at chapter eight, verse one, in late summer of 592 B.C., and runs to the next date at chapter twenty, a year later. Zedekiah is on the throne in Jerusalem, a puppet of Babylon, quietly looking toward Egypt. Ezekiel is in Tel-abib by the Chebar canal, still mute except when God opens his mouth. Remember that, because this chapter ends with a promise about a mouth that stays shut.

The marriage picture was not new. Hosea had married an unfaithful wife as a living sermon. Jeremiah had remembered Israel's love as a bride in the wilderness. Ezekiel takes the picture further than anyone, and he will do it again in chapter twenty-three. It is so blunt that the Mishnah records Rabbi Eliezer ruling that this chapter should not be used as the prophetic reading in the synagogue. Spurgeon felt the same pull. He preached on the end of this chapter in 1876 and began like this. "A very extraordinary chapter this sixteenth of Ezekiel! A minister could scarcely read it in public: he certainly would not like to explain its metaphors to a general audience, nor are we called upon to do so. To read it in private is another thing, and to have it read for you by the Holy Spirit, and to be made to see, and to feel its meaning, not merely as describing the Israelites, but as very much setting forth yourself, is a very different matter."

That is how to read it. Not as a story about them. As a mirror.

The commission comes in verse two. Son of man, make known to Jerusalem her abominations. Why would Jerusalem need to be told? Calvin says vice blinds people to what everyone else can plainly see. The neighbors could see it. The city could not.

The chapter moves in five scenes: the foundling, the harlot, the sentence, the sisters, and the everlasting covenant.

Scene one. Verse three. Your origin and your birth are of the land of the Canaanites; your father was an Amorite and your mother a Hittite. That is meant to sting. Jerusalem really was a Jebusite city until David took it, but the point is moral, not genetic. Calvin says God is describing what they were in themselves. Left to their own nature they were no different from the pagans around them.

Verses four and five, the birth with no one to help. The cord not cut, no washing, no salt rubbed on the skin, no swaddling cloths. No eye pitied you, verse five says, but you were cast out on the open field. Exposing unwanted babies, especially girls, was common in the ancient world. Every listener knew what this meant.

Then verse six. And when I passed by you and saw you wallowing in your blood, I said to you in your blood, Live! I said to you in your blood, Live!

The Hebrew is one word, chayi. Live. A command spoken to something that cannot obey a command. It is the word of a creator, not an adviser. And set it against verse five. No eye pitied you, and then God's eye did. Matthew Poole, the Puritan, saw it. God, he says, "saw thee, in such manner as to pity and consider how to relieve. To Omniscience every thing is seen, but here compassion is included in this seeing, this was the only eye that pitied."

Where is this in Israel's history? Calvin, Henry, Gill and Keil agree. Egypt, where Pharaoh tried to strangle the nation at birth by killing its boys. Calvin says that with a hundred deaths around them, only the secret voice of God kept them alive, and he thinks the word is doubled because the oppression was long. God did not say live once and walk away. He kept saying it, generation after generation, in the brick pits.

Verse eight. When I passed by you again and saw you, behold, you were at the age for love, and I spread the corner of my garment over you and covered your nakedness; I made my vow to you and entered into a covenant with you, declares the Lord God, and you became mine.

The corner of the garment is Ruth's language to Boaz. Take me under your protection as your wife. In Israel's history this is Sinai. And Calvin is struck by the oath. "If we think of the majesty of God, and of what his people was, this is surely incredible, that God should deign to descend so far as to swear like men accustomed to pledge their faith, and to sanction it by an oath." God did not need to swear. He swore so that she would never have to wonder.

Then verses nine through fourteen, the washing and the wardrobe. Oil, embroidered cloth, linen, silk, bracelets, a crown. You grew exceedingly beautiful and advanced to royalty. That is David and Solomon and the temple. And then the line the whole chapter turns on. It was perfect through the splendor that I had bestowed on you.

Scene two begins with one small word in verse fifteen. But. But you trusted in your beauty and played the whore because of your renown.

That is the turn, and it is the most ordinary sin in the world. She did not first go looking for lovers. First she started believing the beauty was hers. John Trapp says she should have spoken of it the way the young prophet in Second Kings six spoke of the axe head that flew into the Jordan. "Alas, master, it was but borrowed." The gifts became her reason to leave the giver.

From here the verb that dominates the chapter is zanah, to play the harlot. Watch what she does with the gifts. The garments become shrines. My gold and my silver become images. My oil, my incense, my bread, all set before idols. God keeps saying my. Then verses twenty and twenty-one, the worst line in the chapter. You took your sons and your daughters, whom you had borne to me, and these you sacrificed to them to be devoured. Keil points out that from the time of Ahaz, Judah actually burned children to Molech in the Valley of Hinnom. And God calls them my children.

Then the lovers get names. Egypt. Assyria. Chaldea. That is foreign policy. Calvin says Judah committed two kinds of fornication, idolatry and treaties with pagan powers. Ahaz bought help from Assyria. Hezekiah showed his treasures to Babylon. Zedekiah, right now, is flirting with Egypt. Three times the chapter says it. You were not satisfied.

Then the strangest twist, verses thirty-three and thirty-four. Men give gifts to all prostitutes, but you gave your gifts to all your lovers, bribing them to come to you. She pays them. Calvin notes that the word for different in verse thirty-four comes from a verb meaning to overturn. She is the inversion of a harlot, sinning at a loss. You know this pattern. Sin rarely pays what it promised. We keep paying it anyway.

Scene three is the sentence, and it has a terrible justice. I will gather all your lovers. The nations she ran to will strip her and stone her. Ligonier's daily devotional on this passage puts it well. "Many people think that God's answer to sin is to keep people from committing it. On the contrary, His answer is often to give people over to it." God gives her what she wanted, and what she wanted destroys her.

Scene four. Like mother, like daughter. Then her sisters. Samaria to the north, already destroyed by Assyria. And to the south, Sodom. And God says something no one in Jerusalem expected. Verse forty-eight. As I live, your sister Sodom and her daughters have not done as you and your daughters have done.

Verse forty-nine. This was the guilt of your sister Sodom: she and her daughters had pride, excess of food, and prosperous ease, but did not aid the poor and needy. Some readers use this verse to say Sodom's sin was only a lack of hospitality. But verse fifty goes on. They were haughty and did an abomination before me. That points straight back to Genesis nineteen. Ezekiel is not replacing Genesis. He is showing the road that led there. Calvin says Sodom began with smaller sins, and that pride is the mother of contempt for God and of cruelty. John Chrysostom quoted this verse to a congregation that wanted nothing from God but laughter and play, and told them that was exactly what Sodom wanted.

Why is Jerusalem worse? Because she had more. The law, the temple, the priests, the prophets. Sodom sinned in the dark. Jerusalem sinned with the lights on.

That is my first New Testament move, and Jesus makes it himself. The ESV cross-references this verse to Matthew eleven, where Jesus tells Capernaum, the town where he did his mighty works, that it will be more tolerable on the day of judgment for the land of Sodom than for you. More light, more guilt.

Now the hardest question in the chapter. Verse fifty-three. I will restore their fortunes, both the fortunes of Sodom and her daughters, and the fortunes of Samaria and her daughters, and I will restore your own fortunes in their midst. Sodom is under the Dead Sea. What can it mean to restore Sodom?

Calvin reads it as irony, reasoning from an impossibility. You will be safe when Sodom is, which is to say never. Some older Jewish writers, and Grotius, said Sodom here means Moab and Ammon, Lot's descendants. Calvin calls that trifling, and Keil agrees. Keil takes it as a real promise about the real Sodom, fulfilled beyond this present age, and a pledge that mercy will reach the nations.

Here is where I land. Read verse fifty-three with verse sixty-one, where Jerusalem receives her sisters, and God says, I give them to you as daughters. Even Calvin reads that verse as the nations brought into the church through the gospel that went out from Zion. So I lean with Keil toward a real promise of mercy wider than Israel, while keeping Calvin's point that the proud city as it stood had no hope. Either way the knife cuts the same direction. Verse fifty-six says Sodom was a byword in your mouth in the day of your pride. Now Jerusalem stands in the same line as the people she despised, waiting on the same mercy.

Scene five. After fifty-eight verses of the worst news imaginable, verse sixty begins with yet. I will deal with you as you have done, you who have despised the oath in breaking the covenant, yet I will remember my covenant with you in the days of your youth, and I will establish for you an everlasting covenant.

She did not remember the days of her youth. Verses twenty-two and forty-three both say so. God did.

Calvin says this everlasting covenant can only be the new covenant made by the hand of Christ, and yet it grows out of the old one, the same in substance and different in form. That is what our Confession says in chapter seven: not two covenants of grace, but one, under different administrations.

Then the last verse. That you may remember and be confounded, and never open your mouth again because of your shame, when I atone for you for all that you have done, declares the Lord God.

When I atone for you. The verb is kipper, the great word of the Day of Atonement. Spurgeon said the idea is to cover a thing with something that sticks to it, and that the related word is used for the pitch that sealed Noah's ark inside and out. Forgiveness is not a coat of paint. It is pitch.

That is my second New Testament move, and the text demands it. Who makes this atonement? Not the bride. She has nothing left to pay with. God does it himself, and on the night he was betrayed, Jesus took the cup and called it the new covenant in my blood.

Now notice the order in that last verse. Forgiveness does not make her forget. It makes her remember, and blush, and go quiet. Calvin says this is what separates God's children from hypocrites. "Hypocrites boast with swelling words, that they rely on the mercy of God, and speak mightily of the grace of Christ, but meanwhile they wish the memory of their sins to remain buried."

And the closed mouth. Ezekiel's own mouth is closed right now as a sign of judgment. This is a different silence. Spurgeon says it shuts the mouth of boasting, of excuses, and of complaint against God. When you know what you were forgiven, you have nothing to brag about and nothing to argue about. He summed up the chapter in one line. "Two words, if you can learn them, will teach you the deepest practical wisdom: sin and grace."

So here is what to carry out of Ezekiel today. The bride's ruin started when she forgot the field. She began to think the jewels were hers, and then she gave them to anything that flattered her. The cure is memory. Remember what you were when he found you. Remember who said live. Remember whose gold that is around your neck. That memory will keep you from pride on your good days and from despair on your worst ones, because the God who said live to you in your blood has not changed his mind.

Job chapter five.

Eliphaz is still talking. Chapter four was the first half of his speech, and chapter five finishes it. It has three movements. Verses one through seven: no one in heaven will take your side, fools are cut off, and trouble is man's lot. Verses eight through sixteen: As for me, I would seek God, followed by a small hymn to the God who sends rain and lifts the lowly and traps the clever. Verses seventeen through twenty-seven: blessed is the one whom God reproves, followed by a string of promises if Job will accept it. Then the closing line in verse twenty-seven, with the confidence of a man who has never been wrong in his life. Behold, this we have searched out; it is true.

Verse seven is the famous one. Man is born to trouble as the sparks fly upward. In Hebrew, the sparks are bene reshef, sons of reshef. Reshef can mean a flame. Song of Songs eight uses it for the flashes of fire in love. Keil calls the sparks children of the flame. Gregory the Great, reading the Latin Bible, had a different text, man is born to labor and the bird to flying, and he drew a lovely thought from it. "So then, man is born to labour, and a bird to flying, for the mind flies free on high for the very same reason that the flesh toils the sorer below." Both pictures go up. Trouble is as natural as fire rising, and for the believer, trouble is also what drives the soul upward.

Now the question from yesterday. Verse thirteen. He catches the wise in their own craftiness. Paul quotes it in First Corinthians three, verse nineteen, with the words for it is written. That is the only time the New Testament quotes any of Job's three friends. Yet God says in chapter forty-two that they did not speak of him what is right. So how can Paul quote Eliphaz as Scripture?

Spurgeon preached on this chapter in 1855 and faced the question head on. "We do not believe all that Job's friends said. They spoke very often as uninspired men, for we find them saying many things that are not true." But then he notes that Paul quotes verse thirteen, "thus giving sanction to this passage as having been inspired of God, at all events as being most certainly truthful." Matthew Henry says the same: the friends were not always directed by the Spirit, but their general doctrines expressed the faith of the patriarchal age, and Paul quoted verse thirteen as Scripture. Calvin, commenting on First Corinthians, simply says Paul applied it with good reason.

Here is how I would put it. Inspiration guarantees that Job records every speech accurately, including the wrong ones. It does not make every sentence spoken in the book true. We test each sentence by the rest of Scripture. Verse thirteen passes. God really does catch the clever in their own schemes. Ask Haman. Ask Ahithophel. Eliphaz's problem was not his sentences. It was his patient.

Look at verses seventeen and eighteen. Behold, blessed is the one whom God reproves; therefore despise not the discipline of the Almighty. For he wounds, but he binds up; he shatters, but his hands heal. That is true. Proverbs three says the same, and Hebrews twelve quotes Proverbs. Then he promises Job that if he accepts the correction, he will laugh at famine, his children will be many, and he will go to his grave like a ripe sheaf.

Think about saying that to this man. His children are dead, crushed when the house fell on them. And his friend is promising him many children if he repents of whatever he did. Ligonier's devotional on this chapter names the problem exactly: Eliphaz has no category for discipline that is not punishment. Gregory said Eliphaz assumed Job was being purified when he was actually being proved.

So this is the wisdom for today. The right verse at the wrong moment can be cruel. When a friend is suffering, before you reach for a true sentence, ask the honest question. Do I actually know why this is happening to him? Usually you do not. Eliphaz did not. And neither, yet, did Job.

John chapter eight, verses twenty-one through thirty.

We are still in the temple treasury, after the Feast of Booths, where no one could arrest Jesus because his hour had not yet come. Verse twenty-one. So he said to them again, I am going away, and you will seek me, and you will die in your sin. Where I am going, you cannot come.

Back in chapter seven he said almost the same thing, and they wondered whether he meant to go teach the Greeks. Now the guess is uglier. Will he kill himself? There is a dark irony here. He would lay down his life, and they would be the ones to arrange it.

Verse twenty-three. You are from below; I am from above. You are of this world; I am not of this world. And verse twenty-four. Unless you believe that I am he you will die in your sins.

Notice that verse twenty-one says sin, singular, and verse twenty-four says sins, plural. Calvin thinks the singular names the root sin, unbelief, which is the source of all the others. Every other sin can be forgiven. The one that refuses the forgiver cannot.

Now the phrase that matters most. I am he. There is no he in the Greek. Jesus says, ego eimi. I am. The same words appear in the Greek Old Testament at the burning bush, and through the second half of Isaiah, where God says that you may know and believe me and understand that I am he.

The voices disagree. Augustine paused in his sermon on this verse and said, "There is much implied in His only saying I am; for so also had God said to Moses, I am who am." Matthew Henry and John Gill agree: it is the divine name. Calvin pushes back. He says some ancient writers deduced Christ's divine essence from this verse, and that is a mistake, since Jesus is speaking of his office as Messiah. I appreciate Calvin's caution. He never wanted to prove a true doctrine from a weak text. But here I think Augustine is right. The phrase stands alone, without a title, and John is building toward the end of this chapter, where Jesus says, before Abraham was, I am, and they pick up stones. They knew what they had heard.

Verse twenty-five. Who are you? The Greek answer is famously hard. The ESV says, just what I have been telling you from the beginning. Augustine's Latin led him to read, I am the Beginning. Calvin reads it, I am what the prophets promised from the beginning. Either way, he has not changed his story.

Then verse twenty-eight. When you have lifted up the Son of Man, then you will know that I am he. This is the second of three lifting up sayings in John. The first was chapter three, as Moses lifted up the serpent. The third is chapter twelve, I, when I am lifted up from the earth, will draw all people to myself, and John adds that he said this to show by what kind of death he was going to die.

How will a crucifixion reveal who he is? R. C. Sproul, preaching this passage, said the lifting up has a double meaning. It is the cross and it is the exaltation. "It is at the same time His darkest hour and His greatest hour, as it is in that hour that He brings us our redemption." Chrysostom said that when they saw that death could not hold him, they would know.

And some did. Augustine believed Jesus was looking at future converts in that crowd, the men who would cry out for his blood and then, at Pentecost, be cut to the heart. Augustine asked, what murderer need despair, when even those who killed Christ were forgiven?

Verse thirty. As he was saying these things, many believed in him. Tomorrow Jesus will test that faith, and it will not go well. Hold that thought.

Second Peter chapter two, verses seventeen through twenty-two.

This is the end of Peter's long portrait of the false teachers, and he closes with pictures. Verse seventeen. These are waterless springs and mists driven by a storm. A spring promises drink and a cloud promises rain. These promise and do not deliver. Calvin says they had flash and polish in their words, but they were dry and barren inside, so the appearance of a fountain was a lie.

Verse eighteen. Speaking loud boasts of folly, they entice by sensual passions of the flesh those who are barely escaping from those who live in error. Entice is that fishing word again from verse fourteen, to catch with bait. And look at the target. Not the mature. Those who are barely escaping. The new believer, still half out of the old life. That is who the wolves go after.

Verse nineteen. They promise them freedom, but they themselves are slaves of corruption. For whatever overcomes a person, to that he is enslaved. It is a soldier's rule. If you lose the battle, you become the property of whoever beat you. Calvin's comment is short: no one can give what he has not. A slave cannot hand out freedom.

Augustine quoted this verse in the City of God when he was writing about slavery in the Roman world, and he said something that would have shocked his readers. "And beyond question it is a happier thing to be the slave of a man than of a lust; for even this very lust of ruling, to mention no others, lays waste men's hearts with the most ruthless dominion." The freest-looking man in the room may be the most chained.

Then verses twenty and twenty-one, and here is the hard question. For if, after they have escaped the defilements of the world through the knowledge of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ, they are again entangled in them and overcome, the last state has become worse for them than the first. For it would have been better for them never to have known the way of righteousness than after knowing it to turn back.

Does that mean a true Christian can lose his salvation? Our Confession, chapter seventeen, says those whom God has effectually called and sanctified "can neither totally nor finally fall away from the state of grace." Our Baptist brothers in the 1689 Confession say exactly the same. So how do we read Peter?

Notice what Peter says these people had. They escaped the defilements, outwardly. They had knowledge of Christ. They knew the way of righteousness. He never says they were born again. John Gill says men may outwardly reform and still be destitute of the grace of God. Calvin draws the line this way: the faithful also sin, but they do not let sin rule, and so they do not fall away.

And then Peter gives the proof himself, in verse twenty-two. What the true proverb says has happened to them. The dog returns to its own vomit, and the sow, after washing herself, returns to wallow in the mire. The first line is Proverbs twenty-six. The second was a common saying. And here is the point. The sow was washed, but she was still a sow. The dog emptied its stomach, but it was still a dog. Gill says it would be impossible for them to be otherwise unless their natures were changed. The outside was cleaned. The nature never was.

Calvin turns it into a picture of the gospel. "But what Peter meant is briefly this, that the gospel is a medicine which purges us by wholesome vomiting, but that there are many dogs who swallow again what they have vomited to their own ruin; and that the gospel is also a laver which cleanses all our uncleanness, but that there are many swine who, immediately after washing, roll themselves again in the mud."

So does a warning like this make sense if true believers cannot fall away? Yes. God keeps his people by means, and warnings are one of the means. The sheep hears the warning and stays close. And that is what to take from this paragraph. You do not prove you are a new creature by how clean you once got. You prove it by where you go back to when no one is watching.

Two connections between today's readings, and both are really there in the text.

The first is the danger of knowing much. Ezekiel tells Jerusalem that Sodom was more righteous than she was, because Jerusalem had been washed and dressed and crowned and still went back to the street. Peter says it would have been better never to have known the way of righteousness than to know it and turn back. Same principle in both. Privilege does not protect you. It raises the stakes. The more you have heard, and you have heard a great deal, the more it matters what you do with it.

The second is a phrase. Ezekiel uses one refrain more than any other prophet: then you will know that I am the Lord. Usually it comes after judgment. But in chapter sixteen, verse sixty-two, it comes after mercy. I will establish my covenant with you, and you shall know that I am the Lord. In John eight, Jesus takes up the same kind of language. When you have lifted up the Son of Man, then you will know that I am he. Ezekiel's people would learn who God is through the fall of a city and then through an atonement they did not pay for. Jesus' hearers would learn who he is through a cross. In both places the knowledge of God comes through blood, and in both places it can come as judgment or as grace.

Here is your question for the day. Where were you when he found you? Not in general. Specifically. Take a minute today and remember the field, and then look at what is in your hands right now and ask whose it is.
