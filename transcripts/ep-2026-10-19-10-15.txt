The last thing Zedekiah ever saw was his sons. Babylonian soldiers had caught him on the plain near Jericho and dragged him north to Riblah in Syria. There Nebuchadnezzar killed the boys in front of their father. Then he put out the father's eyes, so that picture would be the last one.

A few months later, a few miles from the ruins, a crowd of survivors stood in front of Jeremiah and said, whether it is good or bad, we will obey the voice of the Lord our God.

Ten days later they found out whether they meant it.

This is Old Paths. Today is day fifteen of the tenth month in the reading plan. The readings are Jeremiah thirty-nine through forty-three, Ecclesiastes twelve, John chapter five, verses one through fifteen, and First Peter chapter one, verses seventeen through twenty-five.

Last time we left Jeremiah in the court of the guard, alive because an Ethiopian official pulled him out of a muddy cistern with ropes and old rags. We left King Zedekiah with his feet, as the prophet put it, sunk in the mud. And I asked you to carry one Hebrew word through those chapters, shama, to hear, the word that also means to obey. Today it reaches the end of the road. In chapter forty-two a whole community takes an oath built on that verb. In chapter forty-three the narrator uses the same verb twice to tell you they broke it.

We also left a promise hanging. In chapter thirty-nine God tells Ebed-melech, the man with the ropes, what will happen to him when the city falls. Today we find out why God kept him.

Ecclesiastes ends today, with a poem about growing old. John takes us to a pool in Jerusalem and a man who had been lying beside it for thirty-eight years. And Peter tells exiles what they were bought with, and quotes a prophet who once spoke to exiles.

So, Jeremiah. Chapter thirty-nine is the hinge of the whole book. For about forty years Jeremiah has said one thing. Babylon will take this city. Now, in ten verses, it happens.

The dates are given with care. The siege began in the ninth year of Zedekiah, in the tenth month. That is January of 588 B.C. The wall was breached in the eleventh year, the fourth month, the ninth day. That is July of 586. Eighteen months of siege, and by the end, chapter fifty-two says, there was no food for the people of the land.

Remember the clay letters from Lachish I mentioned last time. In letter four an officer out in the hills writes that he is watching for the signal fires of Lachish, because he cannot see Azekah. Jeremiah thirty-four named Lachish and Azekah as the last fortified cities left in Judah. You can feel the lights going out one hill at a time.

Verse three. The officials of the king of Babylon come and sit in the middle gate, and one of them is Nebu-sar-sekim the Rab-saris, a title meaning chief official. In 2007 a researcher in the British Museum read a small cuneiform tablet from a temple near Baghdad. It records a gift of gold, dated 595 B.C., from an official named Nabu-sharrussu-ukin, with that same title. That is very likely the same man, eight years before he sat in Jerusalem's gate. And notice where they sit. Back in chapter one, at Jeremiah's call, God said the kingdoms of the north would come and set their thrones at the entrance of the gates of Jerusalem. Forty years later, there they sit.

Zedekiah runs. At night, by the king's garden, east toward the Jordan valley. He is caught in the plains of Jericho, the very door his people first came in by under Joshua. Then verse seven. Nebuchadnezzar put out the eyes of Zedekiah and bound him in chains to take him to Babylon. Jeremiah had said Zedekiah would see the king of Babylon eye to eye. Ezekiel said he would be brought to Babylon but never see it. Both came true at Riblah.

Matthew Henry, the Presbyterian commentator, says Nebuchadnezzar was "so condemning him to darkness for life who had shut his eyes against the clear light of God's word." Zedekiah heard the word privately, more than once, and would not walk by it. Now he would walk by nothing at all.

The city burns. But verse ten has a small reversal. Nebuzaradan leaves some of the poorest people, those who owned nothing, and gives them vineyards and fields. The landless get the land.

Then Nebuchadnezzar himself gives orders about Jeremiah. Look after him well. Jeremiah is handed to Gedaliah, the son of Ahikam, the son of Shaphan. That is the family we met yesterday. Shaphan read the law to Josiah. His son Gemariah lent his room for the reading of the scroll. His son Ahikam protected Jeremiah from a mob in chapter twenty-six. Now the grandson takes the prophet home. Through forty years of kings who would not listen, one family kept standing next to the word of God.

Now the promise we were waiting for. Verse fifteen tells you it was given while Jeremiah was still shut up in the court of the guard, but it is recorded here, after the fall, so you can see it kept. To Ebed-melech God says, I will deliver you on that day, and you shall not be given into the hand of the men of whom you are afraid. The brave man was afraid, and God spoke to the fear.

Then verse eighteen. You shall have your life as a prize of war, because you have put your trust in me. When a city falls everyone grabs plunder. Ebed-melech's plunder is his own life. The verb for trust is batach, to lean your whole weight on something. Jeremiah has used it all through the book for false confidence. In chapter seven the people trusted in deceptive words, the temple of the Lord. In chapter seventeen, cursed is the man who trusts in man. Here at last is one man whose trust was in the right place, and he was not even an Israelite.

In Faith's Checkbook Spurgeon says the great men of Jerusalem fell by the sword and poor Ebed-melech was safe, because his confidence was in the Lord. Then he writes, "No one ever did trust in the Lord in vain, and no one ever shall." Alexander MacLaren, the Baptist preacher of Manchester, notices the words in me. "The object of Faith is not a proposition but a Person." Ebed-melech had a God to lean on, and he leaned. And Calvin adds that the courage that went down to the cistern grew out of that faith, because unbelief is always timid.

Chapter forty. Jeremiah had been swept up with the captives and marched in chains to Ramah. Nebuzaradan finds him there and makes a speech. The Lord your God pronounced this disaster against this place. Because you sinned against the Lord and did not obey his voice, this thing has come upon you. There is our verb, shama, and listen to who is preaching it. A Babylonian general. Matthew Poole, the Puritan, writes, "This pagan commander could see that which the blind Jews would not understand." Calvin draws out the warning. If we will not hear the prophets God sends, God will let ungodly men lecture us about our own sins.

Jeremiah is offered Babylon and chooses to stay. He goes to Gedaliah at Mizpah, a few miles north of Jerusalem. And for a moment it looks like healing. A clay seal impression from the ash layer at Lachish reads, belonging to Gedaliah, who is over the house, the palace steward's title, and many think it was his. Gedaliah tells the scattered officers what Jeremiah had told the kings. Serve the king of Babylon, and it shall be well with you. Judeans who had fled to Moab, Ammon, and Edom come home. And verse twelve says they gathered wine and summer fruits in great abundance. Jerusalem fell in midsummer. That autumn there is a harvest.

Then the warning. Johanan son of Kareah tells Gedaliah that the king of the Ammonites has sent Ishmael son of Nethaniah to kill him, and privately offers to kill Ishmael first. Gedaliah says, you are speaking falsely of Ishmael. Was he wrong? Matthew Henry says Gedaliah had the innocence of the dove without the wisdom of the serpent. But Henry also asks of Johanan, if it is lawful to kill a man for what he might do, who can be safe? Calvin finds the middle path. Gedaliah was right not to murder a man on a rumor, but he was a governor, and he should have investigated. There is a way between being gullible and being cruel, and it usually involves asking questions.

Chapter forty-one. In the seventh month, Ishmael, of the royal family, comes with ten men, and verse one says they ate bread together. Then they rise and strike Gedaliah down. A prince of David's house murders the one man holding the remnant together. John Gill, the Baptist commentator, cites the old Jewish chronicle that dates the murder to the third day of the seventh month, and says the Jews kept a fast for it after the exile. Zechariah seven mentions that fast. Jews still keep it every autumn, the Fast of Gedaliah.

It gets worse. Eighty men arrive from Shechem, Shiloh, and Samaria, beards shaved and clothes torn, carrying offerings for the house of the Lord. They are pilgrims bringing gifts to a burned temple. Ishmael meets them weeping, lures them in, and slaughters them, and throws the bodies into the large cistern King Asa had made for defense against the northern kingdom. Last time a cistern nearly killed the prophet. This time a cistern becomes a mass grave. A pit dug three centuries earlier to protect God's people is filled with God's people.

Johanan catches Ishmael at the great pool of Gibeon, but Ishmael escapes to Ammon and vanishes from the story. Johanan gathers the survivors near Bethlehem, heading south, because, the chapter says, they were afraid.

Now chapter forty-two, the heart of today's reading. Everyone comes, from the least to the greatest, and says, pray to the Lord your God for us, that he may show us the way we should go. Jeremiah answers, I have heard you. I will keep nothing back. Then they swear. May the Lord be a true and faithful witness against us if we do not act according to all the word the Lord sends. Whether it is good or bad, we will obey the voice of the Lord our God.

Calvin noticed that the words translated we will obey are literally we will hear the voice. In his Latin rendering he adds a note that to hear the voice and to obey the voice come to the same thing.

Then verse seven. At the end of ten days the word of the Lord came to Jeremiah. Why ten days? Henry says the delay showed that Jeremiah could not produce a word from God whenever he liked. Calvin says God waited so the answer would carry more weight. There may be another reason. Ten days is long enough to find out what is really in your heart.

The answer is kind. If you will remain in this land, then I will build you up and not pull you down; I will plant you, and not pluck you up. Those are the words of Jeremiah's call in chapter one, to pluck up and break down, to build and to plant. For forty years the first half has been happening. Now God offers the second half, in the ruins. Do not fear the king of Babylon, for I am with you. But if you go to Egypt, where you think you will see no war and hear no trumpet, then the sword that you fear shall overtake you there. What they run from will run after them.

Now the hard question. Were they sincere when they asked? Verse twenty answers. The English Standard Version says, you have gone astray at the cost of your lives. The older translations say, you dissembled in your hearts. The Hebrew verb means to wander, and the phrase is literally against your own souls. The plan was made before the prayer meeting began.

Calvin has a paragraph on this that I think every Christian should read once a year. "He who asks counsel, ought first to see that he bring no prejudice, but be free and honest: but it is, however, a fault too common, that men deliberate and ask counsel, when they have already settled what to do; nay, nothing is more common than this; for those who consult do not, for the most part, wish to learn what is right, but that others should fall in with their own inclinations."

We pray about a decision we already made, and call it seeking the Lord's will. And they were not short of evidence. As a Ligonier devotional on this chapter puts it, they had just watched the word of the Lord come true with their own eyes when Jerusalem fell, and still they did not trust him.

Chapter forty-three. Jeremiah finishes, and Johanan and the insolent men say, you are telling a lie. The Lord did not send you. Baruch has set you against us. They cannot say, God is wrong, so they say, God did not say it. Matthew Poole saw how familiar that move is. "Because it had been downright atheism, and a disclaiming of God, to have said they knew better what to do than God could tell them, they only tell the prophet God had not sent him."

Then the narrator gives his verdict twice. Verse four, they did not obey the voice of the Lord. Verse seven, they came into the land of Egypt, for they did not obey the voice of the Lord. The oath, we will obey the voice, is answered word for word.

They drag Jeremiah and Baruch along, to Tahpanhes on Egypt's eastern frontier, where Pharaoh had a palace. In 1886 Flinders Petrie dug there and found a large brick platform before the fortress, a ruin the locals called the castle of the Jew's daughter. He thought it highly probable this was the very pavement of verse nine. That matters, because there God tells Jeremiah to bury large stones in the pavement at Pharaoh's door and say, Nebuchadnezzar my servant will set his throne above these stones. They ran to Egypt to escape Nebuchadnezzar, and God lays the foundation of his throne at their new front door. My servant, Calvin explains, because Nebuchadnezzar carried out what God decreed, though he never meant to serve him. And a fragment of his own annals records a campaign against Egypt around 568 B.C.

What became of Jeremiah? He preaches once more in Egypt, in the chapter we read next time, and then the record stops. The early church kept a tradition that his own people killed him. Tertullian lists him among the persecuted prophets, Jeremiah stoned. Jerome writes, "Jeremiah announced the captivity and was stoned by the people." Scripture does not confirm it, so hold it loosely.

So here is the one thing to take from these chapters. The remnant did not lack guidance. They lacked a willingness to be guided. They prayed, waited ten days, and swore an oath, and the whole time their faces were set toward Egypt. Before you ask God about the decision in front of you, ask yourself a harder question. Am I willing for either answer? If you are not, tell God honestly. Lord, I do not want what you might say. Make me willing. That is a better prayer than a fake oath. Ebed-melech never swore anything. He just trusted, and walked out alive.

Ecclesiastes twelve. The Preacher's last chapter begins, Remember also your Creator in the days of your youth. That word also ties it to the end of chapter eleven, where we left off. The Hebrew word for Creator here is plural. Matthew Henry and John Gill both hear in it a hint of the Trinity, and many scholars call it a plural of majesty. Either way, the point is simple. You did not make yourself. Remember the one who did.

Then three times the Preacher says before. Verse one, before the evil days come. Verse two, before the sun and the light and the moon and the stars are darkened. Verse six, before the silver cord is snapped. The whole poem hangs on that word. There is a deadline.

What follows is a picture of a great house shutting down. The keepers of the house tremble, the strong men are bent, the grinders cease because they are few, the windows grow dim, the doors to the street are shut. Many readers take each image as part of the aging body, the hands, the legs, the teeth, the eyes. That is close, but do not try to crack every line. It is a poem. Read it the way you would walk through an old house as the lights go off room by room.

One image is worth stopping on. The almond tree blossoms white, and most readers hear in it a head of white hair. Gill adds that the Hebrew name for almond comes from a word for haste or wakefulness, because it is the first tree to bloom, and he sends you to Jeremiah chapter one. Hold onto that. We will come back to it.

Then verse six. The silver cord snapped, the golden bowl broken, the pitcher shattered at the fountain, the wheel broken at the cistern. A lamp and a well, the two things a household cannot live without, light and water. And verse seven. The dust returns to the earth as it was, and the spirit returns to God who gave it. That is Genesis two run backwards. God formed man of dust and breathed into him the breath of life. Now the dust goes back to the ground and the breath goes back to God. And then verse eight. Vanity of vanities, says the Preacher, all is vanity. The book began with that sentence. Hevel, a breath.

Matthew Henry is blunt about what this means for a young man. "It is the greatest absurdity and ingratitude imaginable to give the cream and flower of our days to the devil, and reserve the bran, and refuse, and dregs of them for God." Bran is the husk left over after milling. If you are young, give God the flour. If you are not young anymore, give him what is left, today, and do not wait for the bran.

Then the epilogue. The Preacher weighed and studied and arranged many proverbs. The words of the wise are like goads, the sharp stick that keeps an ox moving, and like nails firmly fixed, which hold things in place. Wisdom pushes you forward and holds you steady. And they are given by one Shepherd. Of making many books there is no end. That is a warning that you can read forever and still not obey.

Verse thirteen. The end of the matter; all has been heard. Fear God and keep his commandments, for this is the whole duty of man. The word duty is not in the Hebrew. It says, for this is the whole of man. Augustine quotes this verse in the City of God, and he reads it in exactly that strong sense. "Fear God, he says, and keep His commandments: for this is every man. For whosoever has real existence, is this, is a keeper of God's commandments; and he who is not this, is nothing."

That is Ecclesiastes' answer to hevel. Everything under the sun is breath. A man who fears God is not breath. He is real. Augustine says the Preacher wrote the whole book so that we would long for a life where there is no vanity under the sun.

Practically, remembering your Creator is not a decision you made once at a youth camp. The Hebrew verb zakar means to call to mind and act on it. It is a daily thing. When you wake up stiff, or notice your eyes are not what they were, let it be a reminder, not a resentment. The almond tree is blossoming. Remember him.

John chapter five. After the official in Cana believed the word of Jesus and walked home, John says there was a feast of the Jews, and Jesus went up to Jerusalem. John does not name the feast. Chrysostom and Calvin both guessed Pentecost.

By the Sheep Gate, the gate rebuilt in Nehemiah chapter three, is a pool called Bethesda, with five roofed colonnades. For a long time skeptics said that detail was invented, that John made up five porches to stand for the five books of Moses. Then excavators near the Church of Saint Anne uncovered two large reservoirs separated by a rock wall, with colonnades on the four sides and a fifth across the middle. Five porches. Here is the irony. Augustine himself read the five porches as the five books of Moses. He said the law gathered the sick but could not heal them. Augustine was preaching an allegory, but the porches were real.

The man has been ill for thirty-eight years. Your footnote explains the missing verse four about an angel stirring the water. It is not in the earliest manuscripts. As John Piper says, how the pool worked is not the point. That Jesus worked is.

Jesus sees him and asks, do you want to be healed? It sounds like a strange question. Spurgeon preached on it in 1870 and argued it was not strange at all. Perhaps, he said, the paralysis of the man's body had spread to his will. Spurgeon says the bow had been bent so long that it had lost its spring. Then he turns it on his hearers. Plenty want heaven, he says. Fewer want holiness. Their real wish fits in one line. "We would fain breakfast with the devil, and sup with Christ." Fain means gladly.

Notice that the man never actually says yes. He says, Sir, I have no one to put me into the pool. Chrysostom admired him for that answer. He did not curse or mock. He told the stranger his trouble gently and kept waiting. And Chrysostom turned it on his own people. "Thirty and eight years had that man been waiting without obtaining what he desired, and withdrew not. And he had failed not through any carelessness of his own, but through being oppressed and suffering violence from others, and not even thus did he grow dull; while we if we have persisted for ten days to pray for anything and have not obtained it, are too slothful afterwards to employ the same zeal."

Ten days. That was the remnant's waiting period at Geruth Chimham. They waited ten days and did not like the answer. This man waited thirty-eight years and got one.

Jesus says, get up, take up your bed, and walk. At once he does. Now that day was the Sabbath, and the authorities stop him. It is not lawful for you to take up your bed. Calvin points to the text they had in mind, and it is in Jeremiah, chapter seventeen, verse twenty-one, do not bear a burden on the Sabbath day. But the healed man has the best possible answer. The man who healed me told me to. The one who could make me walk can tell me what to carry.

Then Jesus withdraws into the crowd. Later he finds the man in the temple. He says, see, you are well. Sin no more, that nothing worse may happen to you. Jesus does not mean that every illness is punishment for a particular sin. He says the opposite in chapter nine about the man born blind. But he does mean that there is something worse than thirty-eight years on a mat, and that is to face God's judgment unrepentant, as he says in verse twenty-nine. Piper sums it up this way. The main issue is holiness, not health.

And then verse fifteen. The man went away and told the Jews that it was Jesus who had healed him. Is that a confession or a betrayal? Chrysostom praised the man's boldness before the authorities a few verses earlier and reads him kindly. Calvin is cooler. He says the man had no solid doctrine or clear light to rely on. John does not tell us his motive, but he does set him beside yesterday's official. That man heard a word and believed it. This man was healed, warned, and went to the authorities. Tomorrow we will hear what Jesus says to them.

First Peter, chapter one, verses seventeen to twenty-five. Last time Peter told us to set our hope fully on grace and to be holy as God is holy. Now he gives two reasons, and the first is that God sees the heart.

Verse seventeen. If you call on him as Father who judges impartially according to each one's deeds, conduct yourselves with fear throughout the time of your exile. The word for exile is paroikia, living somewhere as a resident alien. It is exactly what the remnant in Jeremiah refused to be. They would not live under Babylon, so they ran to Egypt. Peter tells his churches, you are foreigners here, so live carefully before your Father. Calvin says God is not pleased with appearances, for he reads what we are inwardly in our hearts. The remnant's oath was perfect on the outside. God read the inside.

The second reason is the price. Verse eighteen. Knowing that you were ransomed from the futile ways inherited from your forefathers, not with perishable things such as silver or gold, but with the precious blood of Christ, like that of a lamb without blemish or spot. The verb ransomed is lutroo, to buy someone out of slavery. Futile is mataios, empty, from the same Greek family the old Greek translation of Ecclesiastes uses for hevel, vanity of vanities. Calvin's phrase for the life we inherit apart from Christ is unforgettable. He says Peter mentions it "in order that we might know that the whole life of man, until he is converted to Christ, is a ruinous labyrinth of wanderings."

You saw an inherited futile way in Jeremiah today. When Judah was frightened, it ran to Egypt. Isaiah condemned it, Jeremiah condemned it, and the remnant did it anyway. It was in their blood. Only better blood buys you out of what your fathers handed down.

Gregory of Nazianzus, in the fourth century, asked a question most people never think to ask. If Christ's blood was a ransom, to whom was it paid? Not to the devil, he says, as if the thief deserved payment. And the Father, he says, accepted the sacrifice, without asking for it or demanding it, as the way his Son would set us free. The ransom picture tells you how costly your freedom was. Do not push it further than Scripture does.

Verse twenty. Christ was foreknown before the foundation of the world but was made manifest in the last times for your sake. Verse twenty-one. Who through him are believers in God, who raised him from the dead and gave him glory, so that your faith and hope are in God.

Polycarp, bishop of Smyrna, who as a young man knew the apostle John, wrote to the church at Philippi early in the second century. "Wherefore, girding up your loins, serve the Lord in fear and truth, as those who have forsaken the vain, empty talk and error of the multitude, and believed in Him who raised up our Lord Jesus Christ from the dead, and gave Him glory." That is First Peter one, verses thirteen and twenty-one, within a generation or two of Peter's death.

Then verse twenty-two. Having purified your souls by your obedience to the truth for a sincere brotherly love, love one another earnestly from a pure heart. Sincere is literally unhypocritical. That is exactly what the remnant's prayer meeting lacked.

And the ground of it, verse twenty-three. Since you have been born again, not of perishable seed but of imperishable, through the living and abiding word of God. Then Peter quotes Isaiah forty. All flesh is like grass and all its glory like the flower of grass. The grass withers, and the flower falls, but the word of the Lord remains forever. Isaiah forty is the chapter that begins, comfort, comfort my people. It was written to exiles to tell them the word of God outlasts empires. Peter hands it to new exiles.

John Piper noticed that Peter uses the idea of imperishability three times in this chapter. The inheritance is imperishable. The ransom was not perishable silver or gold. The seed is imperishable. And about the ransom he says, "The blood of Christ is of infinite value, and therefore its value never runs out." That is why the new life cannot be bought back into slavery. And Peter ends, this word is the good news that was preached to you.

Two connections before we go.

First, the almond. In Ecclesiastes twelve, the almond tree blossoms. Gill sent us to Jeremiah chapter one, and here is why. At his call, Jeremiah saw an almond branch, in Hebrew shaqed. God said, you have seen well, for I am watching over my word to perform it. The word for watching is shoqed. The almond is the waking tree, the first one up in spring. Today, in Jeremiah thirty-nine, you watched God perform his word, to the day and the month. The almond in Ecclesiastes tells you your hair is going white. The almond in Jeremiah tells you God's word is wide awake. You will wither. The word will not. And that is exactly what Peter quotes from Isaiah. The grass withers, but the word of the Lord remains forever.

Second, look at what God sees. The remnant's words in Jeremiah forty-two were flawless. Pray for us, show us the way, whether good or bad we will obey. But verse twenty says they had gone astray against their own souls. Peter says the Father judges impartially, and Calvin says he reads what we are inwardly. The man at the pool did not even manage to say yes, and Jesus healed him anyway, because Jesus looks at the need, not the speech.

So here is your question for the day. Is there a decision you have been praying about while your bags are already packed for Egypt?
