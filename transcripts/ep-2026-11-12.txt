Ezekiel thirty-seven, verse four. Prophesy over these bones, and say to them, O dry bones, hear the word of the Lord.

Last time, Ezekiel's congregation loved his voice and ignored every word. Today God hands him a worse congregation. A valley floor covered with bones, bleached and very dry. And this congregation obeys.

So what does it take for the dead to hear?

This is Old Paths. Today is day fourteen of the eleventh month in the reading plan, day two hundred sixty-four of three hundred. The readings are Ezekiel chapters thirty-five through thirty-seven, Job chapter thirteen, John chapter eleven, verses one through sixteen, and First John chapter three, verses one through ten.

Yesterday God promised, my servant David shall be their shepherd. Today that promise comes back with a crown and a covenant. Ezekiel's mouth has been open since the fugitive arrived, and now he uses it for the most hopeful sermons in his book. And in John, Jesus turns toward a grave.

Ezekiel thirty-five through thirty-seven.

The news that Jerusalem had fallen reached the exiles in about January of 585 B.C. These chapters carry no date, but they belong to that first dark season after the news. They lay the foundation for the book's long turn toward hope.

Chapter thirty-five judges Edom, already condemned back in chapter twenty-five. Why return to it? Look at how the chapters pair. Chapter thirty-five says, set your face against Mount Seir, Edom's mountain country. Chapter thirty-six opens, prophesy to the mountains of Israel. Two mountain ranges, two futures. Seir will become a perpetual desolation. Israel's mountains will shoot forth their branches.

Edom's crime is spelled out in verse five. You cherished perpetual enmity and gave over the people of Israel to the power of the sword at the time of their calamity. Esau's grudge against Jacob had outlived both brothers by more than a thousand years. Psalm one hundred thirty-seven remembers the Edomites shouting on the day Jerusalem fell, lay it bare, lay it bare, down to its foundations. And a letter on a potsherd from the fortress of Arad, in Judah's last years, moves troops south with a warning most translators read as, lest Edom should come there.

Then verse ten. Edom said, these two nations and these two countries shall be mine, and we will take possession of them, although the Lord was there. Edom saw an empty land waiting for a new owner. God says, I never left. The temple was ash, but the title deed was still in his hand.

Matthew Henry draws out the lesson from Edom's gloating, and it is a lesson for us. Even those that suffer justly, and for their sins, are yet to be pitied and not trampled upon. If the father corrects one child, he expects the rest should tremble at it, not triumph in it.

Chapter thirty-six. God promises the mocked land fruit, rebuilt cities, and flocks of people. Then verse twenty-two lands like a hammer. It is not for your sake, O house of Israel, that I am about to act, but for the sake of my holy name, which you have profaned among the nations to which you came. The nations looked at the refugees and said, these are the people of the Lord, and yet they had to go out of his land.

So God will act for his name. And listen to what he does. Verse twenty-five. I will sprinkle clean water on you, and you shall be clean. Verse twenty-six. And I will give you a new heart, and a new spirit I will put within you. And I will remove the heart of stone from your flesh and give you a heart of flesh. Verse twenty-seven. And I will put my Spirit within you, and cause you to walk in my statutes.

The Hebrew word for heart is lev, and in the Old Testament the heart is not mainly the seat of your feelings. It is the control room, where you think and choose and love. A heart of stone, lev ha'even, cannot be argued into life. So count the verbs. I will sprinkle, I will give, I will remove, I will put, I will cause. In these verses Israel does nothing but receive.

A Ligonier Tabletalk devotional on this passage puts the theological point sharply. In other words, we do not get a new heart as a consequence of faith but faith is a consequence of us having been given a new heart. R. C. Sproul liked to compress that into three words. Regeneration precedes faith. Our own Westminster Confession, in chapter ten on effectual calling, borrows Ezekiel's very language when it speaks of God taking away the heart of stone and giving a heart of flesh.

Jesus himself makes the New Testament connection. When Nicodemus came at night, Jesus told him that he must be born of water and the Spirit. Then he asked, are you the teacher of Israel and yet you do not understand these things? A teacher of Israel should have known Ezekiel thirty-six.

Then chapter thirty-seven shows the same truth as a picture. The hand of the Lord sets Ezekiel down in the valley. It is full of bones. Very many, and very dry. And God asks, son of man, can these bones live? Ezekiel's answer is a masterpiece of honesty. O Lord God, you know. He will not say no, and he cannot say yes.

Then God tells him to preach. To bones. He does, and there is a rattling, bone to its bone, then sinews, flesh, skin. But verse eight. There was no breath in them. So a second command. Prophesy to the breath. Come from the four winds, O breath, and breathe on these slain, that they may live.

Here is the key word of the chapter. Ruach. It means breath, and wind, and spirit, and Ezekiel plays all three notes. It appears ten times in the first fourteen verses. The four winds are ruach. The breath that enters the bodies is ruach. And in verse fourteen, when God says, I will put my Spirit within you, that is ruach too. As Keil and the ancient bishop Theodoret both noticed, the two stages follow Genesis two. First the body is formed, then the breath. This is a new creation.

Now the hardest question in these chapters. Is this vision about the resurrection of the body, or about the restoration of the nation?

Verse eleven seems to settle it. These bones are the whole house of Israel. The exiles were saying, our bones are dried up, and our hope is lost. Keil insists the vision pictures the raising of a slain nation, not the general resurrection, and he points to the word slain in verse nine. But the early church read this as a resurrection text, and Tertullian, writing around the year 200, argued hard against those who called it only a picture of Israel. His point is simple. If God uses bones coming to life as his image, then God must be able to do it. Vacuity is not a consistent basis for a similitude, nor does nonentity form a suitable foundation for a parable. In plain English, you cannot build a comparison out of something that does not exist.

Here is the surprise. Keil, after arguing for the national reading, admits that the fathers were quite correct to treat this as a classic text on the resurrection of the dead. And Spurgeon, preaching on these verses in August of 1882, held both together in one sentence. It is not, however, a mere figure: it is a parable based upon a remarkable representation of the resurrection of the dead.

Here is where I land. The first meaning is the nation coming home from Babylon, as verse eleven says. But the picture would be empty unless God truly raises the dead, and within a generation Daniel will say it outright. Many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake.

Then the two sticks. Ezekiel writes Judah on one and Joseph on the other, and holds them as one in his hand. The kingdom that split after Solomon, more than three hundred years earlier, will be one people again under one king. Verse twenty-four. My servant David shall be king over them, and they shall all have one shepherd. That is yesterday's promise, now with a crown. And verse twenty-seven. My dwelling place shall be with them. The glory of God left the temple in chapter eleven. Ezekiel is telling us it is coming back.

So bring it home. Spurgeon divided verse eleven in two. Our bones are dried up. That, he said, is a true word. Our hope is lost. That is an ill word, and it goes beyond the truth. You may be right about how dry your bones are. A cold prayer life, a child far from God. You are not right that hope is lost. God's question to you is the one he asked Ezekiel. Can these bones live? And the honest answer is still, O Lord God, you know. Then keep speaking his word over the dry places, and keep asking for breath.

Job chapter thirteen.

Job is still answering Zophar, in the middle of the long speech that runs from chapter twelve through fourteen. Chapter thirteen falls into two halves. In verses one through nineteen he speaks to his friends. In verses twenty through twenty-eight he turns and speaks to God.

To his friends he is blunt. Verse four. Worthless physicians are you all. Then verse seven. Will you speak falsely for God and speak deceitfully for him? The friends think they are defending God. Job says they are lying on his behalf, and God does not need a lie.

Then verse fifteen, the most famous line in the book. Though he slay me, I will hope in him.

But look at the footnote in your ESV. Or, behold, he will slay me; I have no hope. Here is why. In the written Hebrew text the little word lo is spelled to mean not. In the reading the scribes marked in the margin it is spelled to mean to him. The two sound exactly alike. So is Job saying, I will hope in him, or, I have no hope?

Ellicott prefers I have no hope, and admits we lose a beautiful line. The Pulpit Commentary answers that Job cannot say I have no hope in verse fifteen and then, in verse sixteen, this will be my salvation. Keil reads it, I wait for him, and calls the familiar rendering thoroughly like Job, close to the answer of the whole book. I think the traditional reading is right, and the very next verse is the reason.

Then Job turns to God. Withdraw your hand far from me. Make me know my transgression. Why do you hide your face? And verse twenty-five. Will you frighten a driven leaf and pursue dry chaff?

Here is the practical wisdom. Job's raw honesty in prayer is better than his friends' tidy theology. He brings his complaint to God's face, not away from it. If you must argue, argue with God, in his presence, with his promise in your hand.

John chapter eleven, verses one through sixteen.

At the end of chapter ten Jesus escaped a stoning in Jerusalem and went back across the Jordan. Now a message comes from Bethany, a village less than two miles from Jerusalem. Lord, he whom you love is ill. Notice that the sisters do not even ask him to come. They tell him who is sick and leave the rest to his love.

Then the hardest sentence in the passage, verses five and six. Now Jesus loved Martha and her sister and Lazarus. So, when he heard that Lazarus was ill, he stayed two days longer where he was. Not although he loved them. So. He loved them, and therefore he waited. Calvin writes, we are taught by this delay on his part, that we ought not to judge of the love of God from the condition which we see before our eyes.

Then Thomas. Let us also go, that we may die with him. Is that courage or despair? Matthew Henry hears courage, a readiness to die with his master, even though his faith was weak. Chrysostom hears fear. The expression is rather one of cowardice, he says. But then he adds what happened next. Afterwards he became stronger than all, and invincible. Either way, Thomas walks toward Judea. Sometimes faithfulness is reluctant feet going where Jesus goes.

First John chapter three, verses one through ten.

Last time John closed chapter two by saying that everyone who practices righteousness has been born of him. Now he stops to marvel at that birth. See what kind of love the Father has given to us, that we should be called children of God; and so we are.

The Puritan Thomas Watson, in his Body of Divinity, shows how God's adoption differs from ours. When a man adopts another for his son and heir, he may put his name upon him, but he cannot put his disposition into him. Then Watson goes on. Whom God adopts he sanctifies; he not only gives a new name but a new nature.

That is the logic of this chapter. Verse two. We shall be like him, because we shall see him as he is. Verse three. Everyone who thus hopes in him purifies himself. Augustine preached on these verses and said, the whole life of a good Christian is an holy desire. He compared it to stretching open a sack so it can hold more. God delays what he has promised in order to stretch your desire, so that when he comes you can hold more of him.

Then verse nine, which has troubled tender consciences. No one born of God makes a practice of sinning, for God's seed abides in him. The phrase makes a practice catches John's present tense. He is not teaching that Christians never sin. He already said in chapter one, if we say we have no sin, we deceive ourselves. Calvin explains that God's children still sin daily, as far as remnants of the old nature remain, but the Spirit rules in them so that sin cannot reign. And to prove it, Calvin appeals to God's promise to give his children a new heart, so that they may walk in his commandments. He is reaching for Ezekiel thirty-six.

That is today's connection, and Calvin found it before I did. Ezekiel saw a valley of bones and heard God promise a new heart and his own Spirit. John says those born of God carry his seed and cannot go on living as the devil's children. Job, with every reason to quit, says, though he slay me, I will hope in him. And Jesus walks toward a tomb and calls the dead man asleep. Life comes to the dead by God's word and God's breath.

So carry this question through the day. Where have you said, my hope is lost, when the truer answer is, O Lord God, you know?
