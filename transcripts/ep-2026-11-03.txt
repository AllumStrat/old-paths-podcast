A canal in Babylon. A group of exiles sitting in the evening, and somebody says the line everybody has been saying for months. The fathers have eaten sour grapes, and the children's teeth are set on edge. People nod. It is clever. It is short. It explains everything. Our parents sinned, and we are paying for it.

Then a prophet who has not spoken a casual word in a year opens his mouth, and God answers the proverb with a question.

Why will you die, O house of Israel?

Not why did your fathers die. Why will you?

So here is the question to carry into today. When your life goes wrong, whose fault is it?

This is Old Paths. Today is day six of the eleventh month in the reading plan, day two hundred fifty-six of three hundred. The readings are Ezekiel chapters seventeen through nineteen, Job chapter six, John chapter eight, verses thirty-one through forty-seven, and Second Peter chapter three, verses one through nine.

Last time we stood in a field in Ezekiel sixteen and heard God speak one word to a baby lying in her own blood. Live. She could not do anything. She could not even cry for help. God simply spoke, and she lived.

Today the same verb comes back, but it sounds different. In Ezekiel eighteen, verse thirty-two, God says, so turn, and live. Now there is a command attached. Now the people have to do something. And one verse earlier he says something stranger still. Make yourselves a new heart. But back in chapter eleven, God promised to give them a new heart himself. So which is it? Does God give it, or do we make it? That is the hard question of the day, and the church fathers wrestled with it long before we did.

Two smaller threads come back too. In Job six you will meet the word tafel, tasteless, the same word Ezekiel used for whitewash on a crumbling wall. And in Second Peter, the scoffers ask, where is the promise of his coming? That is the same complaint we heard in Ezekiel twelve. The days grow long, and every vision comes to nothing. Peter is about to answer it.

Ezekiel seventeen through nineteen.

We are still inside the same block of time. It started at chapter eight, verse one, in late summer of 592 B.C., and it runs to the next date at chapter twenty, verse one, in August of 591. Ezekiel is in Tel-abib by the Chebar canal, still silent except when God opens his mouth.

Zedekiah is king in Jerusalem, put on the throne by Nebuchadnezzar in 597, when the young king Jehoiachin was hauled off to Babylon. And here is the detail that matters. Second Chronicles thirty-six says Nebuchadnezzar made Zedekiah swear by God. A vassal oath, sworn in the Lord's name. Meanwhile Egypt keeps whispering that Babylon can be beaten.

Think of these chapters as one story told three ways. Chapter sixteen told it as a marriage. Chapter seventeen tells it as politics. Chapter eighteen answers the people's excuse, and chapter nineteen is a funeral song. The hinge is a phrase. Sixteen fifty-nine said, you who have despised the oath in breaking the covenant. Seventeen eighteen says the same words about Zedekiah.

Verse two. Propound a riddle, and speak a parable. Two Hebrew words. Chidah, a riddle, the word used for Samson's riddle. And mashal, a parable or proverb. Hold on to that second word, because in chapter eighteen the people have a mashal of their own, and God is going to take it away.

The riddle. A great eagle, rich in plumage of many colors, came to Lebanon and took the top of the cedar, and carried the topmost twig to a land of trade, a city of merchants. That is Nebuchadnezzar, and the twig is Jehoiachin.

And we know what happened to that twig. Near the Ishtar Gate in Babylon, archaeologists dug up clay ration lists from Nebuchadnezzar's storerooms. So much oil for craftsmen from Byblos, so much for Greek workmen, and so much for Ya'u-kinu, king of the land of Yahud, and his five sons. Jehoiachin, still called king, drawing oil in a city of merchants. Exactly as the riddle says.

Then the eagle planted the seed of the land, and it became a low spreading vine with its branches turned toward him. That is Zedekiah. A humble kingdom, but a living one. But verse seven. There was another great eagle, and this vine bent its roots toward him. That is Pharaoh. Verse fifteen gives the plain version. He rebelled by sending his ambassadors to Egypt, that they might give him horses and a large army. Can he break the covenant and yet escape?

Now verse nineteen. As I live, surely it is my oath that he despised, and my covenant that he broke. Why does God call Nebuchadnezzar's treaty his own? Because Zedekiah swore it in God's name. Calvin calls God the guardian of treaties here, and points to Psalm fifteen, the man who swears to his own hurt and does not change. An oath in God's name binds even when it was made to a tyrant.

And notice verse sixteen. In Babylon he shall die. Back in chapter twelve God said of the prince, he shall not see it, though he shall die there. Put them together. He would die in Babylon, and never see Babylon. Five years later, at Riblah, they put out his eyes and led him there in chains.

Then the chapter turns, in verse twenty-two, and the tone changes completely. Thus says the Lord God: I myself will take a sprig from the lofty top of the cedar and will set it out. I will break off from the topmost of its young twigs a tender one, and I myself will plant it on a high and lofty mountain.

Do you hear the I myself? Nebuchadnezzar planted a twig, and it failed. God plants one, and it will not fail. The word for tender is rak, soft, young, weak. It is Isaiah's picture of the shoot from the stump of Jesse, and the tender plant out of dry ground. Who is this sprig? Ellicott's commentary says the Messiah, and notes that nearly all interpreters, Jewish and Christian, have read it that way. Gill notes that even the ancient Aramaic Targum takes it as the Messiah. Calvin, who is careful not to force every promise onto Christ, says this one begins with the return under Zerubbabel and reaches its fullness only in Christ's kingdom. That is the right way to hold it.

And here is our first New Testament move, because Jesus himself reached for this image. Verse twenty-three. It will become a noble cedar, and under it will dwell every kind of bird. In Matthew thirteen, Jesus says the kingdom is like a mustard seed, the smallest of seeds, that becomes a tree so that the birds of the air come and make nests in its branches. The tender twig and the tiny seed are the same promise. God starts his kingdom small on purpose.

Verse twenty-four ends the chapter. I bring low the high tree, and make high the low tree, dry up the green tree, and make the dry tree flourish. That is the theme of this whole block. Proud cedars come down. Dry sticks bloom.

Chapter eighteen.

What do you mean by repeating this proverb concerning the land of Israel, The fathers have eaten sour grapes, and the children's teeth are set on edge?

Jeremiah quotes the same proverb. And a few weeks ago, in Lamentations five, we heard it in plain words. Our fathers sinned, and are no more; and we bear their iniquities. The prophets had said Manasseh's sins filled the cup. The people turned that into a shrug. We are being punished for our grandparents.

God answers with an oath. Verse four. Behold, all souls are mine; the soul of the father as well as the soul of the son is mine: the soul who sins shall die. Your father does not own your soul, and he cannot spend it for you.

Then Ezekiel builds a case across three generations. A righteous man, who does not worship at the hill shrines, restores the pledge to the poor man, gives bread to the hungry, covers the naked. He shall surely live. His son, violent, a shedder of blood. He shall not live. Then a grandson who sees his father's sins and does not do them. He shall not die for his father's iniquity.

Matthew Henry, the Presbyterian commentator, looks at that middle generation, the wicked son of a godly father, and writes, "by it we see that grace does not run in the blood, nor always attend the means of grace." Parents are bound to raise their children well, he says, but God is not bound to bless it. That is sobering for every father, and freeing too. Your children's souls belong to God.

Does this contradict Exodus twenty, where God visits the iniquity of the fathers on the children? Read to the end of the clause. Of those who hate me. Poole and Gill both say it. God visits the fathers' sin on children who take it up. And Calvin adds that even with Adam, we are not condemned as innocent people. His corruption became ours. Nobody stands before God as a pure victim of someone else's sin.

Verses twenty-one through twenty-nine turn the principle inward. If a wicked person turns away from all his sins, he shall surely live, and none of his transgressions shall be remembered against him. And a righteous person who turns away to wickedness will not be saved by his old record. Is this salvation by works? No. Verse nine describes a man who keeps God's rules by acting faithfully, a covenant man. And verse twenty-two is pure grace. God forgets the past of the one who turns. John Trapp, the Puritan, puts verse twenty-six memorably. "It is not falling into the water that drowneth a man, but long lying under it."

Twice the people answer back. The way of the Lord is not just. And twice God throws it back. Is it not your ways that are not just? That is the heart of the chapter. The proverb was never really about the fathers. It was a way to accuse God.

And then the appeal. Verses thirty through thirty-two. Repent and turn from all your transgressions, lest iniquity be your ruin. Cast away from you all the transgressions that you have committed, and make yourselves a new heart and a new spirit! Why will you die, O house of Israel? For I have no pleasure in the death of anyone, declares the Lord God; so turn, and live.

The key word is shuv, turn. It runs all through the chapter. And here is our hard question.

In chapter eleven God promised, I will give them one heart, and a new spirit I will put within them. In chapter thirty-six he will say it again. Yet here he says, make yourselves a new heart. And in verse thirty-two he says he takes no pleasure in anyone's death, yet we know that not everyone turns. So is God commanding something people cannot do? And does he want something he does not bring about?

Augustine faced exactly this, writing against the Pelagians, who said that if God commands it, we must be able to do it. He put the two verses side by side and asked, "How is it, then, that He who says, Make you, also says, I will give you? Why does He command, if He is to give? Why does He give if man is to make, except it be that He gives what He commands when He helps him to obey whom He commands?"

He gives what he commands. The command does not measure our strength. It shows our duty, and when we see we cannot do it, it drives us to ask. Calvin says the same: when God teaches what is right, he shows what we ought to do, not what we are able to do. That is why David prays in Psalm fifty-one, create in me a clean heart. The man who prays that has stopped pretending he can make one. The Westminster Confession, in chapter ten, even borrows Ezekiel's language. God takes away the heart of stone and gives a heart of flesh, and yet his people come most freely, being made willing by his grace.

What about no pleasure in the death of anyone? Calvin says the prophet is not opening God's secret counsel. He is speaking God's revealed will, which calls every sinner to turn and promises that no one who turns will be refused. And then he says this about verse thirty-one. "Here God takes up the character of a mourner, saying, Why will ye die, O house of Israel?"

A mourner. Not a hangman.

Spurgeon preached on these verses in August 1884. He noticed Ezekiel says it three times, as a question in eighteen twenty-three, a statement in eighteen thirty-two, and an oath in thirty-three eleven. Then he reached for a courtroom. A judge who enjoyed hanging men should be removed from the bench. A judge who went home after every death sentence and sat in silence all evening, that is the man you want. "Aversion to punishment is necessary to justice in a judge."

R. C. Sproul, a century later, reached for the very same picture, a judge whose own son stands guilty before him, and who sentences him with tears. Sproul put it this way. "God doesn't take any pleasure in the death of the wicked. Nevertheless, though He doesn't enjoy it, as it were, He still decrees it." A Baptist in London and a Presbyterian in Florida, landing on the same image. God's grief is real. God's justice is real. He will not lie about either one.

Chapter nineteen is a song. Take up a lamentation for the princes of Israel. The word is qinah, a funeral dirge, often written with a limping beat, a long line and then a short one, like a mourner's steps. Ezekiel writes the funeral song for David's house while the patient is still breathing.

What was your mother? A lioness! Her first cub devoured men, and the nations brought him with hooks to Egypt. That is Jehoahaz, who reigned three months in 609 before Pharaoh Neco dragged him away. Her second cub they put in a cage and brought to the king of Babylon. Gill thinks that is Jehoiakim. Keil says Jehoiachin, and I think Keil is right, because Jehoiachin actually arrived in Babylon. We just saw his oil ration.

Then the mother becomes a vine whose strong stems were rulers' scepters. Plucked up in fury, planted in the wilderness. And verse fourteen. Fire has gone out from the stem of its shoots, so that there remains in it no strong stem, no scepter for ruling. Calvin sees Zedekiah there. The fire came out of the vine's own branch. His broken oath set the whole vine burning.

No scepter. Jacob had promised in Genesis forty-nine that the scepter would not depart from Judah, and now Ezekiel sings that there is none left. But you have already heard the answer, the tender sprig on the high mountain. And in chapter twenty-one God will say the crown is overturned until he comes whose right it is. The scepter is not lost. It is waiting for its king.

This is a lamentation and has become a lamentation. Ezekiel wrote it as prophecy, and within five years it was history.

So what do you do with Ezekiel eighteen this morning? Kill the proverb in your own mouth. Every one of us has a version. My father was cold, so I am cold. My church was harsh, so I am bitter. Some of that history is real, and God knows it better than you do. But he will not let you use it as a verdict. All souls are mine. Find the sentence you use to explain why you cannot change, and hear God answer it. Why will you die? Turn, and live. Then ask him for the heart he commands, because he gives it.

Job chapter six.

Last time Eliphaz finished his long speech. Now Job answers. The chapter has four movements. The weight of his grief, in verses one through seven. His wish to die, in eight through thirteen. His friends as a dry riverbed, in fourteen through twenty-three. And a plea, in twenty-four through thirty.

He starts with honesty. Oh that my vexation were weighed! It would be heavier than the sand of the sea; therefore my words have been rash. He does not defend every word of chapter three. He says, weigh what is on the other side of the scale before you weigh my words.

Verse four. For the arrows of the Almighty are in me; my spirit drinks their poison. Notice whom Job blames. Not the Sabeans. Not the Chaldeans. Not Satan, whom he never sees. Shaddai, the Almighty. Job will say things in this book that he later repents of, but he is right that his suffering comes finally from God's hand.

Then two pictures from the farm. Does the wild donkey bray when he has grass, or the ox low over his fodder? Animals do not complain when they are fed. My groaning is not a sign of bad character. It is a sign that I am starving.

And verse six. Can that which is tasteless be eaten without salt? There is our word. Tafel. In Ezekiel thirteen it was the whitewash that made a flimsy wall look solid. Here it is food with no flavor. And Job means Eliphaz's speech. Spurgeon preached on this verse in 1883, using the old King James phrase, the white of an egg, and he described the friends as men "who inflamed his wounds by pouring in salt and vinegar instead of oil and wine." Matthew Henry, nearly two centuries earlier, used almost the same words, vinegar instead of oil. Put Ezekiel and Job side by side and you have two failures of the same kind. False prophets put whitewash on a wall that was about to fall. Job's friends serve tasteless food to a man who is starving. One is too soft, and the other is too dry, and neither one tells the truth with love.

Verses eight through thirteen are Job's darkest wish. Let God crush me. But then a strange comfort in verse ten. I would even exult in pain unsparing, for I have not denied the words of the Holy One. Whatever else he has lost, he has not betrayed God. That is a clear conscience, not a claim to sinlessness.

Then verse fourteen, the hinge of the chapter. He who withholds kindness from a friend forsakes the fear of the Almighty. The word for kindness is chesed, steadfast covenant love. The Hebrew of this verse is hard, and your footnote says so. It may mean that a man who withholds chesed has abandoned the fear of God. Or it may mean that a despairing man needs chesed from his friend, or he may abandon the fear of God. Either way, Job is saying that friendship is a matter of godliness.

And then one of the great pictures in the Bible. My brothers are treacherous as a torrent-bed. In winter those desert wadis roar with meltwater, dark with ice. In summer, when it is hot, they vanish. And the caravans of Tema and Sheba, crossing the Arabian desert, turn aside to the place where they remember water. They come there and are disappointed. They die in the sand. Imagine driving a desert highway at night with the needle on empty, and the sign says gas in twenty miles. You coast in on fumes, and the station has been boarded up for years. That is what Job's friends were. Loud in the good season, dry in the hot one.

Verse twenty-one is the honest diagnosis. You see my calamity and are afraid. Why did the friends lecture instead of weep? Because Job's suffering frightened them. If this could happen to a righteous man, it could happen to them. So they needed it to be his fault.

Matthew Henry turns the passage around and says, "God will out-do our hopes as much as men come short of them." The wadi fails. The fountain of living water does not.

The chapter ends with a plea. Teach me, and I will be silent. Do you think that you can reprove words, when the speech of a despairing man is wind? Here is the practical wisdom. When someone you love is in the dark and says wild things, do not correct their grammar. Do not build a theology lecture on the words of a man in pain. Sit with him. Show chesed. Be water in August.

John chapter eight, verses thirty-one through forty-seven.

We are still at the Feast of Booths, still in the temple treasury. Verse thirty said that as Jesus was saying these things, many believed in him. Verse thirty-one turns to them. So Jesus said to the Jews who had believed him, If you abide in my word, you are truly my disciples, and you will know the truth, and the truth will set you free.

Within fifteen verses those same people are trying to kill him. So what kind of belief was this? Chrysostom says they had believed, but they did not continue. J. C. Ryle, the Anglican bishop, says plainly that this was not faith of the heart. And he writes, "It is not beginning, but continuing a religious profession, that is the test of true grace".

That is why Jesus begins with if. If you abide.

Then the claim that offended them. The truth will set you free. What truth? Here the voices pull in two directions. Chrysostom says the truth is Christ himself. You shall know me, for I am the truth. Ryle disagrees in his notes. He says the truth here cannot mean the person of Christ, but the whole truth about him, his nature, his mission, his gospel. I think in John you cannot pull those apart. The truth about the Son sets you free because it brings you to the Son. Verse thirty-six says it. If the Son sets you free, you will be free indeed.

Their answer is almost comic. We are offspring of Abraham and have never been enslaved to anyone. Never? Egypt. Babylon. And as they speak, Roman soldiers are standing in the Antonia fortress overlooking the temple court. Chrysostom asks why Jesus did not simply point that out, and he answers, "But He desired not to show that they were slaves of men, but that they were slaves of sin, which is the most grievous slavery, from which God alone can deliver".

Verse thirty-four. Everyone who practices sin is a slave to sin. The slave does not remain in the house forever; the son remains forever. That is Genesis twenty-one, where Sarah says, cast out the slave woman and her son. Ishmael was Abraham's son by blood, but he did not stay in the house. Jesus is telling them that descent from Abraham does not put you in the house.

So they fall back on their pedigree. Abraham is our father. And Jesus answers, verse thirty-nine. If you were Abraham's children, you would be doing the works Abraham did. Abraham welcomed God's messengers. You are trying to kill one.

Then they reach higher. We were not born of sexual immorality. We have one Father, even God. Some older interpreters heard an ugly jab at the rumors about Jesus' birth. Others, like the Jamieson, Fausset and Brown commentary, think they mean, we are not idolaters, using exactly the prophets' picture of idolatry as harlotry that we heard in Ezekiel sixteen. Either way, Jesus' answer is devastating. If God were your Father, you would love me.

And verse forty-four. You are of your father the devil. He was a murderer from the beginning, and does not stand in the truth. Augustine points out that the devil did not murder Adam with a sword. He spoke a lie to him, and the lie killed him. That is why a liar and a murderer are the same person in this verse. And that is why these men, who will not hear the truth, are reaching for stones.

Verse forty-seven closes it. Whoever is of God hears the words of God. The reason why you do not hear them is that you are not of God. Hearing is the evidence of birth. Ryle has one more line on this passage that belongs here. Time and wear, he says, test metals and show whether they are solid or only plated. The question for you is not whether you once said yes. It is whether you are still listening.

Second Peter chapter three, verses one through nine.

This is Peter's last letter. He told us in chapter one that the putting off of his body would be soon. He is writing probably in the mid sixties of the first century, near the end of Nero's reign. Verse one. This is now the second letter that I am writing to you, beloved. In both of them I am stirring up your sincere mind by way of reminder.

Peter keeps saying remind. He is not giving them new truth. He is guarding old truth. Calvin says that when the warnings stop, even godly minds grow dim, as if they gathered rust. Repetition is not a sign that preaching has run out of ideas. It is how truth stays sharp.

Then, verse three, the scoffers. They will say, Where is the promise of his coming? For ever since the fathers fell asleep, all things are continuing as they were from the beginning of creation. Listen to that argument. Nothing has changed, so nothing will change. It is the same mood we heard from the exiles in Ezekiel twelve. The days grow long, and every vision comes to nothing. Your Bible's cross-references even link the two verses.

Peter answers with three acts of the same word. By the word of God the heavens existed and the earth was formed out of water. By that water the world that then existed was deluged and perished. And by the same word the present heavens and earth are stored up for fire. The scoffers say all things continue. Peter says, not at the flood they didn't. The world has already ended once.

Then he turns to the believers. Verse eight. Do not overlook this one fact, beloved, that with the Lord one day is as a thousand years, and a thousand years as one day. That is Psalm ninety, the prayer of Moses.

Now, some of the earliest Christians read this as a timetable. Irenaeus, in the second century, reasoned from the six days of creation and wrote, "For in as many days as this world was made, in so many thousand years shall it be concluded." Six days, six thousand years. Spurgeon would have none of it. Preaching on this verse in 1862, he said, "It is certain however, that our text does not teach the doctrine of the Sabbatic seventh thousand years". Peter is not handing us a calendar. He is telling us that God does not measure time the way impatient people do.

And verse nine is the reason for the wait. The Lord is not slow to fulfill his promise as some count slowness, but is patient toward you, not wishing that any should perish, but that all should reach repentance. The word for patient is makrothumei. Long of temper, slow to boil. The delay that looks like weakness to the scoffer is mercy toward the sinner.

But who are the any and the all? Here two good men disagree. John Gill, the Baptist, says the verse means God's elect, the beloved Peter is writing to, not every individual human being. Calvin reads it more widely, and he faces the obvious question head on. "But it may be asked, If God wishes none to perish, why is it that so many do perish? To this my answer is, that no mention is here made of the hidden purpose of God, according to which the reprobate are doomed to their own ruin, but only of his will as made known to us in the gospel."

I side with Calvin here, and you already know why. It is the same distinction he made in Ezekiel eighteen. God's revealed will is a sincere call to every sinner to turn. His secret purpose is his own. Peter is not opening the book of decrees. He is telling scoffers, and believers, why the clock has not run out yet.

Two connections between today's readings, and both are really in the text.

The first is the heart of God. Ezekiel eighteen, verse thirty-two. I have no pleasure in the death of anyone; so turn, and live. Second Peter three, verse nine. Not wishing that any should perish, but that all should reach repentance. Peter is not describing a new attitude in God. Ezekiel heard it first. And it tells you what the delay is. When the exiles said the vision was late, and when the scoffers said the promise had failed, the answer was the same. God is giving you time to turn. Every day the world goes on is another day of that patience.

The second is family. In Ezekiel eighteen, a righteous father cannot carry his violent son into life, and a wicked father cannot drag his righteous son into death. In John eight, Jesus says the same thing to the children of Abraham. If you were Abraham's children, you would be doing the works Abraham did. Your lineage does not save you, and your lineage does not damn you. Not your fathers, not your church, not your upbringing. The soul who sins shall die. The one who turns shall live.

Here is your question for the day. What is the proverb you say about yourself? The one sentence you use to explain why you cannot change? Write it down if you have to. Then put next to it the question God asks in Ezekiel eighteen. Why will you die? And ask him for the new heart he commands.
