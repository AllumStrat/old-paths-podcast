Ezekiel forty-eight, verse thirty-five. And the name of the city from that time on shall be, The Lord Is There.

The book opened with a priest by a canal in Babylon, a long way from home, and the heavens opened over him. It closes with four words. The Lord Is There.

This is Old Paths. Today is day nineteen of the eleventh month in the reading plan, day two hundred sixty-nine of three hundred. The readings are Ezekiel forty-eight, Job seventeen, John twelve, verses twelve through nineteen, and First John five, verses one through twelve.

Last time I said the book of Ezekiel would end with a name, and today we reach it. We watched the glory leave the temple in chapters ten and eleven. We watched it come back through the east gate in chapter forty-three. Today God writes his presence onto the city itself. I also left something on the table yesterday, the foreigners at the end of chapter forty-seven, so we will start there. And in Job, the man who said my witness is in heaven now asks that witness for a pledge.

Ezekiel forty-eight.

We are still inside the vision dated in chapter forty, about 573 B.C., the twenty-fifth year of the exile. Ezekiel was carried off with King Jehoiachin in 597. He saw his first vision about twenty years before this one. Jerusalem has been rubble for fourteen years. And this is the last page he will give us.

Go back to the end of chapter forty-seven. God draws the borders, and Patrick Fairbairn notes they are the old borders of Numbers thirty-four, the land west of the Jordan. Every tribe comes across the river. Then verse fourteen says, you shall divide equally. And then verses twenty-two and twenty-three. The sojourners, the foreigners who have settled among you and raised children, shall be to you as native-born children of Israel. They get an inheritance in whatever tribe they live. Fairbairn asks a fair question. If this were a literal map, where would you even put them? He says the verse makes sense only as a sign that the line between Jew and Gentile would one day be taken away.

Now chapter forty-eight. It is built like a map read from top to bottom. Seven tribes in the north, each one a band of land running from the Jordan to the sea. Dan, Asher, Naphtali, Manasseh, Ephraim, Reuben, Judah. Then a broad strip in the middle. Then five tribes in the south. Benjamin, Simeon, Issachar, Zebulun, Gad.

That middle strip has a name, and it is our Hebrew word for today. Terumah. Your Bible says the portion which you shall set apart. The root means to lift. Keil translates it the heave, the piece lifted off the whole and handed to God, the way a worshipper would lift off part of his harvest. Ellicott figures it at about one fifth of the land. Inside it, the priests' portion, with verse ten saying, the sanctuary of the Lord in the midst of it. Then the Levites. Then a strip for the city, with farms worked by people from all the tribes of Israel. And the prince's land on both sides.

Verse fourteen adds that none of it may be sold or exchanged, for it is holy to the Lord. What has been lifted off for God is not for sale.

Now look at who is placed where, because nothing in this list is careless. Dan was the last tribe to get land under Joshua, and here Dan is named first. But Dan is also at the far northern edge. That was the tribe that set up its own idol in the book of Judges, and John Gill and others note that Revelation seven leaves Dan out altogether. Judah and Benjamin sit right against the holy portion, and Barnes and Fairbairn say that honored the two tribes that stayed with the temple and the house of David. Reuben, the firstborn who lost his rank over his sin with his father's concubine, is brought in from across the Jordan and set right next to Judah. Matthew Henry takes it as a sign that an old disgrace can at last be forgotten. And here is something you can see for yourself in the list. The sons of the two servant women, Dan, Naphtali, Gad, and Asher, are the four tribes at the far ends of the land. Nobody is left out. Everybody has a full share. But everybody lives facing the middle.

And the middle belongs to no tribe. Fairbairn makes this point, and I think it is the key to the chapter. Under the old kings the temple stood in the southern kingdom, and the north built rival altars at Bethel and at Dan. Here the center is nobody's property. It is God's. He says the old jealousies between Judah and Ephraim were meant to end for good.

Now the hard question. Three episodes ago, in chapter forty, I said the temple in this vision is a picture, not a blueprint. Fair enough. But is the land a picture too? Plenty of people want the temple symbolic and the land literal. Fairbairn says you cannot have it both ways. If the land is literal, the sacrifices are literal, and he says splitting them brings complete arbitrariness and confusion into the interpretation of the prophecy. The details push the same direction. Henry says that even the uncertainty about the measuring unit is a hint that this is to be read spiritually. And a Ligonier devotional on this chapter calls the allotments pictures of a simple truth, that the full number of God's people will be saved and none left without an inheritance.

That is where I land. Ezekiel is drawing a whole people, every tribe and even the foreigner, with a full inheritance, gathered around the presence of God, in the only shapes his hearers had. And the New Testament takes up these very gates. In Revelation twenty-one the holy city has twelve gates with the names of the twelve tribes of Israel written on them, and a voice from the throne says, Behold, the dwelling place of God is with man. That is my one New Testament move today, and John is plainly reading Ezekiel when he makes it.

And then the name. Ezekiel never calls this city Jerusalem. Jerome, writing on this verse, as Albert Barnes quotes him, said: The name of the city shall be no longer Jerusalem, the vision of peace, but Adonai shama, the Lord is there, because Yahweh will never again withdraw from it, as He once withdrew, but will hold it as His everlasting possession.

In Hebrew the name is Yahweh shammah. Sham means there. Fairbairn argues that the little ending on shammah means thither, toward that place, so that God's eyes go out from the temple toward the city, and from the city to the whole land. Keil answers that Ezekiel uses this same form simply to mean there, and that the name says God will live and rule in her. I side with Keil on the grammar. But Fairbairn's picture is still true of the vision. The river already showed us that what lives at the center flows out.

Fairbairn ends his commentary by remembering when Ezekiel saw this. The cause of Heaven was then at its lowest ebb. The temple that had been, together with the kingdom it symbolised and represented, were laid in ruins. An exile in Babylon, with his city flattened and his temple burned, writes the last line of his book, and it is not a lament. It is a name. The Lord Is There.

Here is the application. Spurgeon preached on this verse in January 1891. He opened by saying, these words may be used as a test as well as a text. Then he asked whether we reckon the presence of the Lord to be the greatest of blessings. And he gave his people a motto. Our motto is: With God, anywhere. Without God, nowhere.

So take the test. When you think of heaven, is God the center of the picture or the edge of it? When you think of your church, your marriage, your work, what would you miss first? Ezekiel's city has gates, walls, farmland, and a prince. None of that is its name. Its name is the One who lives there.

Job seventeen.

Last time, Job looked up through his tears and said, my witness is in heaven. This chapter starts low. My spirit is broken; my days are extinct; the graveyard is ready for me. Then verse three. Lay down a pledge for me with you; who is there who will put up security for me?

This is courtroom language. Job asks God to put down a bond for him, to stand surety. The Hebrew verb is arav. Barnes explains it as standing in another person's place. It is the same word Judah used in Genesis forty-three, when he told his father, I will be a pledge of his safety, and offered his own life for Benjamin. And Job asks God to do that for him with God. As in chapter sixteen, the Cambridge Bible notes, Job appeals from the God who seems to persecute him to the God who will guarantee his innocence. He is reaching for something he cannot yet name.

Then he sinks again. He has become a byword, one before whom men spit. His eye is dim. And then, out of nowhere, verse nine. Yet the righteous holds to his way, and he who has clean hands grows stronger and stronger. The Cambridge Bible calls the passage perhaps the most surprising and lofty in the Book. A man with nothing left resolves to keep walking.

Gregory the Great says that if earthly goods were truly great, God would not hand them out to his enemies, so the righteous man sets his heart higher. Spurgeon preached on this verse in 1877 and took it as the doctrine of the perseverance of the saints. He said the righteous man will hold on his way with stern determination, as though he held on by his teeth, resolving never to let go. But notice Job did not say he feels strong. He said he grows stronger. It is a resolve, not a mood.

The chapter ends in the dark. If I call the pit my father and the worm my mother, where then is my hope? Two chapters from now, he answers that question himself.

John twelve, verses twelve through nineteen.

The day after the dinner at Bethany, Passover pilgrims hear that Jesus is coming, and they go out to meet him with palm branches. Sproul points out that since the Maccabees, the palm had become a national symbol of military victory. They sing from Psalm one hundred eighteen. Hosanna, which Calvin explains means, Save, I beseech you. Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord, even the King of Israel.

Then Jesus finds a young donkey. John quotes Zechariah, but with a change. Zechariah said, Rejoice greatly. John writes, Fear not, daughter of Zion. Calvin notices the change and says that true joy begins when fear is removed, and fear is never banished except by knowing that Christ reigns among us.

The fathers loved this donkey. Augustine says the colt that had never been ridden stands for the Gentiles. Calvin waves that kind of allegory away and says the plain truth is that Christ rode one animal, brought along with its mother. I am with Calvin on the donkey. But Augustine is wonderful on the King. For the Son of God to be King of Israel, he says, was an act of condescension and not of promotion; a token of compassion, and not any increase of power. For He who was called on earth the King of the Jews, is in the heavens the Lord of angels.

The crowd wanted a different king. Sproul, preaching on this passage, says it should be a lesson to all of us who come to Jesus with our private agendas. We make our demands to Him, and we become disappointed, then angry, and sometimes bitter when He does not do things the way we would like to do them.

Verse sixteen admits the disciples did not understand any of this until after he was glorified. Then the Pharisees say, Look, the world has gone after him. They meant it as an exaggeration. In the very next verse, Greeks arrive asking to see Jesus.

First John five, verses one through twelve.

John gathers up the letter's three tests, believing, loving, and obeying, and ties them in one knot. Everyone who believes that Jesus is the Christ has been born of God, and everyone who loves the Father loves whoever has been born of him. Augustine reads that last phrase as Christ himself, and then as his members. Calvin names Augustine and says no. John means our fellow believers. Love the Father, and you will love his children. I think Calvin has the context.

Verse three. His commandments are not burdensome. Doesn't experience say otherwise? Calvin answers that the trouble is not in the law. It is in our flesh. And he adds that obedience gets lighter because we are forgiven when we fall short.

Then verse four. This is the victory that has overcome the world, our faith. Has overcome, past tense. Calvin says the Lord thus bids his people to be certain, while yet he would not have them to be secure; but on the contrary, he declares that they have already overcome, in order that they may fight more courageously and more strenuously. Certain, but never careless.

Verse six is the hard one. He came by water and blood, not by the water only. Irenaeus tells us that a teacher named Cerinthus said the divine Christ came down on the man Jesus at his baptism and left before the cross. So water, yes. Blood, no. John answers that the same Jesus Christ came through the baptism and through the death. Calvin hears John chapter nineteen in it instead, the water and blood from the pierced side. Either way, John means the whole Christ, all the way to the cross.

And then the end of the argument. Whoever has the Son has life; whoever does not have the Son of God does not have life.

Here is the connection today, and it is real. Every reading asks where God is. Ezekiel names a city, The Lord Is There. In John the Lord rides into the actual city on a donkey, and its rulers want him dead. Job, broken, asks God to stand beside him as his own pledge. And John says God's testimony is that life is in his Son. You do not climb up to God's presence. He comes, and you either have the Son or you do not. Spurgeon, in that same sermon on Job seventeen, reached for First John five, verse four, and said, Now, faith is in itself a conquering principle.

So carry this question through the day. If the Lord were not there, in your home, your church, your heart, how long would it take you to notice?
