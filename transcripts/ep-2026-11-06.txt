Ezekiel twenty-four, verse eighteen. So I spoke to the people in the morning, and at evening my wife died. And on the next morning I did as I was commanded.

Read that again slowly. Morning, a sermon. Evening, a funeral. Next morning, obedience.

There is no complaint in that verse. No explanation either. Just a man who lost the person he loved most and got up the next day to do exactly what God told him to do.

What kind of obedience is that? And what kind of God asks for it?

We will not rush past that question today.

This is Old Paths. Today is day nine of the eleventh month in the reading plan, day two hundred fifty-nine of three hundred. The readings are Ezekiel chapters twenty-four through twenty-six, Job chapter nine, verses one through twenty, John chapter nine, verses thirteen through twenty-five, and First John chapter one, verses five through ten.

Last time God walked the walls of Jerusalem looking for a man to stand in the breach, and found none. We watched Oholibah, Jerusalem, handed her sister's cup. Today the cup is lifted to her lips. Ezekiel twenty-four opens on the day the siege begins.

Two older threads come together here as well. Back in episode sixteen, in chapter eleven, the leaders of Jerusalem had a slogan. This city is the cauldron, and we are the meat. They meant, we are safe in here. Today God picks up their cooking pot and puts it on the fire. And ever since chapter three we have been tracking Ezekiel's strange silence, a prophet who could speak only when God opened his mouth. Today God tells him when that silence will end.

In John, the man born blind is about to be questioned by people who do not want to see. In Job, our friend finally answers Bildad. And in First John, the apostle who told us he touched the Word of life now tells us what that Word said. God is light.

Ezekiel twenty-four.

Verse one gives a date, and verse two tells Ezekiel to write it down. In the ninth year, in the tenth month, on the tenth day of the month. Son of man, write down the name of this day, this very day. The king of Babylon has laid siege to Jerusalem this very day.

That is January of 588 B.C. The same date appears in Second Kings twenty-five, verse one, and after the exile it became a fast day; Zechariah eight mentions the fast of the tenth month. Ezekiel is hundreds of miles away in Babylon, and Ellicott points out that the verse insists he knew it the same day. Matthew Henry adds why that matters. Weeks later, when travelers arrived with the news, the exiles would find the prophet had dated it to the day. If he was right about the news, he was right about what came next.

Then comes a parable, verses three through fourteen. Set on the pot, set it on; pour in water also; put in it the pieces of meat, all the good pieces, the thigh and the shoulder.

Hear the leaders' slogan in that. This city is the cauldron, and we are the meat. Fine, God says. You are the meat. Pile on the logs. Boil it well.

Then the picture turns. Verse six. Woe to the bloody city, to the pot whose corrosion is in it. The Hebrew word for corrosion is chel'ah. Ellicott notes that it appears five times in these verses and nowhere else in the Bible, and the old Greek translators rendered it rust. The problem is not just the meat. The pot itself is eaten through. And the meat comes out piece after piece, without making any choice, literally, no lot falls on it. Nobody is drawn to be spared.

Verse seven tells us why. The blood she has shed is in her midst; she put it on the bare rock; she did not pour it out on the ground to cover it with dust. The law said spilled blood was to be covered with earth. Jerusalem left the blood of her victims on the rock in plain sight. She was not even ashamed. So God says, I have set it on the bare rock myself. You would not cover it. Now I will not let it be covered.

Then the empty pot is set on the coals until the copper glows, to burn the corrosion out. Verse twelve is the saddest line in the parable. Its abundant corrosion does not go out of it.

Gregory the Great, in his book on pastoral care around the year 590, used this verse for people who are struck again and again and never change. His Latin Bible read, her exceeding rust went not out from her, not even by fire. And Gregory says: "For He brings upon us the fire of tribulation, that He may purge us from the rust of vices; but we lose not our rust even by fire, when even amid scourges we lack not vice."

That is the frightening possibility in verse thirteen. I would have cleansed you, and you were not cleansed. And verse fourteen closes the door. I will not go back; I will not spare; I will not relent.

Then, on what Keil argues was the same day, God turns to Ezekiel's own house.

Verse sixteen. Son of man, behold, I am about to take the delight of your eyes away from you at a stroke; yet you shall not mourn or weep, nor shall your tears run down.

The delight of your eyes. In Hebrew, machmad eneykha, the thing your eyes long for. It is the only window the book gives us into Ezekiel's marriage, and it is enough. D. A. Carson does the arithmetic. If Ezekiel was thirty in chapter one, he is no more than thirty-four or thirty-five now, and his wife was probably no older. And God tells him beforehand, which Carson calls both a blessing and an agony.

Verse seventeen lists what is forbidden, and every item is a mourning custom. Mourners uncovered their heads and threw dust on them; Ezekiel must keep his turban on. Mourners went barefoot; he must wear his shoes. They covered the lower face; he must not. Neighbors brought bread to the house of the dead, the way we bring casseroles; he must not eat it. And Ellicott adds that Ezekiel was a priest, and the law allowed priests to mourn their nearest relatives. He is made the exception.

Then verse eighteen, the verse we opened with. Carson writes: "One can almost feel the massive restraint in the terse words". Ezekiel does not tell us how she died, or even her name. He tells us that he obeyed.

The people notice. Verse nineteen. Will you not tell us what these things mean for us, that you are acting thus? They know he loved her.

And verse twenty-one answers. Behold, I will profane my sanctuary, the pride of your power, the delight of your eyes, and the yearning of your soul. Same phrase. Ezekiel's wife was the delight of his eyes. The temple was the delight of theirs. As long as it stood, the exiles thought, God could never let Jerusalem fall. Now God says, I am taking it, the way I took her.

So here is the hardest question in today's reading. Why would God take a faithful man's wife to make a point? And why forbid him to grieve?

Start with what the text does not say. It never says she died for any sin. This is not punishment. Matthew Poole's comment is that God takes her from Ezekiel, but takes her to himself. She is not lost. She is taken home.

Second, Ezekiel's whole life has been the message. He lay on his side for months in chapter four, shaved his head in chapter five, dug through a wall in chapter twelve. Now his marriage becomes the sign. Henry says the sign cost the prophet very dear. And he draws the argument out of verse twenty-one. If a faithful servant is tried like this, will a city of rebels walk away untouched?

Spurgeon preached on verse nineteen in 1890, and he did not soften it. "It was a terrible lesson, and it was terribly taught." Then he turned it on his own congregation. Many things we do, he said, have some relation to others. Your grief and your obedience are always preaching to somebody.

Third, what does it mean that the people will not mourn either? You shall not mourn or weep, but you shall rot away in your iniquities and groan to one another. Henry hears stunned despair, people murmuring to one another instead of praying. Carson rejects the idea that they will be too callous to care, and he notices something important: "Ezekiel is not silent because of the depth of his loss but because of the command of God." He thinks they are being forbidden to grieve for a judgment so richly deserved. Keil sees grief too deep for tears, only sighing over the sins that caused it, and he says that kind of grief can lead to real repentance.

I think Keil is closest. The phrase, rot away in your iniquities, comes from Leviticus twenty-six, the chapter of covenant curses. And the next verses there say, if they confess their iniquity, then I will remember my covenant. The tearless grief of the exiles is not the end of the story. It is the ground where repentance can grow.

Does this mean grief itself is wrong? No. This was a sign commanded once, to one man, at one terrible moment. Abraham wept for Sarah. David wept for Jonathan. Henry draws the line well: mourning for the dead is a duty, but it must be kept under the government of religion and right reason. Paul says the same in First Thessalonians four. Grieve, but not as others do who have no hope.

And Henry gives a line from verse sixteen to carry with you. "That is least safe which is most dear; we know not how soon the desire of our eyes may be removed from us and may become the sorrow of our hearts".

Here is the application, straight from the parallel between verses sixteen and twenty-one. The people made the temple the delight of their eyes. Not God, but the building where God had once been. The glory had left in chapter eleven, and they never noticed. So ask yourself honestly. What is the delight of your eyes? A person, a church, a career, a plan for your children? Good gifts, all of them. But if God took it, could you say, the next morning I did as I was commanded? Or would you discover that the gift had quietly become your god?

The chapter ends with a promise. On the day the city falls, a fugitive will come with the news, and on that day your mouth will be opened, and you shall be no longer mute. We will see it in chapter thirty-three, almost two years later. Until then, Ellicott notes, Ezekiel says nothing more to Israel. He turns to the nations.

Chapters twenty-five through thirty-two are those oracles, and their placement is deliberate. Carson points out that there are seven nations, as in Amos, and Babylon is not one of them, because Babylon is God's instrument. Placed between the start of the siege and the news of its end, they make you wait with the exiles.

Chapter twenty-five takes four neighbors, each oracle with the same shape. Because you did this, therefore I will do that, and you will know that I am the Lord. Ammon said Aha over the sanctuary, one gloating syllable in Hebrew, he'ach, and clapped and stamped. Moab said, the house of Judah is like all the other nations, a theological sneer: your God was supposed to make you different. Edom took revenge, the old grudge of Esau, and Psalm one hundred thirty-seven remembers Edom shouting, lay it bare, down to its foundations. The Philistines nursed never-ending enmity.

Then chapter twenty-six, and a much bigger target. Tyre.

The date is the eleventh year, the year Jerusalem fell. Tyre was the great Phoenician trading city, built mostly on a rocky island about half a mile offshore, with colonies as far away as Carthage and Spain. And listen to verse two. Aha, the gate of the peoples is broken; it has swung open to me. I shall be replenished, now that she is laid waste.

That is not religious hatred. Henry and Ellicott both note that Tyre was not like Ammon or Edom. Hiram of Tyre had been David's friend. Tyre's interest is commercial. Jerusalem sat on the inland trade routes, and with Jerusalem gone, the business would come to Tyre. A competitor had gone bankrupt.

And God says, Behold, I am against you, O Tyre.

Henry's comment goes to the heart, because respectable people commit this sin. "To be secretly pleased with the death or decay of others, when we are likely to get by it, with their fall when we may thrive upon it, is a sin that does most easily beset us, but is not thought to be such a bad thing, and so provoking to God, as really it is."

The colleague who gets fired, and you get the office. The church down the road that splits, and its families come to yours. Have you felt that small rush, now I shall be replenished? Tyre never lifted a sword against Jerusalem. She just counted the profit. And God took it personally.

Verse three. I will bring up many nations against you, as the sea brings up its waves. Wave after wave against an island rock. Then verse seven names the first wave, Nebuchadnezzar, king of kings. The historian Josephus quotes Tyre's own records, which say Nebuchadnezzar besieged the city for thirteen years in the days of King Ithobal, and that years later Tyre had to send to Babylon for its kings.

But watch the grammar in verse twelve. Up to verse eleven it is he, Nebuchadnezzar. In verse twelve it becomes they. They will cast your stones and timber and soil into the midst of the waters. Ellicott sees the prophet looking past the first wave to those that followed. And one of them did exactly that. In 332 B.C. Alexander the Great built a causeway out to the island, and Matthew Poole notes he built it from the ruins of the old mainland city, its stones, timber, and rubbish. Old Tyre was literally thrown into the sea.

That raises a second hard question. Verse fourteen says, You shall never be rebuilt. But Tyre was rebuilt. John Gill, the Baptist commentator, admits it. The book of Acts tells us that Paul landed at Tyre and found disciples there, and Gill says Jerome wrote that in his own day it was a noble and beautiful city. Gill's answer is that the words must be understood with some restriction, and Ellicott shows where. Verse twenty is aimed at the Tyre that then was, the proud mistress of the sea. That Tyre went down to the pit and never came back. The towns that carried her name afterward were never that power again. Ezekiel will say something surprising about Nebuchadnezzar's siege a few chapters from now, and we will be honest about it when we get there.

The chapter ends with the merchant princes of the coastlands trembling on the ground, lamenting the city renowned. And then the final turn, verse twenty. Tyre goes down to the pit, but I will set beauty in the land of the living. The word is tsevi, the word Ezekiel used in chapter twenty for the land God gave Israel. Tyre thought Jerusalem's ruin was her opportunity. God says, Tyre will be buried, and my land will be made glorious again.

Job chapter nine.

Bildad has finished, and Job answers. Matthew Henry notices something gracious about the way he does it. Bildad opened by insulting him. Job does not return the insult. He begins by agreeing. Verse two. Truly I know that it is so. Henry draws a lesson for every argument you will ever have: we should be ready to own how far we agree with those with whom we dispute.

But then Job asks a question. But how can a man be in the right before God?

That is Eliphaz's question from chapter four, verse seventeen, nearly word for word. Eliphaz asked it to shame Job. Job takes it back and asks it for real. And notice the language. To be in the right is the Hebrew tsadaq, a word from the law court. Verse three uses another. If one wished to contend with him, the word is riv, to bring a lawsuit. Job is picturing a courtroom, with himself on one side and God on the other.

Henry admits that some read verse two as an angry complaint, but he takes it as a pious confession of man's sinfulness. Gregory the Great, in his great commentary on Job, reads it more sharply still. His Latin text said that man put with God is not justified, and he builds on that preposition. "For man being put under God receives righteousness; being put with God he loses it: for everyone that compares himself with the Author of all good things, bereaves himself of the good which he had received."

Under God, you receive righteousness. Alongside God, as his equal in court, you lose even what you had.

In verses five through ten Job sings a hymn to God's power. He removes mountains, commands the sun, made the Bear and Orion and the Pleiades. Henry notices that verse ten repeats Eliphaz's words from chapter five almost exactly. Job is saying, I know your theology. I believe every word of it. That is the problem.

Two lines deserve a closer look. Verse eight. He alone stretched out the heavens and trampled the waves of the sea. In the old world, a man walking on the waves was a picture of the impossible. Only God walks there. And verse eleven. Behold, he passes by me, and I see him not; he moves on, but I do not perceive him. When the disciples saw a figure walking on the sea in the storm, Mark tells us he meant to pass by them. They thought it was a ghost. Job could not see the God who walks on the waves. One night on Galilee, that God came walking toward his friends.

For Job, though, the unseen God is terrifying. He snatches away; who can turn him back? Even the helpers of Rahab, the old image of the chaos monster, bowed beneath him. How then can I answer him?

Then verses fourteen through twenty, the impossible lawsuit. Verse fifteen. Though I am in the right, I cannot answer him; I must appeal for mercy to my accuser. Verse twenty. Though I am in the right, my own mouth would condemn me; though I am blameless, he would prove me perverse.

Spurgeon preached on verse twenty more than once. In 1860 he called it a blow at self-righteousness, and in 1876 he said this: "if there ever was a man in this world who might have been justified before God by his own works, it was Job." If even Job cannot stand on his record, Spurgeon argued, no one can. That is true, and it is a good use of the verse.

But in context Job is saying something a little different. He is not mainly confessing sin. He is saying the contest is impossible. The Pulpit Commentary renders it this way: even if I were perfect, my mouth would be made to look perverse. Job feels that God is so overwhelming that innocence itself would not survive the cross-examination. That is not a confession. It is a cry.

And yet, look again at verse fifteen. I must appeal for mercy. Without quite knowing it, Job has found the only place a man can stand before God. Not on his rights, but on mercy. Gregory saw it centuries ago. Even our righteousness, he says, needs prayer following after it. When you cannot make sense of God, do what Job does. Keep talking to him. And keep appealing for mercy. Next time, in verse thirty-three, Job will cry out for an arbiter, someone to stand between him and God. Hold on to that.

John chapter nine, verses thirteen through twenty-five.

The man born blind can see, and now the trouble starts. They brought to the Pharisees the man who had formerly been blind. Now it was a Sabbath day when Jesus made the mud and opened his eyes.

Why does the Sabbath matter? Because in the rabbis' list of thirty-nine kinds of work forbidden on the Sabbath, recorded in the Mishnah, one of them is kneading. Jesus spat on the ground and kneaded mud. Calvin says Christ chose the Sabbath on purpose. He could easily have waited a day. He did not, because the controversy would make his power shine brighter.

Verse sixteen. This man is not from God, for he does not keep the Sabbath. But others said, How can a man who is a sinner do such signs? And there was a division among them. That word, schisma, keeps appearing in John. In chapter seven the crowd divided over Jesus. In chapter ten it will happen again. Jesus divides people. Nobody stays neutral long.

Now watch the healed man. Each time he is questioned, his answer grows. In verse eleven, Jesus was the man called Jesus. In verse seventeen, when they ask him what he thinks, he says, He is a prophet. By the end of the chapter he will say, if this man were not from God, he could do nothing, and finally, Lord, I believe. His sight is coming in stages too.

And he has every reason to be afraid. This is a beggar facing the most powerful religious body in the city. Calvin sees the hand of God in his nerve. "In this respect the Lord disappoints them; for when a poor man disregards their threatenings, and boldly maintains that Christ is a Prophet, we ought justly to ascribe it to the grace of God; so that this boldness is another miracle."

Two miracles in one chapter. New eyes, and a new spine.

Then the authorities call the parents, hoping to prove there was no miracle. The parents give a careful answer. This is our son. He was born blind. How he sees, we do not know. Ask him; he is of age. And John explains why, in verse twenty-two. They feared the Jews, for the Jews had already agreed that if anyone should confess Jesus to be Christ, he was to be put out of the synagogue.

Some critics say that kind of expulsion belongs to a later generation. Calvin answers that excommunication was an ancient custom long before this, used against those judged to have abandoned the law. Here it is simply turned against the followers of Jesus. And think about what it meant. To be put out of the synagogue could cost you your community, your livelihood, your place at weddings and funerals. The parents had a real fear.

Augustine, preaching on this verse, says one sentence that turns the whole threat upside down. "It was no longer a bad thing to be put out of the synagogue. They cast out, but Christ received."

Then, verse twenty-four, they call the man back a second time. Give glory to God. We know that this man is a sinner. Calvin points out that give glory to God was a solemn formula, the words Joshua used to Achan when he wanted him to confess what he had stolen. They are putting the man under oath and telling him what the true confession must be. Augustine translates their meaning bluntly: deny what you have received.

And the man answers, verse twenty-five. Whether he is a sinner I do not know. One thing I do know, that though I was blind, now I see.

Chrysostom says he was not truly doubting whether Jesus was a sinner. He was refusing to argue on their ground and letting the fact speak. And Chrysostom marvels at the scene. "Do you see the bold-speaking of a beggar towards Scribes and Pharisees? So strong is truth, so weak is falsehood."

Spurgeon called this verse an unanswerable argument. You can debate theories. You cannot argue a man out of his eyes. Personal testimony, he said, drives in the nail.

You may not know how to answer every objection. Neither did he. But you can say what Christ has done.

First John, chapter one, verses five through ten.

Last time, John told us he heard, saw, and touched the Word of life. Now verse five tells us what that Word announced. This is the message we have heard from him and proclaim to you, that God is light, and in him is no darkness at all.

Calvin explains the image in two parts. Light means that there is nothing in God but what is bright and pure. And light exposes. It lets nothing hide. No spots, no fraud, no hypocrisy stays hidden in the light.

The rest of the paragraph is built on a pattern. Three times John writes, if we say. Verse six, if we say we have fellowship with him while we walk in darkness. Verse eight, if we say we have no sin. Verse ten, if we say we have not sinned. These were almost certainly slogans of the false teachers who had left the church. And twice John answers them with a different if. Verse seven, if we walk in the light. Verse nine, if we confess our sins.

Look at verse seven. If we walk in the light, as he is in the light, you would expect John to say, we have fellowship with him. He says, we have fellowship with one another. You cannot claim to be close to God and stay out of the light with his people. And then, the blood of Jesus his Son cleanses us from all sin. The verb is present tense. It keeps cleansing. Calvin says this forgiveness is not given once at the start of the Christian life and then left behind. It is a benefit perpetually residing in the church, offered to believers every day.

Verses eight and ten deal with two different denials. Verse eight, we have no sin, denies the sinful nature. Verse ten, we have not sinned, denies the acts. The Westminster Confession, chapter six, uses these verses to teach that the corruption of nature remains even in those who are born again, and that it is truly sin, though pardoned through Christ. Christians are not people who have stopped sinning. They are people who have stopped lying about it.

Augustine has a warning about the sins we consider small. "But these which we call light, do not make light of. If you make light of them when you weigh them, be afraid when you count them. Many light make one huge sin: many drops fill the river; many grains make the lump."

Then verse nine. If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness. To confess is the Greek homologeo, to say the same thing. Confession is agreeing with God about what you did, calling it by his name for it.

But why does John say just? We would expect merciful. Here the voices differ, and it is worth seeing both. Calvin says God's justice here is his faithfulness to his promise. He could have dealt with us by strict justice, but since he has bound himself by his word, he would not count himself just unless he forgives. Spurgeon preached this verse in 1859 with a sharper edge. Because Christ has paid the debt, he said, justice itself now stands on the forgiven sinner's side. God would be unjust to demand payment twice. I think both are right, and the reason is coming in our next reading. First John two calls Christ the propitiation for our sins. The promise rests on the payment.

And Calvin adds why this matters so much for your peace. "It is of great moment to be fully persuaded, that when we have sinned, there is a reconciliation with God ready and prepared for us: we shall otherwise carry always a hell within us."

R. C. Sproul told a story about this verse. As a young man, burdened with guilt, he went to a minister who had him read verse nine again and again, until he saw that he was trusting his feelings more than God's promise. On verse eight Sproul says, "We all have sin, and therefore we all have contracted guilt. And if we deny that, we are engaged in perhaps the worst kind of deception", which he names as self-deception. Years later a woman told him she had confessed the same sin a hundred times and still felt guilty. Sproul told her to confess one more sin, the sin of arrogance, of refusing to believe God when he says he forgives.

There are two real connections in today's readings.

The first is the question. Job nine, verse two. How can a man be in the right before God? Job asks it in a courtroom where he cannot win. He knows he needs mercy, but he cannot see how a holy God could give it without ceasing to be just. First John one gives the answer Job was groping for. Walk in the light, confess your sins, and the blood of Jesus cleanses you, and the God who is light is faithful and just to forgive. Not because you won the case, but because someone stood in it for you. Next time, Job will cry out for exactly that someone.

The second is smaller. In Job nine, verse eleven, Job says, he passes by me, and I see him not. In John nine, verse one, as he passed by, he saw a man blind from birth. Job could not see the God who passed by him. In John, God passes by in the flesh, and he is the one who sees, and the blind man ends up seeing him.

Here is your question for today. Is there a sin you have been calling by a softer name? Call it what God calls it. Then believe him when he says it is forgiven.
