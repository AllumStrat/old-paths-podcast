Daniel chapter one, verse eight. But Daniel resolved that he would not defile himself with the king's food, or with the wine that he drank.

A teenage captive in the greatest city on earth. A new language, new books, a new name. And the line he draws is at the dinner table. Why there? And what would Babylon have to give you, every day, to make you one of its own?

This is Old Paths. Today is day twenty of the eleventh month in the reading plan, day two hundred seventy of three hundred. The readings are Daniel one and two, Job eighteen, John twelve, verses twenty through thirty-six, and First John five, verses thirteen through twenty-one.

Last time we closed the book of Ezekiel on four words, The Lord Is There. Today a new book opens, and it opens in Babylon. Job asked, where then is my hope, and today his friend Bildad answers for him, badly. In John, the Pharisees grumbled that the world has gone after him, and now the world shows up, Greeks asking to see Jesus. And I promised you the hardest verse in First John. We will get there.

Daniel one and two.

First, where we are. Ezekiel was carried to Babylon in 597 B.C. Daniel went eight years earlier, in 605, probably as a teenager. And Ezekiel knew his name. In Ezekiel fourteen, God names three righteous men, Noah, Daniel, and Job. In Ezekiel twenty-eight, the prophet mocks the king of Tyre, you are indeed wiser than Daniel.

The book has two halves, court stories in chapters one through six and visions in seven through twelve. In chapter two, verse four, the wise men speak to the king in Aramaic, and from that word on the book itself switches into Aramaic, the common tongue of the empire, until the end of chapter seven. Ellicott says God is about to reveal the future of the Gentile world, so he speaks in a Gentile language. The theme of the whole book is in Daniel's prayer in chapter two. He removes kings and sets up kings.

Daniel dates his capture to the third year of Jehoiakim, and we can watch that year happen. The Babylonian Chronicle, a clay tablet, records that Nebuchadnezzar, still crown prince, crushed the Egyptian army at Carchemish. His father died in August. And early in September of 605, it says, he sat on the royal throne in Babylon. Somewhere in that sweep, Daniel was marched east.

Then verse two, the key to the book. The Lord gave Jehoiakim king of Judah into his hand. Nebuchadnezzar thought his god had won. He carried the temple vessels to the land of Shinar, the old name for the plain of Babel, and set them in his god's treasury. Daniel answers him in one verb. The Lord gave.

Now watch the program in verses three through seven. Take the best young men of the royal family. Teach them the literature of the Chaldeans. Feed them from the king's table. And change their names. Daniel means God is my judge. Azariah, whom the Lord helps. Babylon replaced names that carried the God of Israel with names that honored Bel and Nebo. Spurgeon, in a sermon on this chapter published in 1893, put it this way. But, though they might change Daniel's name, they could not change his nature, nor would he give up anything that he believed to be right.

So here is the hard question, and it is a real one. Why the food? Daniel took the Babylonian education. He answered to the Babylonian name. Why is the menu the place he plants his feet?

The commentators split three ways. Keil says the heathen offered part of every meal to their gods, so eating at that table meant sharing in idol worship. A Ligonier devotional rejects that, since the vegetables were offered too, and rejects the food laws, since wine was clean. Its answer is that Daniel wanted to display God's power.

Calvin takes a third road, and I find it the most convincing. He says there was nothing abominable in the food itself. But the luxuries were meant to make Babylon feel like home. So Daniel refused them, Calvin says, desiring by his very food perpetually to recall the remembrance of his country. He wished so to live in Chaldea, as to consider himself an exile and a captive, sprung from the sacred family of Abraham. And John Gill notes that in chapter ten, as an old man in mourning, Daniel gives up meat and wine for three weeks, which tells you that ordinarily he ate them. The issue was whose table, and what it was doing to him. In that world, to eat daily from the king's portion was to be the king's man. And look at how Daniel does it. No speech. He asks, and offers a ten-day test. Jerome says setting a deadline was not rashness but faith.

After three years the king finds them ten times better than all his magicians. And the chapter closes with a sentence covering nearly seventy years. Daniel was there until the first year of King Cyrus. He outlasted the empire that took him.

Chapter two. Nebuchadnezzar cannot sleep, and he demands that his wise men tell him the dream itself, not just its meaning. Anyone can invent a meaning.

The death order reaches Daniel. He asks for time and asks his three friends to pray. That night God reveals the mystery. The Aramaic word is raz, a secret, and it appears eight times in this chapter. Before Daniel goes to the king, he worships. He changes times and seasons; he removes kings and sets up kings. Then before the throne, verse twenty-eight. There is a God in heaven who reveals mysteries. And verse thirty. Not because of any wisdom that I have.

The dream is a giant statue. Head of gold, chest and arms of silver, belly and thighs of bronze, legs of iron, feet of iron mixed with clay. The head is Nebuchadnezzar; Daniel says so. The rest, as Calvin, Jerome, Matthew Henry, and Ligonier all read it, are Medo-Persia, Greece, and Rome.

Then the stone. Cut out by no human hand, it strikes the feet, the whole statue becomes chaff on a summer threshing floor, and the stone becomes a mountain that fills the earth.

What does no human hand mean? Here the fathers and the Reformers part ways. Jerome writes: However, at the final period of all these empires of gold and silver and bronze and iron, a rock, namely, the Lord and Savior, was cut off without hands, that is, without copulation or human seed and by birth from a virgin's womb; and after all the empires had been crushed, He became a great mountain and filled the whole earth.

Calvin knows that reading and calls it unnecessary. For him, no human hand means the kingdom is divinely sent and owes nothing to earthly power. I think Calvin has the text, because verse forty-four explains it. The God of heaven will set up a kingdom that shall never be destroyed. The contrast is with a statue men build. But Jerome is right that Christ is the stone. Jesus takes up this very image. In Luke twenty, after the parable of the tenants, he says that everyone who falls on that stone will be broken to pieces, and when it falls on anyone, it will crush him. That is my one New Testament move today, and Jesus makes it for us.

The chapter ends strangely. The king falls on his face, orders incense offered to Daniel, and confesses, your God is God of gods. And in the next chapter he builds a statue of gold, all of it gold this time. He heard that his kingdom would end, and he built one that would not.

Here is the application. Babylon still runs the same program. Educate you, feed you, rename you. Much of it you can receive, as Daniel did. The danger is the comfort that makes exile feel like home. Spurgeon said Daniel would never have been called a man greatly beloved if he had not been made by grace a youth greatly decided. Decide early where your table is, and decide it quietly.

Job eighteen.

Bildad speaks a second time, and he is angrier. Why are we counted as cattle? Then a jab in verse four. You who tear yourself in your anger, shall the earth be forsaken for you? Must the universe bend so Job can be innocent?

The rest is one long portrait. The light of the wicked is put out. His own feet walk him into a net, and Bildad piles up six words for traps in three verses. The firstborn of death eats his skin. He is torn from his tent and brought to the king of terrors. The Cambridge Bible puts it simply. He spent his life fleeing terrors, and at last he is brought to their king.

Now read it again with Job sitting there. A tent emptied, and Job's children died in a house. Skin eaten by disease, and the Pulpit Commentary thinks Bildad means Job's own. Sulfur on his dwelling, and fire from heaven burned Job's flocks in chapter one. No children left. Every line is aimed. Then the last verse. Such is the place of him who knows not God. Bildad is telling the most God-fearing man on earth that he does not know God.

So is Bildad wrong? Gregory the Great answers carefully. As a description of this life, Bildad is mistaken, because the wicked often prosper and the godly suffer. As a description of where the ungodly end, it is true. And then Gregory adds, Which if it might have been rightly spoken in regard to an ungodly man, ought never to have been delivered against a holy man set fast in the midst of scourges.

That is the wisdom for today. A true sentence becomes a cruel one when you aim it at the wrong man. Before you tell a suffering friend what God does to the wicked, ask whether you are describing the end of the story, or reading his heart off his circumstances.

And notice what Bildad assumes, that death is a king. Matthew Henry says that for the saints, through Christ, the king of terrors becomes a friend and a servant. Job cannot see that yet. But in the very next chapter, from under this heap of insults, he will say, I know that my Redeemer lives.

John twelve, verses twenty through thirty-six.

The Pharisees had just said, the world has gone after him. Now some Greeks at the feast come to Philip. Sir, we wish to see Jesus. Sproul notes that earlier that week Jesus had cleared the Court of the Gentiles, as far into the temple as Greeks could come. And we never learn whether he met them.

Instead he says, The hour has come for the Son of Man to be glorified. All through John the hour has not yet come. Now it has. And what kind of glory? Unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies, it remains alone. Chrysostom says Jesus is explaining why the Gentiles' arrival means his death. Their coming is the harvest, and a harvest needs a buried seed. Sproul, preaching on this passage, says Jesus is telling us, In the death, in the pain, in the suffering is My glory, not that the glory comes through it, but that the glory is there.

Then, Now is my soul troubled. Will he pray, Father, save me from this hour? No. For this purpose I have come. Father, glorify your name. A voice answers from heaven, and some hear only thunder.

Then verse thirty-two. And I, when I am lifted up from the earth, will draw all people to myself. Who is all? Augustine's Latin Bible read all things, and he took it as the whole person, or all who are foreordained. Calvin answers that the Greek manuscripts say all men, and he agrees with Chrysostom that all means the church gathered from Jews and Gentiles alike. With Greeks standing at the edge of the scene, I think that is right.

First John five, verses thirteen through twenty-one.

John tells us why he wrote. I write these things to you who believe in the name of the Son of God, that you may know that you have eternal life. His Gospel was written so that you may believe. This letter, so that believers may know.

From assurance he moves to prayer. If we ask anything according to his will, he hears us. And one use of that confidence is praying for a brother who sins. Then the verse I promised. There is sin that leads to death; I do not say that one should pray for that.

Derek Thomas, answering this on a Ligonier podcast, begins with honesty. Once we believe that all Scripture is inerrant, that doesn't necessarily mean that all Scripture is equally clear. He lays out four views. Warfield thought it meant physical death. Thomas himself holds that it is apostasy, a professing believer falling away in a way that shows he was never truly born of God.

The early church fought over this verse. Tertullian, in his later and harsher years, used it to argue that the church could never restore an adulterer. Thus there is no course left for you, but either to deny that adultery and fornication are mortal sins; or else to confess them irremissible, for which it is not permitted even to make successful intercession.

Calvin will not have it. For him the sin unto death is not a single fall, however grievous, but apostasy, a man wholly turning from God. And he warns us that such cases are rare. We must not rashly conclude that anyone has gone that far. Love, he says, ought to lean toward hope.

I stand with Calvin and Thomas. Look at the next verse. Everyone who has been born of God does not keep on sinning, and the Son of God protects him. John does not want you scanning your brothers for the unforgivable sin. He wants you praying for them, and resting in the One who keeps you. And we know the Son of God has come, so that we may know him who is true.

And the last line of the letter. Little children, keep yourselves from idols. Spurgeon, preaching on it in 1874, noticed that a letter full of love ends with a warning. Love is jealous for the true God.

Here is the connection, and it is in the text. Every reading today asks who the true God is. Nebuchadnezzar confessed, your God is God of gods, then offered incense to a man and built a golden image. Bildad spoke about him who knows not God, and misjudged both God and his friend. And John ends, we know him who is true, keep yourselves from idols. A young man pushing away the king's plate in Babylon was keeping John's last line six hundred years before John wrote it.

So carry this question through the day. What is the king's table for you, the daily portion that is slowly teaching you to feel at home in Babylon?
