As for the word that you have spoken to us in the name of the Lord, we will not listen to you.

Those are the last words anyone speaks to Jeremiah in his book. They do not come from a king this time, or a court official with a penknife. A crowd of ordinary men and women in Egypt, with their baking pans and their incense, stand in the open and say it to the prophet's face.

For forty years people dodged the word of God. They burned it, dropped its messenger into a cistern, called it a lie. Here, at the end, nobody pretends anymore.

God answers with a question. Whose word will stand, mine or theirs?

This is Old Paths. Today is day sixteen of the tenth month in the reading plan. The readings are Jeremiah forty-four through forty-six, the Song of Songs chapter one, John chapter five, verses sixteen through thirty, and First Peter chapter two, verses one through eight.

Last time a remnant of Judah asked Jeremiah for a word from God and swore to obey it, whether good or bad. They waited ten days, heard the answer, and marched to Egypt anyway, dragging the prophet and his secretary Baruch with them. For several episodes I have asked you to carry one Hebrew word, shama, to hear, which is also the word for obey. I promised we would close that thread in chapter forty-four, and we will.

Baruch was blamed in chapter forty-three for turning Jeremiah against the people. Today he gets a chapter of his own, a short one, and it is not what you would expect.

A new book begins today, the Song of Songs, and it will get its introduction when we reach it. In John the argument about the Sabbath turns into an argument about who Jesus is. And Peter tells us what to do with the word that gave us new birth.

So, Jeremiah forty-four. It opens with a list of places. The word came concerning all the Judeans who lived in the land of Egypt, at Migdol, at Tahpanhes, at Memphis, and in the land of Pathros. Migdol was a fortress in the far northeast. Tahpanhes was the frontier town where we left them. Memphis was the old capital. Pathros is Upper Egypt, the long valley running south toward Thebes. A few years ago these people were a frightened column on the road. Now they are spread across the whole country, with houses and local gods.

This is Jeremiah's last recorded sermon. He is old now, in a country he was told never to enter, preaching to people who brought him there against his will.

Here is how today's chapters fit together. Chapter forty-four is that last sermon and the people's answer. Chapter forty-five is a private word to Baruch, dated almost twenty years earlier. Chapter forty-six opens a new section of the book, the oracles against the nations, which runs through chapter fifty-one, and Egypt comes first.

The sermon begins with memory. You have seen all the disaster that I brought upon Jerusalem. Then verse four. Yet I persistently sent to you all my servants the prophets, saying, Oh, do not do this abomination that I hate! But they did not listen or incline their ear. There is our verb, shama. Oh, do not do this. That is a father pleading.

Verse seven asks the question underneath the whole chapter. Why do you commit this great evil against yourselves? Sin is self-harm. And verse ten gives the diagnosis. They have not humbled themselves even to this day.

Then the people answer, from verse fifteen. All the men who knew that their wives had made offerings to other gods, and all the women who stood by, a great assembly. Verses sixteen and seventeen. As for the word that you have spoken to us in the name of the Lord, we will not listen to you. But we will do everything that we have vowed, make offerings to the queen of heaven and pour out drink offerings to her.

The queen of heaven was almost certainly the great sky goddess of the ancient Near East, Ishtar in Mesopotamia, Astarte in Canaan. Verse nineteen mentions cakes bearing her image. Back in chapter seven Jeremiah described the whole family at it. The children gather wood, the fathers kindle fire, and the women knead dough. Household religion, the kind you stop noticing.

Now listen to the verb in verse sixteen. In Hebrew it is a participle, we are not listening to you. It is not a slip. It is a settled position.

This is where the shama thread ends, so look back at how far it has come. The Rechabites obeyed a dead ancestor's voice. Jehoiakim heard the scroll read and cut it up. Zedekiah asked in secret for a word and would not obey the one he got. The remnant swore, we will obey the voice, and then said, you are telling a lie, the Lord did not send you. Even that kept up a pretense.

Matthew Poole, the Puritan commentator, saw what changes here. In chapter forty-three, he says, Johanan only denied that God had spoken. "But now these women and men rise higher, they acknowledge Jeremiah had spoken to them in the name of the Lord, but tell him in plain and direct terms they would not obey it."

Each step took off one layer of disguise, and the last layer is gone. Yet they still think of themselves as worshippers. Verse twenty-six tells you they still swear, as the Lord God lives, while they burn incense to the queen of heaven. Calvin says hypocrites pretend to worship God while they reject the men he sends with his commands. Then he writes, "But God will not and cannot have himself separated from his word." You cannot keep God's name and refuse his voice. In the end you lose the name too.

Now the hardest part of the chapter, and the most modern. Verses seventeen and eighteen are an argument, and it is not a stupid one. For then we had plenty of food, and prospered, and saw no disaster. But since we left off making offerings to the queen of heaven, we have lacked everything and have been consumed by the sword and by famine.

Think about it from where they stood. In 622 B.C. King Josiah tore down the shrines to the host of heaven. Thirteen years later Josiah was dead at Megiddo. Then came Egyptian tribute, Babylon, three deportations, siege, famine, and fire. To an ordinary person in Judah the sequence looked like proof. We stopped serving her, and everything fell apart.

Jeremiah answers in verses twenty-one through twenty-three. The offerings that you offered in the cities of Judah, did not the Lord remember them? It is because you made offerings, and because you sinned against the Lord and did not obey the voice of the Lord, that this disaster has happened to you. In other words, you have the timeline backwards. Second Kings twenty-three says that even after Josiah's reform, the Lord did not turn from his great wrath, because of all the provocations of Manasseh. The judgment was delayed, not cancelled. The years of plenty, Calvin says, were not God's approval. They were his patience, and while God bears with men for a time, he does not stop being their judge.

That argument did not die in Egypt. When Rome crumbled, pagans blamed the Christians. Augustine answers them in the City of God, and he quotes the proverb of his day: "Drought and Christianity go hand in hand." In the same book he admits that as a young man in Carthage he attended the festivals of Caelestis, the heavenly virgin goddess, whom the Baptist commentator John Gill identifies with this very queen of heaven. A thousand years on, the same cult was still teaching people to read their history by their stomachs.

So here is the application, and it comes straight from the text. Do not let your circumstances interpret God's word. Let God's word interpret your circumstances. That cuts both ways. When things go well while you do what God has forbidden, that is not his approval. It is his patience, and patience ends. And maybe you started doing the right thing, ended a relationship, refused a shortcut at work, began to give, and things got harder. That is not proof that obedience failed. The people in Pathros looked at their pantry and decided which god was real. Go back to what God has said.

Then God takes them at their word. Verse twenty-five. Then confirm your vows and perform your vows! The verb is qum, to stand. Make your vows stand. Verse twenty-six. I have sworn by my great name that my name shall no more be invoked by the mouth of any man of Judah in all the land of Egypt. People who would not hear God's name will not be allowed to speak it. Verse twenty-seven. Behold, I am watching over them for disaster and not for good. Watching is shoqed, the word from the almond branch in chapter one, I am watching over my word to perform it. The same wakefulness that guarantees the promise guarantees the threat.

And verse twenty-eight, the line I opened with. They shall know whose word will stand, mine or theirs. Qum again. You made your vows stand. Now we will see whose word stands.

God even names a sign. He will give Pharaoh Hophra into the hand of his enemies. Hophra is the Pharaoh whose army briefly drew Babylon away from Jerusalem in chapter thirty-seven, the protector they trusted. The Greek historian Herodotus says he believed not even a god could remove him from his throne. Within a few years his general Amasis had overthrown him, and the Egyptians strangled him. Matthew Henry notes simply that we hear no more of these Jews in Egypt. The word stood.

Chapter forty-five is a surprise. Five verses, dated to the fourth year of Jehoiakim, 605 B.C., the year Baruch wrote the scroll of chapter thirty-six. Why put it here, nearly twenty years out of order? Henry and Gill say a private word was placed after the public ones, the way Paul's little letter to Philemon follows his letters to churches. But look at the setting. In chapter forty-three the remnant blamed Baruch. Now you see what kind of man he was. Chapter forty-four ends with people who tried to save their lives in Egypt and will lose them. Chapter forty-five ends with a man told not to grasp, who is given his life wherever he goes. And chapter forty-six opens with the same date, the fourth year of Jehoiakim. Baruch's chapter is the hinge between Judah's story and the nations' story.

His complaint is verse three. Woe is me! For the Lord has added sorrow to my pain. I am weary with my groaning, and I find no rest. Baruch was the grandson of a governor of Jerusalem, educated and connected, and he tied himself to the most hated man in the city.

God answers, verse four. Behold, what I have built I am breaking down, and what I have planted I am plucking up, that is, the whole land. Those are the verbs of Jeremiah's call. And notice the pronouns. What I have built. God is tearing down his own house. Then verse five. And do you seek great things for yourself? Seek them not, for behold, I am bringing disaster upon all flesh. But I will give you your life as a prize of war in all places to which you may go.

What was Baruch after? Calvin thinks safety, to be spared while everyone else suffered. Gill guesses a post at court. Jewish tradition says the gift of prophecy: Joshua served Moses, Elisha served Elijah, why not me? Poole admits no one can be sure. But Calvin makes an observation I find very helpful. Baruch did not shrink at the start. He had read the scroll at the risk of his life. He faltered, Calvin says, in the middle of his course. That is when this temptation comes, years into faithful, unseen work, when you notice what your classmates have become.

Matthew Henry, the Presbyterian commentator, gives it a picture. "It is absurd for thee to be now painting thy own cabin. Canst thou expect to be high when all are brought low, to be full when all about thee are empty?" The ship was sinking, and Baruch was redecorating his room.

This verse changed Charles Spurgeon's life. As a young preacher in Cambridge he was meant to meet the head of a Baptist college about training for the ministry. A servant showed him into the wrong room, and the meeting never happened. That afternoon, walking across Midsummer Common to preach in a village, he says, "I seemed very distinctly to hear the words, Seek you great things for yourself? seek them not! This led me to look at my position from another point of view, and to challenge my motives and intentions." He stayed with his little country church. Years later he wrote, "Had it not been for those words, in all probability I had never been where and what I now am."

Baruch's promise is the one Ebed-melech received: your life as a prize of war. No promotion, no prophetic office, just his life, carried out of the burning city like plunder. And the last phrase matters. In all places to which you may go. When they dragged him to Egypt, the promise went too.

Chapter forty-six turns to the nations, and starts with Egypt. Verse two goes back to 605 B.C. and Carchemish on the Euphrates, where the crown prince Nebuchadnezzar crushed Pharaoh Neco's army. The Babylonian Chronicle, a clay tablet in the British Museum, says the Babylonians chased the survivors to Hamath and none of them got home. Jeremiah, verse six. The swift cannot flee away, nor the warrior escape. Neco is the Pharaoh who killed Josiah, which is why verse ten calls it a day of vengeance. Verse eleven mocks the land famous for its doctors. In vain you have used many medicines.

The second poem looks ahead to Nebuchadnezzar invading Egypt itself, and verse seventeen has a joke. Call the name of Pharaoh, king of Egypt, Noisy one who lets the hour go by. The verb sounds like the name Hophra. His new throne name is All Noise, Missed His Chance.

Then comes the end of the chapter, and it is pure grace. But fear not, O Jacob my servant, nor be dismayed, O Israel. I will make a full end of all the nations to which I have driven you, but of you I will not make a full end. I will discipline you in just measure.

These words nearly repeat chapter thirty. Why say them again here? Think about who just walked off the page. The Judeans in Egypt said, we will not listen. If any people deserved a full end, they did. And God still calls Jacob my servant. Calvin explains that God calls him servant not because Jacob obeyed, but because God had chosen him. The promise rests on election, not on the remnant's record. Then Calvin gives a picture. "As when a tree stands, when its root is dead; so also heathen nations, as it were, stand, but in the meantime they are consumed, for God has doomed them to eternal ruin. But consummation is said to be as to God's children, when nothing appears on the surface, but perhaps a dry trunk; yet a living root remains, which will again grow up, and from it branches will arise."

Egypt looks like a tree in full leaf, and its root is dead. Judah looks like a stump in the desert, and its root is alive. If you belong to God, he will discipline you, in just measure, and it may be hard. But he will not make a full end of you.

Now the Song of Songs. We finished Ecclesiastes last time, where the Preacher told us that everything under the sun is a breath. The next book begins with a kiss.

The title is a Hebrew superlative, Song of Songs, built like holy of holies or King of kings. It means the greatest song. Verse one says, which is Solomon's. First Kings four tells us Solomon wrote a thousand and five songs, and the church has traditionally received this one as his, from the tenth century B.C.

No book of the Bible has been read in more ways. Around A.D. 100, when some rabbis questioned whether it belonged in Scripture, Rabbi Akiva answered that all the writings are holy, but the Song of Songs is the holy of holies. Jews still read it at Passover, as the love story of God and Israel. Christians from Origen onward read it as Christ and the church, or Christ and the soul. Bernard of Clairvaux preached eighty-six sermons on it and never got past the start of chapter three. The Puritans Durham and Sibbes loved it. In modern times most interpreters read it simply as love poetry between a man and a woman.

So which is it? Here is the answer I hold, and I think it is the right one. Start with the plain sense. Ligonier, summarizing R. C. Sproul's teaching on the wisdom books, gives his description of what the Song is in its plainest sense: "A spirit-inspired expression of love between a bride and her groom. A love that is not to be ashamed." God made marriage and he made desire, and he put a whole book of love poetry in your Bible.

Then follow the line Scripture itself draws. Paul says that marriage was always a picture, that this mystery is profound, and refers to Christ and the church. So a Christian reads the Song as a love song first, and it is legitimate to hear in it the love between Christ and his people, because God designed marriage to show that. The older Reformed interpreters added a guardrail I would keep: do not press every detail. Not every henna blossom is a doctrine.

Your Bible probably labels the speakers, she, he, and others. The labels are not in the Hebrew, but Hebrew marks gender and number, so you can usually tell who is talking.

She begins with longing. Let him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth! For your love is better than wine. Notice the shift from him to you. She starts by talking about him, and by the second line she is talking to him, as if longing had brought him near. Then verse four. Draw me after you; let us run. She does not say drive me. She says draw me, and then, let us run. Being drawn does not make her passive. It makes her run.

Verses five and six are the heart of the chapter. I am very dark, but lovely, O daughters of Jerusalem, like the tents of Kedar, like the curtains of Solomon. Do not gaze at me because I am dark, because the sun has looked upon me. My mother's sons were angry with me; they made me keeper of the vineyards, but my own vineyard I have not kept!

The plain sense is sunburn. In that world a pale face meant a life of leisure, and a dark one meant outdoor labor. Her brothers put her to work in the fields. Kedar's tents were the black goat-hair tents of desert nomads, and Solomon's curtains were the finest fabric in the palace. She is both, weathered and beautiful at once. This verse is about sun and work, not race, and it has been misused that way.

Then she says, my own vineyard I have not kept. Taking care of everyone else's vineyards, she has neglected her own. Spurgeon preached on that line to Sunday school teachers and church workers, and his warning is the practical point of this chapter. "Feed on the word yourselves, or else your own vineyard will not be kept." You can serve on every committee, teach every class, and lead every Bible study, and starve.

And there is a marriage point here too, plain on the surface of the text. In verse fifteen he says, behold, you are beautiful, my love. In verse sixteen she says, behold, you are beautiful, my beloved. He says it and she says it back. If you are married, when did you last say it out loud?

John chapter five, verses sixteen through thirty. Last time a man who had lain by the pool of Bethesda for thirty-eight years picked up his mat and walked, on the Sabbath, and then told the authorities who had healed him. Verse sixteen. And this was why the Jews were persecuting Jesus, because he was doing these things on the Sabbath.

Jesus answers in one sentence, verse seventeen. My Father is working until now, and I am working.

Genesis two says God rested on the seventh day from all his work. Jewish teachers already knew that could not mean God stopped holding the world together. Philo of Alexandria said God never ceases from making something. Calvin says God finished creating in six days, but his governing of the world goes on, and his rest is not idleness.

So Jesus' defense is that my Father works on the Sabbath, and so do I. John Chrysostom, the great preacher of Antioch and Constantinople, noticed what a dangerous defense that is. If a servant broke the king's law and said, the king breaks it too, that would only make things worse. The defense works only if the one making it stands where the king stands. Chrysostom writes, "He said not, He works, and I minister, but, As He works, so work I; and has declared absolute Equality."

His enemies understood him perfectly. Verse eighteen. This was why the Jews were seeking all the more to kill him, because not only was he breaking the Sabbath, but he was even calling God his own Father, making himself equal with God. John does not tell you they misunderstood. He lets the charge stand, and Jesus spends the rest of the passage explaining it. Augustine has a sharp line here. "Behold, the Jews understand what the Arians do not understand." Arius said the Son was a lesser being. Jesus' enemies heard him better.

But verse nineteen seems to cut the other way. The Son can do nothing of his own accord, but only what he sees the Father doing. Arius loved that verse. Read the rest of the sentence, though. For whatever the Father does, that the Son does likewise. Whatever. A lesser being could not do everything God does. The fathers concluded that there are not two sets of works, the Father's and the Son's. There is one work of God, done by the Father through the Son. The Son can do nothing of his own accord because he is never acting apart from the Father. That is not weakness. It is unity. Calvin adds a twist. He reads these verses of the Son sent in the flesh, the Mediator doing the Father's will on earth, while insisting just as firmly that the Son is fully equal to the Father.

Then Jesus claims two things that belong only to God. He gives life, verse twenty-one: the Son gives life to whom he will. And he judges, verse twenty-two: the Father has given all judgment to the Son, that all may honor the Son, just as they honor the Father.

Verse twenty-four is one of the great assurance verses in the Bible. Truly, truly, I say to you, whoever hears my word and believes him who sent me has eternal life. He does not come into judgment, but has passed from death to life. Notice the tenses. Has eternal life. Has passed. J. C. Ryle, the Anglican bishop of Liverpool, says salvation is present. "It is not a far distant thing which he is to have at last, if he does his duty and is good. It is his own in title the moment he believes."

Then come two resurrections. Verse twenty-five: an hour is coming, and is now here, when the dead will hear the voice of the Son of God, and those who hear will live. That is happening now. Spiritually dead people hear the gospel and come alive. Verse twenty-eight: an hour is coming when all who are in the tombs will hear his voice and come out. The words and is now here are missing this time. That is the last day, and bodies. Augustine put it in a phrase: now they rise in the mind, then in the flesh.

And what about verse twenty-nine, those who have done good to the resurrection of life? Does that contradict verse twenty-four? Calvin answers that Christ is not explaining the cause of salvation here. He is describing the people who are saved, by their fruit. Life comes by hearing and believing. Good works are how you recognize the living.

Think back to the man at the pool. Jesus said, get up, and he got up, a small picture of verse twenty-five.

First Peter, chapter two, verses one through eight. Last time Peter ended with Isaiah forty, the grass withers, but the word of the Lord remains forever, and this word is the good news that was preached to you. Chapter two begins with so. Because you were born by the word, do something with it.

Verse one. So put away all malice and all deceit and hypocrisy and envy and all slander. Every one of those sins poisons a church.

Verse two. Like newborn infants, long for the pure spiritual milk, that by it you may grow up into salvation. There is a wordplay here. In verse one, put away deceit, the Greek word dolos. In verse two, long for milk that is pure, adolos, literally without deceit. Put away the deceit and drink the milk with none in it. And the word translated spiritual is logikos, which can mean rational, or simply of the word. Calvin took it to mean rational. His own nineteenth-century English editor disagreed in a footnote and said it plainly means the milk of the word, which picks up the end of chapter one. John Piper puts the logic simply. "So, you were conceived by the word; you're going to grow by the word."

But is milk not for beginners? Paul and the writer of Hebrews both rebuke Christians who still need milk instead of solid food. Peter is not doing that. He is not comparing levels of teaching. He is talking about appetite. Calvin says that because the infancy of the new life never ends, Peter commends milk as food for the whole of life. Watch a hungry newborn. Nothing else matters.

And verse three tells you where the appetite comes from. If indeed you have tasted that the Lord is good. That is Psalm thirty-four, taste and see. Piper again: "This is what the new birth gives you: a new taste." Clement of Alexandria, around A.D. 200, quotes the verse as, if you have tasted that the Lord is Christ. In Greek the word for good, chrestos, sounds almost exactly like Christos.

Then the picture changes from babies to stones. Verse four. As you come to him, a living stone rejected by men but in the sight of God chosen and precious, you yourselves like living stones are being built up as a spiritual house, to be a holy priesthood, to offer spiritual sacrifices acceptable to God through Jesus Christ.

It is worth remembering who is writing. Jesus gave this man his name, Peter, the rock. And when Peter writes about stones and building, he never puts himself at the foundation. Christ is the living stone, and every believer is a living stone built onto him. And Peter knew what a rock of offense was. He had been one, when he tried to talk Jesus out of the cross.

Every believer is also a priest. No priestly class stands between you and God, and your praise and your life are offerings acceptable through Christ. That does not abolish the ordained ministry. The Larger Catechism still says the word is to be preached by those duly approved and called to that office. A minister is not a priest standing between you and God. He is a servant of the word among a priestly people.

Then come three stones from the Old Testament, in verses six through eight. Isaiah twenty-eight, a cornerstone laid in Zion: whoever believes in him will not be put to shame. Psalm one hundred eighteen: the stone that the builders rejected has become the cornerstone. And Isaiah eight: a stone of stumbling, and a rock of offense. It is the same stone each time. To one person it is a foundation, and another breaks himself against it. Calvin says there is no middle ground: "we must either build on him, or be dashed against him."

Verse eight ends with a hard phrase. They stumble because they disobey the word, as they were destined to do. Reformed interpreters differ on exactly what was destined, the disobedience or the stumbling that follows it. Calvin leans one way and his own editor leans the other. Both agree that God is sovereign even over unbelief, and that the guilt belongs entirely to the one who refuses. The Scottish archbishop Robert Leighton, whose commentary on First Peter has been treasured for three centuries, takes the posture I would recommend. "Here it would be easier to lead you into a deep, than to lead you forth again. I will rather stand on the shore, and silently admire it, than enter into it."

Two connections before we go.

First, Peter's phrase in verse eight: they stumble because they disobey the word. That is a one-line summary of Jeremiah forty-four. The people in Pathros heard the word and said, we will not listen. The word did not move. They broke against it. God asked, whose word will stand? Peter tells us the answer has a name. The stone the builders rejected is still standing.

Second, the shama thread ends today, and it ends in two places. In Egypt, living people say to God's messenger, we will not listen. In Jerusalem, Jesus says the dead will hear the voice of the Son of God, and those who hear will live. The living refused to hear. The dead hear, because his voice makes them hear. That is grace. Whoever hears my word, Jesus says, has passed from death to life.

So here is a question to carry through your day. Where have you been letting your circumstances interpret God's word, instead of letting his word interpret your circumstances?
