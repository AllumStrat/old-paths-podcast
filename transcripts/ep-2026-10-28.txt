Unless.

That is very nearly the last word of the book of Lamentations. Restore us to yourself, O Lord, that we may be restored! Renew our days as of old, unless you have utterly rejected us, and you remain exceedingly angry with us.

And then the book stops. No answer. No closing hymn. Just a prayer hanging in the air.

The rabbis who read this book aloud every summer could not bear it. So in the synagogue, when the reader got to the end, he went back and read verse twenty-one again, so that the last sound in the room would be, Restore us.

I want to ask you whether they were right to do that. And I want you to know, by the end of this episode, what to do with a prayer that ends with a question.

This is Old Paths. Today is day twenty-five of the tenth month in the reading plan, day two hundred fifty of three hundred. The readings are Lamentations chapters four and five, the Song of Songs chapter eight, verses eight through fourteen, John chapter seven, verses twenty-five through thirty-six, and First Peter chapter five, verses eight through fourteen.

And that is three books finishing on the same morning. Lamentations ends today. The Song of Songs ends today. First Peter ends today. Only John keeps walking.

Last time we stood in the very middle of Lamentations, and a broken man in the ruins called something to mind and found that the Lord's mercies are new every morning. I told you then that I have been following two threads through this book. One is a little word, like. Like a widow in chapter one. Like an enemy in chapter two. Like a bear and a lion in chapter three. The other thread is comfort. In chapter one Zion had no one to comfort her. In chapter two the poet asked what he could say to comfort her, and could not answer. In chapter three, memory began to answer.

Today both threads come to their end, and I think you will be surprised by where they land.

And when Peter signs off today, he sends greetings from Babylon, which takes us back to Jeremiah fifty and fifty-one.

Lamentations chapter four.

We are still in the aftermath of 586 B.C. Jerusalem fell in the fifth month, the temple was burned, and the survivors are counting what is left. Chapter four is the fourth poem, and it begins the way chapters one and two began, with the word eikah. How. How the gold has grown dim, how the pure gold is changed!

It is an alphabet poem again, twenty-two stanzas. But chapters one and two had three lines to a stanza. Chapter four has two. And chapter five will have twenty-two lines and no alphabet at all. The poet is running out of breath. The grief that could once be arranged letter by letter is coming apart in his hands.

The first half of chapter four is before and after pictures. Verse two, the precious sons of Zion, worth their weight in fine gold, how they are regarded as earthen pots. Remember Jeremiah smashing the clay flask in chapter nineteen. Gold has become clay, and the clay is broken.

Verse three. Even jackals nurse their young, but the daughter of my people has become cruel, like the ostriches in the wilderness, the bird God describes in Job thirty-nine as leaving its eggs on the ground. Starving mothers turn away from children they cannot feed. Wild dogs do better.

Then verse six, which is startling. The chastisement of the daughter of my people has been greater than the punishment of Sodom, which was overthrown in a moment. How can Jerusalem's punishment be worse than Sodom's? Because Sodom's was quick. Matthew Henry puts it with terrible exactness. "Jerusalem is kept many months upon the rack, in pain and misery, and dies by inches, dies so as to feel herself die." Sodom had fire from heaven for one morning. Jerusalem had a siege that dragged on for well over a year. And Sodom never had the law, the temple, and the prophets. The more light, the heavier the reckoning.

Now the second movement, verses eleven through sixteen, asks why. Verse twelve: The kings of the earth did not believe, nor any of the inhabitants of the world, that foe or enemy could enter the gates of Jerusalem. Sennacherib had surrounded it in Hezekiah's day and gone home in disgrace. The temple of the Lord, the temple of the Lord, the people chanted in Jeremiah seven. The city believed it could not fall.

Then verse thirteen gives the reason. This was for the sins of her prophets and the iniquities of her priests, who shed in the midst of her the blood of the righteous. Think of the prophet Uriah in Jeremiah twenty-six, dragged back from Egypt and killed, and the priests and prophets in that chapter demanding Jeremiah's death. The religious professionals did not just fail to stop the rot. They led it.

And now look at verse fifteen. Away! Unclean! people cried at them. Away! Away! Do not touch! In Leviticus thirteen that was the leper's own cry. Ellicott points out the reversal. Now the people shout it at the priests. The men whose job was to declare what was clean and unclean have become the unclean thing.

And the mouth comes before the eye once more. Verse sixteen, the pe stanza, opens with penei, the face of the Lord, turned away. Verse seventeen, the ayin stanza, has our eyes failing, watching for a nation which could not save. That was Egypt. In Jeremiah thirty-seven Pharaoh's army marched up, the Babylonians pulled back, the city exhaled, and Jeremiah said the Egyptians would go home. They did.

Then verse twenty. The breath of our nostrils, the Lord's anointed, was captured in their pits, of whom we said, Under his shadow we shall live among the nations.

Here is our first hard question. Who is this? Who is the breath of our nostrils?

The Hebrew is ruach appeinu, the breath of our nostrils, the life we breathe. And the Lord's anointed is meshiach Yahweh. Messiah. You can see why the early church could not read this verse without stopping.

There are three answers. The Aramaic Targum said good King Josiah. But John Gill points out that Josiah was slain, not taken, and by the Egyptians, not the Babylonians. The details do not fit.

The second answer is the church fathers'. They read it straight of Christ. Justin Martyr, around the year 150, notices that the human face, with the nose running down from the brow, makes the shape of a cross, and quotes this verse from his Greek Bible: the breath before our face is the Lord Christ. And Irenaeus, a generation later, reads the shadow in the same verse this way. "And by shadow he means His body. For just as a shadow is made by a body, so also Christ's body was made by His Spirit." Under his shadow we shall live. Irenaeus hears the incarnation.

I love the fathers' instinct. But the third answer is right as history, and Calvin, Henry, Gill, and Carson all take it. The anointed one is Zedekiah, who fled by night when the wall was breached and was overtaken in the plains of Jericho. Zedekiah, the most double-minded king of the line, who met Jeremiah in secret and then did nothing.

So why would the poet call him the breath of our nostrils? Calvin's answer is the key to the whole verse. He says the throne of David was a sacred thing whoever sat on it, and then he says this. "But we must observe what we have before said, that these high terms in which the posterity of David were spoken of, properly belong to Christ only; for David was not the life of the people, except as he was the type of Christ, and represented his person."

The words are too big for the man. They said of Zedekiah, under his shadow we shall live, and he could not save himself. That gap between the office and the man is the gap only a greater Son of David could fill.

This is our first trip into the New Testament, and I think it is warranted, because the verse literally says Messiah. Gill notices a painful echo. On the road to Emmaus, two disciples say of Jesus, we had hoped that he was the one to redeem Israel. They too had said, under his shadow we shall live. They too saw their anointed taken. The difference is that Zedekiah stayed in the pit, blind, in Babylon. The Lord's true Anointed came out of his on the third day.

Matthew Henry warns that whatever creature you make the breath of your nostrils, God may justly stop that breath. A career, a party, a person. Under its shadow we shall live. What have you said that about?

Verse twenty-one turns to Edom, you who dwell in the land of Uz. Hold on to that place name. Edom had cheered while Jerusalem burned; Psalm one thirty-seven remembers them shouting, Lay it bare. The cup of wrath Jeremiah carried to the nations in chapter twenty-five will reach Edom too.

And then verse twenty-two, which is the first ray of light in the chapter. The punishment of your iniquity, O daughter of Zion, is accomplished; he will keep you in exile no longer.

Accomplished. Finished. Paid in full. Henry hears Isaiah forty, verse two: her warfare is ended, her iniquity is pardoned. Spurgeon preached on this verse on a Sunday morning in November 1862 and said something about God's character that I think every Christian should memorize. "God is love, but God is also just, as severely just as if He had no love, and yet as intensely loving as if He had no justice."

That is how Spurgeon read Lamentations four, twenty-two. Punishment is not cancelled. It is accomplished. And for the Christian, that is our second and last New Testament move this morning. When Jesus said, It is finished, he used the same idea. The punishment was not waived. It was carried to the end.

Lamentations chapter five.

This one is different. It is still twenty-two lines, the length of the alphabet, but there is no alphabet. Henry notes that some ancient versions titled it, The Prayer of Jeremiah. Everything is now we. And the first word is a prayer. Remember, O Lord, what has befallen us; look, and see our disgrace!

Then a catalogue of life under occupation, and in verse three, this. We have become orphans, fatherless; our mothers are like widows.

There it is. Our little word, for the last time. The book opened in chapter one, verse one, with the city sitting like a widow. It closes with every mother like a widow. Widow, enemy, bear, and lion, and now widow again. The like thread has come full circle. The first widow was a city. Now the widowhood has reached into every house. Verse four, they pay the occupier for water from their own wells and wood from their own trees.

Verse seven is the one that should make you stop. Our fathers sinned, and are no more; and we bear their iniquities. Tomorrow we open Ezekiel, and in chapter eighteen of that book the exiles in Babylon are quoting a proverb. The fathers have eaten sour grapes, and the children's teeth are set on edge. Is this the same complaint? Is the poet saying, we are paying for their sins, it is not fair?

Calvin and Henry both say no, and the proof is nine verses later. Verse sixteen. The crown has fallen from our head; woe to us, for we have sinned! The people are not pleading innocence. Calvin says they are pleading the timing. The generation that provoked God has died. Lord, they are gone. Now look on us.

Verse eighteen. Mount Zion lies desolate; jackals prowl over it. Micah had said Zion would be plowed as a field.

And then, with no warning at all, verse nineteen. But you, O Lord, reign forever; your throne endures to all generations.

This is the hinge of the whole chapter, and I would say of the whole book. The crown has fallen from our head, verse sixteen. But your throne endures, verse nineteen. Zedekiah's throne is gone. The Lord's has not moved.

Calvin was lecturing on this verse near the very end of his course in Geneva, and he said this. "Now the remedy is, to raise up our eyes to God, for however confounded things may be in the world, yet he remains always the same. His truth may indeed be hidden from us, yet it remains in him. In short, were the world to change and perish a hundred times, nothing could ever affect the immutability of God."

A hundred times. Jerusalem's world had perished once, and that was more than they could bear. God was untouched.

Which brings us to the last two verses, and our second hard question.

Verse twenty-one. Restore us to yourself, O Lord, that we may be restored! Renew our days as of old. In Hebrew it has a lovely echo. Hashivenu Yahweh elekha venashuvah. Cause us to turn, O Lord, to you, and we will turn. The same verb, shuv, turn or return, is on both sides. You turn us, and we will be turned.

Now here the voices actually disagree, and it is worth hearing. Calvin insists this is not a prayer for repentance at all. He thinks it means, Lord, turn back to us in favor, and we will be restored. Matthew Henry reads it the other way. He notices they do not pray, turn to us, but turn us to you, and he says that is an admission that the distance was their fault, and that only God's grace can bring them back. John Piper sides with Henry. "There is no hope for God's people unless God causes them to return from their sliding and leaping into sin and unbelief."

I think Henry and Piper are closer to the words, because the prayer says, to yourself. But Calvin is guarding something true. The emphasis is on what God does, not on what we manage. Either way, the turning starts with him.

Then verse twenty-two. Unless.

The Hebrew is two small words, ki im. The old King James took them as but, and made the book end in despair. But thou hast utterly rejected us. The synagogue felt that so heavily that they reread verse twenty-one at the end, and they did the same at the end of Isaiah, Ecclesiastes, and Malachi.

Calvin translated it unless, a little pleased that no one else had seen it. The poet, he says, is reasoning from something impossible. Restore us, unless you have rejected us forever, and that cannot be, because God does not cast off the people he has chosen. Ellicott and the Cambridge Bible agree. Keil and Delitzsch quote a German scholar named Gerlach, who said the hope here only glimmers from far off, like the morning star through clouds. It does not end the night. But it tells you the sun is coming.

So were the rabbis right to reread verse twenty-one? I understand them. But I think the book is better as God gave it. Lamentations ends with an honest prayer that has not been answered yet. And that is the answer to our comfort thread. Chapter one, no comforter. Chapter two, no words. Chapter three, memory. And chapter five does not end with comfort received. It ends with a people still praying. A Ligonier devotional on this chapter makes the point well. If there were no possibility of reconciliation, there would be no point in praying at all.

So here is the application. Some of your prayers do not resolve. You pray for a child, a marriage, a church, and the line ends with unless. Lamentations lets you pray exactly that, without a bow on it. The fact that you are still saying, restore us, is itself the evidence that you have not been rejected. D. A. Carson asks whether our prayers sound anything like this chapter, or whether we have become "content to be rich in information and impoverished in wisdom and godliness." Pray Lamentations five this week. Out loud, with the unless left in.

One last thing. Calvin finished his lectures on Lamentations with this sentence. "As it has so seemed good to the brethren, I will begin tomorrow the explanation of Ezekiel." So will we.

The Song of Songs, chapter eight, verses eight through fourteen. The end of the Song.

It does not end with a wedding march. It ends with a family conversation, a vineyard, and a lover running away over the hills. That is strange, and I think it is on purpose.

Verses eight and nine. We have a little sister. What shall we do for our sister on the day when she is spoken for? These are her brothers, the ones in chapter one, verse six, who were angry with her and sent her to keep the vineyards. Now they plan to guard a younger sister until she is old enough to marry. If she is a wall, firm and chaste, they will crown her with silver. If she is a door, open to anyone, they will board her up with cedar.

Then the bride answers for herself in verse ten. I was a wall, and my breasts were like towers; then I was in his eyes as one who finds peace. I kept myself, she says. And in his eyes I found peace. The Hebrew for peace is shalom, and listen to the names around it. Solomon in Hebrew is Shelomoh. She is called the Shulammite. The Song plays on peace all the way through. The Cambridge Bible notes the siege language. She was a walled city that would not be taken, so the king made peace.

Then verses eleven and twelve. Solomon had a vineyard at Baal-hamon. Ellicott points out that Baal-hamon means lord of a multitude. Solomon is lord of a crowd. He rents his vineyard out, a thousand pieces of silver from each tenant. And the bride says, My vineyard, my very own, is before me; you, O Solomon, may have the thousand.

Remember verse seven, which we read last time. If a man offered for love all the wealth of his house, he would be utterly despised. Here it is in a picture. Solomon has a multitude and can buy anything. She has one vineyard, her own self, and it is not for sale. In chapter one she said, my own vineyard I have not kept. Now she has kept it, and she gives it freely to the one she loves.

Verse thirteen. He speaks. O you who dwell in the gardens, with companions listening for your voice; let me hear it.

Spurgeon preached on this verse in April 1883 and called it the bridegroom's parting word. The lovers are about to part for a while, and the last thing he asks for is to hear her voice. Spurgeon applied it to Christ and his people, and then he turned it on himself. "O believer, will you let the Lord Jesus as it were with tears in His eyes, say to you, 'You talk to everybody but to Me. You lay yourself out to please everybody but Me. You are a charming companion to everybody but to Me'?" And Spurgeon confessed that the text went through him like a dagger, because he had spoken all day to others and had scarcely a word for Christ.

That is the practical word for today. If you are married, it is first a word about your marriage. Is your wife the one person who gets the leftovers of your conversation? And it is a word about prayer. You may talk about the Lord all day. When did you last talk to him?

Then verse fourteen, her last word. Make haste, my beloved, and be like a gazelle or a young stag on the mountains of spices.

The verb is barach, which normally means flee. The Cambridge Bible says plainly it should be flee. It repeats her words from chapter two, verse seventeen. It is a lover's way of saying, go, run, and come back to me quickly. The Song ends in longing, not in arrival.

Gill noticed that the church's last word in the Song sounds like the church's last word in the whole Bible. At the end of Revelation the bride says, Come, Lord Jesus. The Song closes the way the Christian life is lived. Loved, sealed, sure, and still saying, make haste.

John chapter seven, verses twenty-five through thirty-six.

Jesus is in the temple at the Feast of Booths, teaching openly. Last time, the crowd from out of town said, you have a demon, who is trying to kill you? Now verse twenty-five, some of the people of Jerusalem speak up. They are locals. They know exactly who is trying to kill him. Is not this the man whom they seek to kill? And here he is, speaking openly, and they say nothing to him! Can it be that the authorities really know that this is the Christ?

Then they talk themselves out of it. Verse twenty-seven. But we know where this man comes from, and when the Christ appears, no one will know where he comes from.

Where did that idea come from? In Justin Martyr's Dialogue with Trypho, a Jewish teacher says the Christ, if born, is unknown and powerless until Elijah comes to anoint him. A hidden Messiah, appearing out of nowhere. And yet the same people knew Micah five, that he would come from Bethlehem. Verse forty-two of this very chapter says so.

J. C. Ryle has a line about that contradiction that I think about often. "Men's memories are often sadly dependent on their wills." They forgot Bethlehem when it was inconvenient to remember it. We all do that. The verse you do not want to obey is the verse you cannot quite recall.

Verse twenty-eight. Jesus cries out, You know me, and you know where I come from. But I have not come of my own accord. He who sent me is true, and him you do not know. It is irony. Yes, you know my hometown. You know Mary and Joseph. You do not know where I really come from, because you do not know the one who sent me. Chrysostom says that it is possible to know and yet not know.

Verse thirty. So they were seeking to arrest him, but no one laid a hand on him, because his hour had not yet come.

What is his hour? Is Jesus on some cosmic timetable he cannot change? Augustine heard people in his own day talking about fate, and he answered sharply. "The Lord was not born under fate. This is not to be believed concerning you, much less concerning Him by whom you were made." His hour is his will, Augustine says, the hour in which he would consent to be put to death. And Calvin draws the comfort for us. The time of every man's death has been fixed by God, which means a believer is safe until God calls him home. Ryle says the servants of Christ are immortal until their work is done.

Then the chief priests send officers to arrest him, and Jesus says, I will be with you a little longer, and then I am going to him who sent me. You will seek me and you will not find me. Where I am you cannot come.

Augustine notices the tense. Not where I will be, but where I am.

And the crowd's guess in verse thirty-five is wonderful. Does he intend to go to the Dispersion among the Greeks and teach the Greeks? It is a sneer. Is this rabbi going to go teach pagans? Augustine says they prophesied without knowing it. And Ellicott points out that the Greek word for Dispersion, diaspora, appears in only two other places in the New Testament. One of them is the first verse of the letter we are finishing this morning.

First Peter chapter five, verses eight through fourteen.

Peter has just said, cast all your anxieties on him, because he cares for you. And then, without a breath, verse eight. Be sober-minded; be watchful.

Watchful is gregoreo, stay awake. Think who is writing. In Gethsemane Jesus said to Peter, Simon, are you asleep? Could you not watch one hour? The man who fell asleep that night is now telling the churches to stay awake.

Your adversary the devil prowls around like a roaring lion, seeking someone to devour. Adversary is antidikos, the opponent in a lawsuit. Devil is diabolos, the slanderer. He accuses you to God and slanders God to you. And Ellicott notes the verb for prowling around recalls the book of Job, where Satan comes from going to and fro on the earth. Devour is katapiein, to gulp down, to swallow whole.

Why the lion right after anxiety? Spurgeon saw it. "He hopes to devour you by care. Cast all your care upon God, for if you are worried you cannot be sober or watchful. Satan rides on the back of carnal care and so obtains entrance into the soul." An anxious man is a distracted man, and a distracted man is easy prey.

Verse nine. Resist him, firm in your faith. Robert Leighton, the Scottish archbishop, has two lines on this verse I love. "A man cannot fight upon a quagmire." You need ground under your feet, and faith stands on God's promises. And then, "Faith sets the stronger Lion of the tribe of Judah, against this roaring lion of the bottomless pit; that delivering Lion, against this devouring lion." Last time in Lamentations three the man said God was a lion to him. Here there are two lions, and you are not left with the wrong one.

Knowing, Peter adds, that the same kinds of suffering are being experienced by your brotherhood throughout the world. Calvin says Satan tries us most dangerously when he makes us feel alone. You are not the only one.

Then verse ten. After you have suffered a little while, the God of all grace, who has called you to his eternal glory in Christ, will himself restore, confirm, strengthen, and establish you.

Four verbs, and the first two are personal for Peter. Restore is katartizo. Vincent's Word Studies notes it is the word for mending nets, the very thing James and John were doing in their boat when Jesus called them. And confirm is sterizo, strengthen. On the night before the cross, Jesus told Peter that Satan had demanded to sift him like wheat, and then said, but I have prayed for you, and when you have turned again, strengthen your brothers. Sterizo. The same word. The man the lion nearly swallowed is now doing exactly what Jesus told him to do.

Verse twelve. Silvanus, who is Silas from the book of Acts, carried or helped write the letter. And Peter sums it up. This is the true grace of God. Stand firm in it.

Then verse thirteen. She who is at Babylon, who is likewise chosen, sends you greetings, and so does Mark, my son. She is the church.

So which Babylon? Eusebius, the church historian, tells us that Papias and Clement of Alexandria said Peter wrote from Rome and called the city Babylon by a figure. Calvin disagreed, and said Peter was probably in the real Babylon on the Euphrates, where many Jews still lived. I think Eusebius has the better of it. Mark was in Rome, and Revelation uses Babylon for Rome. And remember Jeremiah fifty and fifty-one. Babylon is the city of exile, the city God judges, the city his people are told to seek the welfare of and then come out of. Peter is telling Christians in Turkey that the church in the capital of the world is living in exile too.

Verse fourteen. Greet one another with the kiss of love. Peace to all of you who are in Christ. That is how Peter ends. Peace.

Two connections, and both are in the text.

First, the dispersion. In Lamentations four, verse sixteen, the Lord himself has scattered them. That is the diaspora beginning, God's people sent out among the nations in judgment. In John seven, the crowd sneers, will he go to the dispersion and teach the Greeks? And Peter writes his whole letter to the elect exiles of the dispersion, and signs it from Babylon. The scattering that began in Lamentations as a curse became, in Christ, the road the gospel ran on. God turned the exile into a mission.

Second, the endings. Lamentations ends, restore us, unless. The Song ends, make haste, my beloved. First Peter ends, after a little while, he will restore you. All three end looking forward. The words differ in Hebrew and Greek, but the hope is the same. Restore us, prayed the poet in the ruins. He will himself restore you, says the apostle from Babylon.

Notice too where all three are said. Lamentations is prayed in the ruins. The bride speaks from the gardens, with the beloved gone over the mountains. Peter writes from Babylon. None of them is home yet. Neither are you. That is not a reason to stop praying. It is the reason to keep going.

So here is your question to carry through the day. What prayer have you stopped praying because it ended in unless?

Ezekiel one through three, the prophet among the exiles by the river Chebar seeing the glory of God, and Job chapter one, a man who lived in the land of Uz, where we just met Edom this morning, and a Satan who goes to and fro on the earth, like Peter's prowling lion. We also read John seven, verses thirty-seven through forty-four, rivers of living water, and Second Peter one, verses one through eleven.
