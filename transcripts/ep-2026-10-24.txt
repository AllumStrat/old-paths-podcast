On the ninth day of the month of Av, in synagogues all over the world, the lights are turned down low. People take off their leather shoes. They sit on the floor or on low stools, the way mourners sit. And someone begins to chant, slowly, in a tune used for nothing else all year. One Hebrew word. Eikah.

How.

It is not really a question. It is the sound a man makes when he walks into the ruin of his own house.

That is the first word of the book you read this morning. For two and a half thousand years, God's people have opened their mouths on the worst day of their year with it.

This is Old Paths. Today is day twenty-two of the tenth month in the reading plan, day two hundred forty-seven of three hundred. The readings are Lamentations chapter one, the Song of Songs chapter six, John chapter six, verses sixty through seventy-one, and First Peter chapter four, verses one through eleven.

Last time we closed the book of Jeremiah. Fifty-two chapters ended with a captive king at Babylon's table, eating his daily portion, a small, deliberate hope. And I left you with Zechariah's promise that the fast of the fifth month would one day become a feast. I also said the road there runs through Lamentations first. Today we start down that road.

Here is a detail I love. Adam Clarke owned an old handwritten English Bible from the days of Wycliffe, and in it there is no break between the books. Right after the last verse of Jeremiah fifty-two, the scribe writes that Jeremiah the prophet sat weeping, and then, in red letters, Here beginneth the Lamentation of Jeremye. Aleph. How sitteth aloon the city. The fire of chapter fifty-two is still smoking when the poem begins. Read it that way. Same city, the morning after.

Lamentations. Start with the shape, because the shape is part of the message. The book is five poems. Four of them are acrostics. Chapters one, two, and four have twenty-two stanzas, one for each letter of the Hebrew alphabet, aleph to taw. Chapter three triples it, three verses for every letter, sixty-six in all. And chapter five has twenty-two lines but drops the alphabet completely, as if the poet could no longer hold it together.

Why write grief in alphabetical order? Partly for memory. These poems were meant to be learned by heart. But an acrostic also says, We are going to grieve this from A to Z, nothing left out. And it puts a border around the grief. Twenty-two stanzas, and then it stops. The sorrow is total, but it is not endless. It has a shape. Grief that has a shape can be prayed.

Much of it is in the qinah rhythm, a long line and a shorter one, so the verse limps like a man behind a coffin.

Who wrote it? The book does not say. The ancient tradition, going back to the preface in the Greek Old Testament, says Jeremiah, sitting and weeping after the captivity, and our confessional tradition has generally held that. It sounds like him. In chapter nine of his prophecy he wished his eyes were a fountain of tears. In verse sixteen today, my eyes flow with tears. Matthew Henry says that here the prophet has simply become the poet.

And it was written after the fact. Some rabbis, and Jerome among the fathers, thought these were the laments Jeremiah wrote for good King Josiah, mentioned in Second Chronicles thirty-five. John Calvin will have none of it. Jerome was badly mistaken, he says. Josiah was buried with his fathers. This book is about a city burned and a people led away. That means the summer of 586 B.C., the fifth month, the month of Av, when Nebuzaradan burned the house of the Lord.

You can almost touch it. In 2019 archaeologists on Mount Zion, in what was the southwestern quarter of the old city, found a layer of ash with Babylonian-style arrowheads mixed into it. Broken lamps and pottery. And a single earring, a little gold bell with a cluster of silver grapes beneath it. As the excavator, Shimon Gibson, pointed out, people do not throw gold jewelry and arrowheads out with the kitchen trash. Someone wore that earring the week the city fell.

Eusebius tells us Origen wrote volumes on this book at Alexandria, but they are almost all lost, so today our guides are mostly Reformers and Puritans.

Now the poem itself. It falls into two halves. In verses one through eleven a narrator, the prophet, looks at Jerusalem and describes her. In verses twelve through twenty-two, Jerusalem herself speaks. She is pictured as a woman, and the first thing she says when she takes over the poem is, Is it nothing to you?

Verse one. How lonely sits the city that was full of people! How like a widow has she become, she who was great among the nations! She who was a princess among the provinces has become a slave.

Three reversals. Full, then alone. Great, then a widow. A princess, then a slave. God had said of Zion, This is my resting place forever. So Calvin writes, "For this change, as I have said, was as though the sun had fallen from heaven; for the sun has no firmer standing in heaven than Jerusalem had on earth, since its preservation was connected with the eternal truth of God."

Cities fall all the time. This one carried God's promise. If Jerusalem can fall, what does that mean about God?

Notice the little word like. Like a widow. John Gill passes on a comment from Rashi, the great medieval Jewish commentator. "It is not said a widow simply, but as a widow, because her husband would return again; and therefore only during this state of captivity she was like one." Even in the first verse of the darkest book, the grammar leaves a door open. Not a widow. Like a widow. Her Husband is not dead.

Verse two. She weeps bitterly in the night, with tears on her cheeks; among all her lovers she has none to comfort her.

Here is the key word of the chapter. Menachem, comforter, from nacham, to comfort. Verse two, she has none to comfort her. Verse nine, she has no comforter. Verse sixteen, a comforter is far from me. Verse seventeen, there is none to comfort her. Verse twenty-one, there is no one to comfort me. Five times. It is the refrain of the poem, tolling like a bell.

Who are the lovers who did not come? Egypt, and the neighbors Judah flirted with instead of trusting the Lord. In Jeremiah thirty God said, All your lovers have forgotten you. Now they have. Matthew Henry has a line on verse two that I have not been able to shake. "The staff that breaks under us may do us as great a mischief as the staff that beats us." Babylon beat Judah. Egypt broke under her. Either one wounds.

Verse three, Judah dwells among the nations, but finds no resting place. Moses warned in Deuteronomy twenty-eight that a covenant-breaking Israel would find among the nations no resting place for the sole of her foot. Verse five, her foes have become the head. Deuteronomy twenty-eight again, where obedience makes you the head and disobedience the tail. The curses of Moses are being read out over the ruins. And verse four, the roads to Zion mourn, for none come to the festival. Even the roads are grieving.

Then verse seven. Jerusalem remembers in the days of her affliction and wandering all the precious things that were hers from days of old. She remembers now. Calvin's point is sharp. When she had all those precious things, she never thought about them. Prosperity had made her drunk, he says, and the fall woke her up. Ingratitude, he says, is an abyss that swallows every blessing God pours into it. Do not wait for the loss to learn what the gift was.

Verse eight names the cause. Jerusalem sinned grievously, literally, sinned a sin. Verse five already said the Lord afflicted her for the multitude of her transgressions. Babylon was the instrument. The Lord acted, and sin was the cause.

Verse ten is the worst moment in the first half. The enemy has stretched out his hands over all her precious things; for she has seen the nations enter her sanctuary, those whom you forbade to enter your congregation. That is Deuteronomy twenty-three. No Ammonite or Moabite may enter the assembly of the Lord. And Ezekiel twenty-five says the Ammonites cried, Aha, over the sanctuary when it was profaned. The nations barred from the assembly watched strangers walk into the holy place. Calvin says that hurt the faithful more than losing their property, because the worship of God itself was trampled.

Verse twelve. Is it nothing to you, all you who pass by? Look and see if there is any sorrow like my sorrow, which was brought upon me, which the Lord inflicted on the day of his fierce anger.

And here is our hard question. Is this verse about Christ?

You have probably heard it that way, in Handel's Messiah and in countless Good Friday sermons. Matthew Henry, in his short commentary, says far more can be learned here from the sufferings of Christ than from Jerusalem's. Spurgeon preached at least two sermons on this verse, both about the cross.

But in its own setting the speaker is Jerusalem, a guilty city suffering for her own transgressions. The New Testament never quotes this verse, and Calvin, going word by word, never applies it to Christ.

So who is right? Spurgeon himself gives the most honest answer, and it surprised me. In his 1881 sermon he says this has been a favorite text in every age of the church, "not as directly meaning what we shall say upon it, but as suggesting an adaptation, and asking to be used in our case as Jeremiah used it in his own."

An adaptation. That is exactly right. Lamentations one, verse twelve, is not a prophecy of the cross. It is Zion's cry. But it gives us words. And there is a real line from Zion's sorrow to Christ's. Zion bore the curse of the covenant for her own sins. On the cross that curse fell on the one man who had none of his own. Jerusalem's yoke was woven from her own sins. His was woven from ours. So you may borrow the words, if you know you are borrowing them.

But do not skip what the verse means first. Suffering under God's hand is heavy, and it is a sin to walk past it unmoved. Matthew Henry adds a note of realism. We are all tempted, he says, to think no sorrow is like ours. Then he writes this. "If our troubles were to be thrown into a common stock with those of others, and then an equal dividend made, share and share alike, rather than stand to that we should each of us say, Pray, give me my own again."

Give me my own again. You would take your own burden back rather than swap it.

Verses thirteen through fifteen pile up images of God's direct action. Fire into the bones. A net for the feet. The Lord has trodden as in a winepress the virgin daughter of Judah. Judah is not the vine now. She is the grapes. And verse fourteen, my transgressions were bound into a yoke. You remember Jeremiah walking the streets in chapter twenty-seven with a wooden yoke on his neck, and Hananiah breaking it, and God answering, You have broken wooden bars, but you have made in their place bars of iron. Now Zion admits what the yoke was made of.

Verse seventeen is the one place in the second half where the narrator breaks back in. Zion stretches out her hands, but there is none to comfort her. Keil and Delitzsch suggest it is as if her voice were choked with tears, so the prophet speaks for her while she catches her breath.

And then she speaks again, and verse eighteen is the turning point of the whole chapter. The Lord is in the right, for I have rebelled against his word.

The Lord is in the right. Tsaddiq hu Yahweh. Righteous is he, the Lord. In the middle of the ash and the arrowheads, Zion says, He is righteous. I rebelled.

Calvin sets this beside Virgil's lament for Troy, where the mourners blame cruel Jupiter for ruining a nation that did not deserve it. Both laments weep, he says. The difference is faith. "The faithful do not harden themselves in their mourning, but reflect on themselves and examine their own life, and of their own accord prostrate themselves before God, and willingly submit to the sentence of condemnation, and confess that God is just."

And that answers our question from verse one. If Jerusalem can fall, does that mean God failed? No. He told them, in Deuteronomy and through Jeremiah for forty years. The city fell because his word does not fail. His threats are as true as his promises, which is exactly why the promises can still be trusted.

But notice also what verse eighteen does not do. It does not stop crying. The very next words are, But hear, all you peoples, and see my suffering. Confession does not turn off the tears. Some Christians think that if you deserved it, you are not allowed to hurt. Others think that if you hurt, you must not have deserved it. Lamentations says both at once. The Ligonier study notes put it simply. "Lamentations shows us that there can be a godly grief over the fate of a people even when that fate is deserved."

The chapter ends with a prayer that God would deal with Zion's gloating enemies as he has dealt with her. We met a prayer like that in Jeremiah fifty-one, so I will be brief. Calvin's rule is that you may pray this way only against God's enemies, and never to plead your own private grudge. And notice that she asks God to deal with them as he dealt with her, because of all her transgressions. She does not ask him to be harder on them than on herself.

So the chapter ends with no comforter. The poem does not resolve it, and Scripture lets that stand. But the Bible does answer it. More than a century before these days, God had already given Isaiah words that sound as if they were written to answer this poem. Isaiah forty. Comfort, comfort my people, says your God. Speak tenderly to Jerusalem. Isaiah forty-nine, Zion said, The Lord has forsaken me, and God answers, I will not forget you. And Isaiah fifty-one, the most direct answer of all. I, I am he who comforts you.

The poem said there is no comforter. God says, I am the comforter.

And one more New Testament move, because Jesus made it. Blessed are those who mourn, for they shall be comforted. He did not say blessed are those who never have to mourn. He said comfort comes to mourners. Lamentations is the school where God's people learned to mourn rightly.

So here is the application, straight from verse eighteen. When your life falls apart, and some of it is your own fault, you do not have to choose between honesty about the pain and honesty about the sin. Sit in the ruins and say both. This is unbearable, and the Lord is in the right. That is not despair. It is faith, and faith that talks like that is still talking to God, which means it is still hoping.

Calvin has a beautiful summary of the whole book. When the church was in total disorder and reduced almost to nothing, he says, Jeremiah still built some sort of building out of the ruins. That is what this book does. It builds something out of rubble. Twenty-two stanzas at a time.

The Song of Songs, chapter six. Last time the bride let her husband knock and wait, and by the time she opened, he was gone. She searched, the watchmen wounded her, and she praised him to the daughters of Jerusalem until they wanted to find him too.

So chapter six opens with their question. Where has your beloved gone, that we may seek him with you?

And her answer is lovely, because she knew all along. Verse two. My beloved has gone down to his garden, to the beds of spices. Remember, in this book the garden is the bride herself. Chapter four called her a garden locked. Chapter five said he came to his garden. So the man she was searching the streets for was never really lost. He went where he always goes. To her.

Then verse three. I am my beloved's and my beloved is mine.

You have heard that line before, in chapter two, but turned the other way. Back then she said, My beloved is mine, and I am his. Mine first. Now, after the failure and the searching, it is his first. I am his. Hudson Taylor, the missionary to China, saw the change and put it this way. "Then her first thought of Christ was of her claim upon Him: His claim upon her was secondary. Now she thinks first of His claim; and only afterwards mentions her own." And in chapter seven it goes one step further. I am my beloved's, and his desire is for me. She does not even mention her claim.

That is maturing love. Early on you think about what you get. Later, about whom you belong to.

Then from verse four he speaks, and this is the surprise of the chapter. After she failed him at the door, you might expect a lecture. Instead he praises her again. You are beautiful as Tirzah, my love, lovely as Jerusalem. Tirzah was the royal city of the north in the days of the divided kingdom, and its name means pleasing. Jerusalem was the capital of the south. He compares her to two capitals.

Then, awesome as an army with banners. The word is nidgalot, bannered. In chapter five she called him distinguished among ten thousand, and that word was dagul, bannered. She raised his flag. Now he raises hers.

And verses five through seven repeat, almost word for word, what he said in chapter four. The hair like goats on Gilead, the teeth like washed ewes, the cheeks like pomegranates. Why repeat it? Matthew Henry says it was "to show that he had still the same esteem for her since her unkindness to him." He takes nothing back. Richard Sibbes, in his sermons on this book, says, "As she was large in his commendations, so he is large in hers, and more large. He will have the last word."

When your wife has had her worst week, do you hold back praise until she earns it back? He praises right after the failure.

Verses eight and nine are bold coming from Solomon's household. There are sixty queens and eighty concubines, and virgins without number. My dove, my perfect one, is the only one. You know from First Kings eleven what Solomon's harem became. And yet in the middle of that world this song says the many are nothing beside the one. The Song praises one man and one woman, God's pattern from Genesis two.

Verse ten. Who is this who looks down like the dawn, beautiful as the moon, bright as the sun, awesome as an army with banners? Dawn, moon, sun, the light grows as you go.

Verse twelve, the Cambridge Bible says, is probably the most difficult verse in the whole book, and I will not pretend to settle it. Verse thirteen calls her the Shulammite, which is either a woman from the town of Shunem or the feminine form of Solomon's own name. Solomon and Shulammite, he and she.

And yes, the church has always heard more here. Ephesians five calls marriage a mystery that points to Christ and the church. So hear it lightly. When the bride fails, he does not withdraw his praise. And she has learned to say, I am his, before she says, he is mine.

John six, verses sixty through seventy-one. We are still in the synagogue at Capernaum, and John shows you what the bread of life sermon did to its hearers.

Verse sixty. When many of his disciples heard it, they said, This is a hard saying; who can listen to it? Notice who is speaking. Until now the grumblers were the Jews in the crowd. Now it is his disciples.

And the word hard, skleros, does not mean hard to understand. It means harsh, offensive, the word for the hard master in the parable of the talents. They understood him well enough. They did not like what they understood.

Jesus answers with three things. First, verse sixty-two, a sentence he leaves hanging. Then what if you were to see the Son of Man ascending to where he was before? Augustine read it as a kindness. When they see him go up to heaven whole, they will know he never meant butcher's meat. Others read it the other way. If you stumble at the bread, how will you stand the glory?

Second, verse sixty-three. It is the Spirit who gives life; the flesh is no help at all. The words that I have spoken to you are spirit and life. After all that talk of eating his flesh, that sounds like a contradiction. Chrysostom said Jesus meant a fleshly way of hearing. Augustine said flesh alone, without the Spirit, profits nothing, and Calvin follows Augustine. And the second half of the verse tells you how to eat this bread. Through his words, received by the Spirit.

Third, verses sixty-four and sixty-five. But there are some of you who do not believe. And then, This is why I told you that no one can come to me unless it is granted him by the Father. Last time we heard verse forty-four, unless the Father draws him. Jesus repeats it to the people about to leave. They do not believe, and that is their fault. No one comes unless it is given, and that is grace. He does not apologize for either.

Verse sixty-six, one of the saddest sentences in the Gospels. After this many of his disciples turned back and no longer walked with him.

The chapter began with five thousand men wanting to make him king. It ends with twelve. Augustine, preaching on this verse, spoke straight to discouraged pastors. "Behold; it happened to the Lord: He spoke, and lost many; He remained with few. But yet He was not troubled, because He knew from the beginning who they were that believed and that believed not."

Bishop Ryle gives the other half. When someone sees professing Christians fall away and decides the whole thing is fake, Ryle says, "He forgets that there will always be counterfeit coin where there is true money." Nobody counterfeits worthless money.

Verse sixty-seven. So Jesus said to the twelve, Do you want to go away as well? The Greek expects the answer no. It is almost tender. You are not leaving too, are you? Chrysostom noticed that Jesus did not say, Go, which would have pushed them away. He asked.

Peter answers for them all. Lord, to whom shall we go? You have the words of eternal life, and we have believed, and have come to know, that you are the Holy One of God.

Peter does not claim he understood the hard saying. He says there is nowhere else to go. And he picks up Jesus' own word from verse sixty-three. The words are spirit and life. You have the words of eternal life.

The Holy One of God is the reading of the oldest manuscripts. The King James has Christ, the Son of the living God, which probably came in from Matthew sixteen. In the Gospels the only others who call Jesus the Holy One of God are demons, and it echoes Isaiah's title for God, the Holy One of Israel. When you hit a hard saying of Jesus, that is your answer too. Not, I understand it all. Rather, Where else would I go?

Then the chapter ends in shadow. Did I not choose you, the twelve? And yet one of you is a devil. Calvin points out that the choosing here is to the office of apostle, not to eternal life. Judas was chosen, called, and close enough to hear every word, and it was not enough. Peter's name and Judas's name stand three verses apart. One says, To whom shall we go? The other was already going.

First Peter chapter four, verses one through eleven. After the spirits in prison and Noah's ark, Peter picks up the thread from chapter three, verse eighteen. Christ suffered once for sins.

Verse one. Since therefore Christ suffered in the flesh, arm yourselves with the same way of thinking, for whoever has suffered in the flesh has ceased from sin.

Arm yourselves is a soldier's word, hoplizo. The weapon is a settled conviction. Christ suffered in the flesh, and so you are finished with sin.

That last clause does not mean that Christians never sin. Back in chapter two Peter told them to put away malice and deceit, so he knows they do. It means what Paul means in Romans six. You died with Christ. Sin is no longer your master. Spurgeon preached on these verses and put it in one picture. "It once put its foot upon his neck, but now he puts his foot upon its neck." The fight goes on, but the throne has changed hands.

Verse three lists the life they left, the guild banquets and temple feasts of a Roman city, where religion, wine, and sex went together. Verse four is true to life. They are surprised when you do not join them in the same flood of debauchery, and they malign you. Surprised is xenizo, they find it strange, foreign. Peter has already called them sojourners and exiles. Of course they look foreign. They are.

Verse six. For this is why the gospel was preached even to those who are dead. This is not a sermon in the underworld. The most natural reading, held by Robert Leighton, Albert Barnes, and most of our tradition, is that these are believers who heard the gospel while alive and have since died. They were judged in the flesh, dying like everyone dies, but they live in the spirit the way God does. Augustine took the dead to be the spiritually dead, unbelievers who heard and came to life. Either way, there is no second chance after death here. There is comfort for those who have buried believers.

Then verse seven, the hinge. The end of all things is at hand; therefore be self-controlled and sober-minded for the sake of your prayers. At hand is the same word as Jesus' the kingdom of God is at hand. Nothing in God's plan stands between now and the end. And notice what that nearness produces. Not panic. Not charts. A clear head for prayer. Sober-minded is the opposite of the drunkenness in verse three. The world gets drunk. You stay clear enough to pray.

Verse eight. Above all, keep loving one another earnestly, since love covers a multitude of sins. Earnestly is ektenes, stretched out, at full stretch. And love covers a multitude of sins is from Proverbs ten, verse twelve. Hatred stirs up strife, but love covers all offenses.

Here is a real disagreement. Some in the early church read this as your love covering your own sins before God. The homily we call Second Clement put it in a list with almsgiving and fasting, and medieval theology built on that. The Reformers pushed back, and Proverbs is on their side. Love covers the sins of others. It forgives, it does not keep a ledger, it does not spread a brother's failures around town. Only the blood of Christ covers sin before God. Robert Leighton gives the balance, because love is not pretending. "Such never uncover their brother's sores, but to cure them." You uncover a wound for one reason, to heal it.

Verse nine, show hospitality without grumbling. The word means a lover of strangers. The world thinks you are strange, and you open your door to strangers.

Verses ten and eleven. Use your gift to serve one another, as good stewards of God's varied grace. A steward manages another man's goods. The word varied is poikilos, many-colored, and Peter used it in chapter one for your various trials. The trials come in many colors, and so does the grace. Then two kinds of gifts. Whoever speaks, as one who speaks oracles of God. Whoever serves, by the strength that God supplies. That verb supplies first meant paying for a chorus at a public festival. God funds the whole production. So that in everything God may be glorified through Jesus Christ. To him belong glory and dominion forever and ever. Amen.

Two connections before we go.

First, the lovers. Lamentations one, verse nineteen. I called to my lovers, but they deceived me. Zion ran for years to Egypt and the gods of the nations, and in her worst hour none of them came. In John six, when the crowd walks away, Peter looks at every other option and says, Lord, to whom shall we go? Zion learned too late that her lovers had nothing. Peter learned it in time. And years later Peter tells the churches that the time already spent on lawless idolatry is enough.

Second, the bride. Lamentations pictures Jerusalem as a woman sitting alone, like a widow, and Rashi noticed the word like, because her husband would come back. The Song shows you what that coming back looks like. The bride failed him at the door, and he praises her in the same words as before. A faithless city and a faltering bride are not the same thing, and I do not want to blur them. But the same Lord stands behind both books, and Isaiah tells you what he says to Zion. I will not forget you. I, I am he who comforts you.

So here is a question to carry through your day. Is there some grief in your life where you have stopped talking to God because you think you deserved it? Lamentations says you may say both things to him. It hurts, and you are right, Lord.

This has been Old Paths. Grace and peace.
