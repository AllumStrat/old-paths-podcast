Leave your fatherless children; I will keep them alive; and let your widows trust in me.

Those are tender words. But listen to where they sit. They come in the middle of a death sentence. The verse before says of Edom, his children are destroyed, and his brothers, and his neighbors, and he is no more. The verse after says, you must drink the cup.

So which is it? Is God offering shelter to the widows of his enemies, or is he mocking a nation that will have no one left to care for them?

Hold that question.

This is Old Paths. Today is day eighteen of the tenth month in the reading plan. The readings are Jeremiah forty-nine, the Song of Songs chapter three, John chapter six, verses one through fifteen, and First Peter chapter two, verses eighteen through twenty-five.

Last time we followed a single Hebrew verb through Jeremiah's oracles. Shaqat, to be quiet, undisturbed. In chapter forty-seven the sword of the Lord could not be quiet. In chapter forty-eight Moab had been quiet far too long, settled on his dregs. Today that word turns up a third time, and Moab's easy life has a cousin out in the desert. We also watched Moab's oracle end with a surprise, yet I will restore the fortunes of Moab. Today we will see that promise given to two more nations, and withheld, pointedly, from a third.

In the Song of Songs, chapter two ended with a not yet. Until the day breathes and the shadows flee, turn, my beloved. Chapter three opens in the dark, with the bride alone and searching. In John, Jesus said Moses wrote of him, and today a crowd on a hillside decides he is the prophet Moses promised. And in First Peter, the call to submit to every human institution narrows to the hardest case of all, a slave with a cruel master.

Jeremiah forty-nine first. We are in the middle of the oracles against the nations, chapters forty-six through fifty-one. Egypt came first, then Philistia, then Moab. Today Jeremiah covers five more in one chapter: Ammon, Edom, Damascus, the Arab tribes of Kedar and Hazor, and finally Elam, far to the east.

If you want the map behind this chapter, turn back to Jeremiah twenty-five. There God hands Jeremiah the cup of the wine of his wrath and sends him to make the nations drink it. Jerusalem first, then Egypt, the Philistines, Edom, Moab, the sons of Ammon, Dedan and Tema, all the kings of Arabia, all the kings of Elam. Chapter forty-nine is that list worked out nation by nation.

Only one of these oracles has a date. Verse thirty-four says the word about Elam came in the beginning of the reign of Zedekiah. That is 597 B.C., the year Nebuchadnezzar took Jerusalem the first time, carried off King Jehoiachin, and put Zedekiah on the throne. The rest are undated.

Ammon first, verses one through six. Ammon sat north of Moab. Its capital, Rabbah, is today Amman, the capital of Jordan. The oracle opens with three sharp questions. Has Israel no sons? Has he no heir? Why then has Milcom dispossessed Gad, and his people settled in its cities?

The background is this. When the Assyrians carried off the tribes east of the Jordan, Second Kings fifteen tells the story, the land of Gad was left empty, and Ammon moved in. They acted as if Israel had died without heirs and the estate was up for grabs. God's answer is that Israel does have an heir, and the estate is not theirs.

Notice the name Milcom. The ESV footnote gives an alternative, their king. In Hebrew the god's name and the phrase their king are spelled with the same letters, malkam. Keil and Delitzsch think Jeremiah means both at once. Verse three says, Milcom shall go into exile, with his priests and his officials. Yesterday it was Chemosh going into exile on a Babylonian cart. Today it is Milcom. The god and the king leave town together.

Here is a detail that ties back to an episode last week. Remember Gedaliah, the governor of Judah after Jerusalem fell, murdered at his own table by Ishmael. Jeremiah forty, verse fourteen, tells us who sent the assassin: Baalis, king of the Ammonites. In 1984, archaeologists from Andrews University working at Tell el-Umeiri in Jordan found a seal impression from around 600 B.C. It reads, belonging to Milkom-or, servant of Baalisha. Most scholars think that Baalisha is the Baalis of Jeremiah. So when you read this oracle against Ammon, remember that its king had just arranged the murder of the one man holding Judah's survivors together.

And what did Ammon trust in? Verse four. You trusted in your treasures, saying, who will come against me? Then verse six. But afterward I will restore the fortunes of the Ammonites, declares the Lord. There it is again, shub shebut, turn the turning, the same phrase we heard over Moab yesterday.

Now Edom, verses seven through twenty-two, the longest section and the heart of the chapter. Edom lay south of the Dead Sea, and its people descended from Esau, Jacob's twin. Edom was the brother nation.

The oracle opens with a jab at Edom's reputation. Is wisdom no more in Teman? Has counsel perished from the prudent? Teman was a district of Edom famous for its wise men. Eliphaz, the first of Job's counselors, was a Temanite. Calvin's comment here is that when men abuse their knowledge, God blinds them, so that they learn they cannot be wise of themselves, but only as far as wisdom is given from above. Edom was clever. Cleverness did not save it.

Verses nine and ten. If grape gatherers came to you, would they not leave gleanings? If thieves came by night, would they not destroy only enough for themselves? But I have stripped Esau bare. Grape pickers miss a few clusters. Burglars take what they can carry. God will leave nothing. Those lines are almost word for word in the little book of Obadiah, which is entirely about Edom. Several verses in this oracle line up with Obadiah. Calvin thought Obadiah came first and Jeremiah drew on him. Others think the reverse. Either way, two prophets saw the same end for the same nation.

And then verse eleven, our opening line. Leave your fatherless children; I will keep them alive; and let your widows trust in me. This is the hardest question in the chapter, and the commentators really do divide over it.

Calvin reads it as irony. Edom, he says, had raged against orphans and women, sparing neither sex nor age. So God speaks in bitter mockery: leave your orphans to me, as if I would help the children of a people who showed no pity to anyone else's. John Gill, the Baptist, leans the same way, because verse twelve goes straight on to say, you must drink. Matthew Henry takes a third line. There will simply be no one left in Edom to say to a neighbor, leave me your orphans.

On the other side stand Keil and Delitzsch, the nineteenth-century German scholars, and with them Calvin's own English editor, who in a footnote politely says Calvin got it wrong. Keil and Delitzsch read the verse straight. "Yet the Lord will care for them, so that they shall not perish. In this comfort there is contained a very bitter truth for the Edomites who hated Jahveh." That is a striking way to say it. The men who hated God will die. Their widows and children will be cared for by the God the men hated.

The Puritan John Trapp tried to keep both readings. "Thus God speaketh to the profane Edomites in derision, but to all true Israelites in serious sadness: and so it is very comfortable, and must needs be a good stay of mind to a dying saint." A good stay of mind means something that steadies you.

Here is where I come down. Read the Hebrew as it stands and it is an invitation. A command, a promise, and a wish: leave them, I will keep them alive, let them trust in me. Nothing in the grammar signals sarcasm. And it fits the God who calls himself in Psalm sixty-eight a father of the fatherless and protector of widows, and it fits Jeremiah, who wept over Moab in the chapter before. Calvin is right that judgment on the nation is total. The fighting men are gone. But inside that judgment God opens one narrow door, and he holds it open for the weakest people in the land. The men of Edom trusted their heights and their swords. Their widows are invited to trust the Lord. That is not irony. That is grace showing up where you least expect it.

Verse twelve explains why the judgment is certain. If those who did not deserve to drink the cup must drink it, will you go unpunished? This goes straight back to chapter twenty-five, verse twenty-nine, where God begins with the city called by his name. So who are the ones who did not deserve to drink? Most commentators say Judah, not because Judah was innocent, but because it was God's own covenant people. Calvin, though, sees a difference in what the cup does. "Even medicine, though displeasing to the taste because of its bitterness, is yet wholesome; but poison kills men, though its taste is like medicine." Judah and Edom drink from the same cup. For one it is discipline that heals. For the other it is death.

Then comes Edom's real sin, verse sixteen. The horror you inspire has deceived you, and the pride of your heart, you who live in the clefts of the rock, who hold the height of the hill. Though you make your nest as high as the eagle's, I will bring you down from there.

The ESV notes that the clefts of the rock could be read as the clefts of Sela. Sela means rock, and it was an Edomite stronghold. You may picture Petra, the rose-red city carved into cliffs, and Edom did live in that country. But Ellicott reminds us that the famous carved facades at Petra are Roman, built centuries later. Jeremiah's Edomites lived in caves and fortresses on the heights, and they believed no army could reach them. One candidate for Sela, a mountain fortress a few miles north of the capital Bozrah, has a carved relief of a Babylonian king high on the cliff. Scholars connect it to Nabonidus, the last king of Babylon, whose chronicle records a campaign against Edom about 552 B.C. The nest was high. Babylon climbed up to it anyway.

Calvin draws the moral with a lovely image. "Humility has its roots fixed deeply within; so that the state of those who willingly submit themselves, becomes firm and permanent; for the root, which appears not on the surface, sustains the tree." Edom built up, toward the eagle. Humility builds down, toward the root. You cannot see a root, but it is what keeps the tree standing in a storm.

Now notice what is missing at the end of Edom's oracle. Moab got, yet I will restore. Ammon got, afterward I will restore. Edom gets nothing. Verse thirteen says God has sworn by himself that its cities shall be perpetual wastes. Verse eighteen compares it to Sodom and Gomorrah. Calvin notices the silence, and so does Trapp, and both connect it to Edom's peculiar guilt. Edom was the brother. When Jerusalem fell, Psalm one hundred thirty-seven remembers the Edomites shouting, lay it bare, lay it bare, down to its foundations. Obadiah says, you stood aloof, you were like one of them. Treachery from a stranger is one thing. Treachery from your brother is another.

Did any Edomite ever come home? Here the New Testament genuinely adds to the picture. Edom as a nation vanished, and its remnant became the Idumeans of the southern Judean hills. Listen to Max Rogland, writing for Ligonier on the book of Obadiah. "This loss of their ancestral land and national identity proved to be a blessing in disguise, as people from Idumea were among those drawn to follow Jesus the Messiah." He is pointing to Mark three, verse eight, where crowds come to Jesus from Judea, Jerusalem, and Idumea. The nation had no promise. Edomites still found the door. Verse eleven was not a joke.

Now three shorter oracles. Damascus, verses twenty-three through twenty-seven, stands for the Aramean kingdoms of Syria. Hamath and Arpad are confounded. They melt in fear, and then this phrase: they are troubled like the sea that cannot be quiet. There is our verb again. Shaqat. The sword of the Lord could not be quiet. Moab was quiet too long. Now Damascus is like a sea that cannot be quieted. Isaiah fifty-seven uses the same image: the wicked are like the tossing sea, for it cannot be quiet.

Spurgeon wrote an evening devotional on this verse, and he heard in it a word about this whole restless world. "This is not our rest, and the restless billows tell us so." Then he points to a land where there is no more sea.

Kedar and Hazor, verses twenty-eight through thirty-three. Kedar was an Arab tribe descended from Ishmael, tent dwellers with camels and flocks. Hazor here is not the old Canaanite city in Galilee. The word means unwalled settlements, the villages of semi-nomadic Arabs. And the heading names Nebuchadnezzar directly. The Babylonian Chronicle for his sixth year, 599 B.C., says he sent his troops into the desert and they took much plunder from the Arabs, their possessions, animals, and gods. That is verse twenty-nine. Their tents and their flocks shall be taken, their camels shall be led away.

Listen to verse thirty-one, because it should ring a bell. Rise up, advance against a nation at ease, that dwells securely, that has no gates or bars, that dwells alone. Moab was at ease from his youth. Kedar was at ease in the open desert, too remote and too poor, it thought, for any empire to bother with. Calvin asks why God would bother announcing the fall of a few distant desert tribes, and his answer is that the faithful needed to know that even the chaos at the edges of the map was under God's hand.

And finally Elam, verses thirty-four through thirty-nine. Elam lay east of Babylon in what is now southwestern Iran, with its capital at Susa. The Elamites were famous archers. Isaiah twenty-two says, Elam bore the quiver. So God says, I will break the bow of Elam, the mainstay of their might. He will scatter them to the four winds. And then verse thirty-eight. I will set my throne in Elam. Within a couple of years of this oracle, the Babylonian Chronicle records a standoff in which the king of Elam was afraid and, in panic, returned to his own land.

The chapter ends like Ammon's oracle. But in the latter days I will restore the fortunes of Elam. Calvin, Henry, Gill, Poole, and Trapp all point to Acts two, verse nine. At Pentecost, among the crowd that heard the apostles speak of the mighty works of God in their own languages, were Parthians and Medes and Elamites. That is our second and last New Testament move in this segment, and it is a fair one, though the Pulpit Commentary rightly warns that a few pilgrims at Pentecost would be a meager fulfillment by themselves. Elam stands for the far-off nations. The promise is fulfilled every time people from the ends of the earth come to know the God of Israel.

So bring it home. Look at what every nation in this chapter trusted. Ammon trusted treasures. Edom trusted wisdom and the height of its rock. Kedar trusted distance. Elam trusted the bow. Every one of them is a kind of nest built high, a place where you think nothing can reach you.

What is your eagle's nest? Your savings? Your competence? A life arranged so that no one can get close enough to hurt you? None of these are evil in themselves. But they make a terrible place to put your trust. In this entire chapter there is only one offer of safety, and it is not a rock or a bow. It is a person, speaking to the people with the least to hold on to. Let your widows trust in me. Climb down from the nest. Put down roots.

Now the Song of Songs, chapter three. The chapter has two scenes, and they could hardly be more different. The first is a woman alone at night. The second is a royal wedding procession in broad daylight.

Verse one. On my bed by night I sought him whom my soul loves; I sought him, but found him not. The word for night is plural, nights. The Cambridge Bible suggests she is telling of something that happened again and again, perhaps a recurring dream. D. A. Carson leans toward a dream as well. And it does have that quality. She gets up, walks the streets, the watchmen find her, and almost at once she finds him. That happens in dreams. There is a darker twin to this scene in chapter five, where the watchmen beat her. Here the search ends well.

Notice how she names him. Not by name. Four times, him whom my soul loves. When you love someone like that, the description says more than the name.

Then verse four. I held him, and would not let him go. The older interpreters heard Jacob at the Jabbok, wrestling the angel: I will not let you go unless you bless me. Spurgeon preached on this verse in 1877 and asked how a weak sinner could hold on to Christ. His answer: "If Omnipotence helps you to hold Omnipotence, why, then, the deed is done!" Your grip on Christ is real, but it is his grip on you that makes it hold.

She brings him into my mother's house, into the chamber of her who conceived me. Carson reads this as signaling official consummation. The relationship is moving into the family and toward the marriage bed. The Puritans read the mother's house as the visible church, and James Durham, whom we met last time, made a great deal of it. But Matthew Poole, who could allegorize with the best of them, put a wise limit on it right here. "But all particulars in allegorical scriptures are not to be strictly urged, as all learned interpreters agree, many being added only for the decency of the allegory." That is the position we have held in this series. Read the plain sense first. See Christ and his church through the lens Paul gives in Ephesians five. Do not make every bedpost mean something.

And then the refrain for the second time. Do not stir up or awaken love until it pleases. She has just found him and held him, and she still says, wait. Desire is not the same as readiness.

Verse six changes everything. What is that coming up from the wilderness like columns of smoke, perfumed with myrrh and frankincense? The same question comes back in chapter eight, about the bride leaning on her beloved. Here the answer is, Behold, it is the litter of Solomon. A litter is a couch carried on poles, and sixty mighty men surround it against terror by night. In verse nine it becomes a carriage of Lebanon wood with silver posts, a gold back, and a purple seat. The Hebrew word, appiryon, appears only here and seems to be a loanword, perhaps from Greek or Persian. It is exotic, the finest thing anyone has seen.

Its interior was inlaid with love by the daughters of Jerusalem. The preposition is from. The women of the city did the work, and they did it lovingly.

Verse eleven. Go out, O daughters of Zion, and look upon King Solomon, with the crown with which his mother crowned him on the day of his wedding, on the day of the gladness of his heart. His mother is Bathsheba. Jewish grooms wore crowns at their weddings, and the Mishnah records that the rabbis abolished bridegroom crowns during the war with Vespasian, when Jerusalem was about to fall. Joy was suspended for a time of grief.

So what is the wisdom here? Put the two scenes side by side. The night search is private and anxious. The procession is public, guarded, and blessed by family. Love that is real does not stay hidden in the dark. It goes to the mother's house. It gets witnesses, a crown, a public day. That is what a wedding is, a covenant made in the open and guarded by the community. If you are married, that day was meant to be the gladness of your heart. If you are walking toward it, do not settle for the night search. Love wants a wedding.

John chapter six, verses one through fifteen. John has just recorded Jesus's long defense in Jerusalem. Now, after this, we are back in Galilee, on the far side of the lake. Verse four tells us the Passover was at hand. This is the only miracle of Jesus, apart from the resurrection, recorded in all four Gospels.

A large crowd is coming. And Jesus turns to Philip. Where are we to buy bread, so that these people may eat? Verse six: he said this to test him, for he himself knew what he would do. Why Philip? Luke tells us they were near Bethsaida, and John has told us Philip was from Bethsaida. He was the local man. But the fathers noticed something else. Cyril of Alexandria, the fifth-century bishop, remembered what kind of disciple Philip was. "Philip then was a questioner and apt to learn, but not over quick in ready power of understanding the more Divine." This is the Philip who will say in chapter fourteen, Lord, show us the Father. So Jesus tests him.

Philip does the math. Two hundred days' wages would not buy enough for each to get a little. Spurgeon said Philip counted up what would not be enough and left the all-sufficient Lord out of the reckoning. We fail that test daily. We count the resources and forget who is standing next to us.

Andrew finds a boy with five barley loaves and two fish. Barley was the poor man's bread. Some hear an echo of Elisha in Second Kings four, where a man brings twenty barley loaves and the servant says, how can I set this before a hundred men? And they ate and had some left. R. C. Sproul calls the connection speculative, and he may be right, but the pattern is close: barley loaves, a doubting helper, more left over than there was at the start.

Jesus has them sit down on the grass. There was much grass, which fits the spring, the Passover season. He gives thanks, and the bread keeps coming through the disciples' hands. Then verse twelve, and only John tells us Jesus commanded it. Gather up the leftover fragments, that nothing may be lost. Twelve baskets. The fathers saw in the leftovers proof that this was no illusion. I will add one observation of my own. Later in this same chapter, Jesus says, this is the will of him who sent me, that I should lose nothing of all that he has given me. The same Lord who will not lose a crust of barley bread will not lose one of his people.

Verse fourteen. When the people saw the sign, they said, this is indeed the Prophet who is to come into the world. That is Deuteronomy eighteen, the prophet like Moses. Last time Jesus said, Moses wrote of me. Now here is a crowd fed in the wilderness at Passover time, and they connect it to Moses and the manna. They are right about the prophet. Chrysostom noticed when they said it, though. Jesus had done greater things, and they confessed him only once their stomachs were full.

And verse fifteen. Perceiving that they were about to come and take him by force to make him king, Jesus withdrew again to the mountain by himself. Passover was the feast of liberation from a foreign king, and in Jesus's day it was a tinderbox of nationalism. Here was a man who could feed an army in the desert. Sproul put the problem in one line. "They were looking for the kingdom of man, and He came to bring the kingdom of God." And John Piper said this. "Jesus didn't come into the world to lend his power to already existing appetites."

The crowd wanted Jesus. They wanted him badly enough to use force. But they wanted him for what they already wanted. And he walked away. If you want Jesus as the king of your appetites, the one who will make your plans work, he will withdraw to the mountain. He will be king, but on his terms, and his road to the throne runs through a cross the crowd could not imagine.

First Peter two, verses eighteen through twenty-five. Peter has told his churches to be subject for the Lord's sake to every human institution. Now he speaks to household servants. The word is oiketai, domestic slaves. In the Roman world they might be cooks, tutors, secretaries, even physicians, but they were still property. Peter tells them, be subject to your masters with all respect, not only to the good and gentle but also to the unjust. The footnote gives the word's root sense: crooked. Skolios.

Does that mean Peter approves of slavery? No. Notice what he does. He speaks to slaves directly, as moral agents accountable to God, which ancient household codes rarely did. And he calls the cruel master crooked. Robert Leighton, the seventeenth-century Scottish archbishop whose commentary on this letter has been read for three centuries, says the text does not justify or excuse such masters in the least. Albert Barnes, an American Presbyterian who opposed slavery, said this passage cannot be used to prove Peter approved of it. Peter is teaching believers how to live faithfully inside an unjust structure. He is not blessing the structure.

Verse nineteen. This is a gracious thing, when, mindful of God, one endures sorrows while suffering unjustly. The word is charis, grace. There is no credit in enduring a beating you deserved. But to suffer for doing good, and endure it with your eyes on God rather than on the master, that is grace at work.

Then verse twenty-one opens the door to the rest of the passage. Christ also suffered for you, leaving you an example, so that you might follow in his steps. The word for example is hupogrammos. It means the model letters a writing master put across the top of a child's copybook for the child to trace. Leighton takes the image and runs with it. "Every step of His is a letter of this copy; and particularly in this point of suffering. He wrote us a pure and perfect copy of obedience, in clear and great letters, in His own blood."

Then Peter quotes Isaiah fifty-three line by line. He committed no sin, neither was deceit found in his mouth. That is Isaiah fifty-three, verse nine. When he was reviled, he did not revile in return; when he suffered, he did not threaten. Remember who is writing. Peter was in the high priest's courtyard the night Jesus was struck and mocked, while he himself swore he never knew the man. He is not writing theory. Jesus continued entrusting himself to him who judges justly. Calvin says those who long for revenge do not really leave God his office as judge. They want him as their executioner. Entrusting yourself means letting God be the judge, on his timetable.

But if Peter stopped there, Christ would be only a model. And Alexander MacLaren, the Baptist preacher of Manchester, put the problem sharply. "If a copyhead could save the world it would have been saved long ago. Patterns of good are plenty; the mischief is we don't copy them." A copyhead is that model line at the top of the page. We need more than a pattern.

So verse twenty-four. He himself bore our sins in his body on the tree. The tree is the word Peter used in his sermons in Acts, and it reaches back to Deuteronomy twenty-one, which Paul quotes as, cursed is everyone who is hanged on a tree. Christ took the curse. By his wounds you have been healed. The word for wound means the welt raised by a whip. Think of who first heard this letter read aloud: slaves with welts on their own backs, told that they were healed by the welts on his.

One of the earliest Christian writers outside the New Testament, Polycarp of Smyrna, a disciple of the apostle John, wrote to the Philippians and put these verses together exactly as Peter does. He calls Christ the one "who bore our sins in His own body on the tree," and then he says, "Let us then be imitators of His patience; and if we suffer for His name's sake, let us glorify Him." Substitute first, then example. He bore what we could not, so we can follow where he walked.

And verse twenty-five. You were straying like sheep, Isaiah fifty-three, verse six, but have now returned to the Shepherd and Overseer of your souls. The slave had a crooked master on earth. He had a Shepherd who watched over his soul.

Two connections before we go, and I think both are really there.

First, Peter himself. In John six, Peter was among the disciples who carried the barley bread out to the crowd. He watched them try to crown Jesus by force, and he watched Jesus walk away. Thirty years later, in this letter, he writes about the throne Jesus actually took. Not by a crowd's force, but by a tree, with wounds. Peter finally understood the kind of king he had been following.

Second, trust. In Jeremiah forty-nine, God says to the widows of Edom, let your widows trust in me. In First Peter two, Jesus, reviled and beaten, continued entrusting himself to him who judges justly. The weakest people in a ruined nation and the Son of God at the cross take the same posture. Hands open, the outcome left with God.

So here is a question to carry through your day. What nest are you building high, and what would it look like today to climb down and entrust yourself, and the people you love, to the one who judges justly?

Next time we read Jeremiah fifty, as the oracles finally turn on Babylon itself, the Song of Songs chapter four, verses one through seven, John six, verses sixteen through twenty-four, where Jesus walks on the sea, and First Peter three, verses one through seven.

This has been Old Paths. Grace and peace.
