Daniel chapter twelve, verse thirteen. But go your way till the end. And you shall rest and shall stand in your allotted place at the end of the days.

That is the last thing the angel says to Daniel. He has just been shown kings and wars and a resurrection, and he admits he does not understand. The answer is not more explanation. It is, go your way. Could you live with that?

This is Old Paths. Today is day twenty-five of the eleventh month in the reading plan, day two hundred seventy-five of three hundred. The readings are Daniel ten through twelve, Job twenty-two, John thirteen, verses thirty-one through thirty-eight, and Jude, verses seventeen through twenty-five.

Last time we heard Daniel pray with Jeremiah open in front of him, and Gabriel came in swift flight with the seventy weeks. Today Daniel prays again, and this time the answer is delayed three weeks. The reason involves Michael, the same archangel Jude mentioned in his ninth verse. Today we also finish Jude, and Job's friends begin their third and final round.

Daniel ten through twelve.

These three chapters are one vision, the last in the book. Chapter ten is the prologue. Chapter eleven and the first verses of twelve are the message. The rest of twelve is the epilogue.

Verse one dates it to the third year of Cyrus king of Persia. That is about 536 B.C., two years after the prayer of chapter nine. Cyrus has already let the exiles go home, but Daniel, now in his mid-eighties, stayed. And Ezra four tells us the rebuilding was frustrated all the days of Cyrus. So when Daniel mourns for three weeks, straight through Passover, it is not hard to guess why. The promise had been kept, and the work had stalled.

Then he sees a man clothed in linen, with a face like lightning and eyes like flaming torches. Daniel falls on his face. A hand sets him trembling on his hands and knees. Then one touches his lips. Then one touches him again and says, verse nineteen, O man greatly loved, fear not, peace be with you; be strong and of good courage. Three touches before he can stand. Spurgeon, preaching on that verse in 1873, said he was glad the Bible shows Daniel on his face, because it shows a great saint owed everything to grace.

Now the strangest verses in the chapter. Verse twelve. From the first day that you set your heart to understand and humbled yourself before your God, your words have been heard, and I have come because of your words. Verse thirteen. The prince of the kingdom of Persia withstood me twenty-one days, but Michael, one of the chief princes, came to help me.

Who is this prince of Persia? Calvin thought it was the Persian king himself, whom the angel was restraining. John Piper reads it as a spiritual power over the empire, and since Michael is called a prince in the same verse, I think Piper is right. Piper made one observation I can't get past. He wrote, But it's no accident that the messenger said that his struggle with the prince of Persia lasted exactly the same amount of time that Daniel's fasting and prayer did, twenty-one days. The reason for this is that the warfare in the spirit realm was being fought in a real sense by Daniel in the prayer realm.

Then he adds a guardrail. Daniel was not praying about angels, and Piper doubts that we should either. Daniel never knew about the battle until it was over. He just kept asking God.

Chapter eleven.

This is the most detailed prophecy in the Bible, and you need the history to follow it. Verse two. Three more kings in Persia, and a fourth far richer who stirs up all against Greece. That is Xerxes, who invaded Greece in 480 B.C. Verse three, a mighty king, Alexander the Great. Verse four, his kingdom broken toward the four winds and not given to his descendants. Then from verse five on, the king of the south and the king of the north. South means the Ptolemies in Egypt. North means the Seleucids in Syria. And the little land of Judah sits between them, passed back and forth for a century and a half.

Verse after verse matches known events. Verse sixteen, the north takes the glorious land, Judea, around 200 B.C.

Then verse twenty-one. A contemptible person to whom royal majesty has not been given. This is Antiochus the Fourth. He called himself Epiphanes, God made manifest. His enemies called him Epimanes, the madman. Verse thirty, ships of Kittim come against him. In 168 B.C. a Roman envoy drew a circle in the sand around Antiochus and told him to leave Egypt before he stepped out of it. He backed down, and took his humiliation out on Jerusalem. Verse thirty-one. They shall profane the temple and take away the regular burnt offering, and set up the abomination that makes desolate. In December of 167 B.C. an altar to Zeus went up in the temple.

Then verse thirty-two. The people who know their God shall stand firm and take action. That was the Maccabees.

Ligon Duncan, preaching this chapter, asked why God would put so much Seleucid history in the Bible. Here is his answer. Remember, as we look at these verses, that prophecy is not merely telling history beforehand. Prophecy is God's interpretation of that history. Alexander gets two verses. Antiochus gets fifteen. Heaven measures kings by what they do to the people of God.

Now the hard question. This chapter is so exact that many scholars say it must have been written after the events. That objection is old. A pagan philosopher named Porphyry made it in the third century. Jerome answered him in the preface to his commentary on Daniel, and he turned the objection inside out. But this very attack testifies to Daniel's accuracy. For so striking was the reliability of what the prophet foretold, that he could not appear to unbelievers as a predicter of the future, but rather a narrator of things already past.

The real question is whether God rules history. If he does, he can tell it to his servant ahead of time.

There is a second hard question. Verse thirty-six. The king shall do as he wills. He shall exalt himself and magnify himself above every god. Who is that? The critics say it is still Antiochus. But verse forty-five says this king meets his end between the sea and the glorious holy mountain, and Antiochus died far away in Persia. Calvin said the angel jumps here to the Roman Empire. Jerome, and the Puritan Matthew Poole after him, said Antiochus is the type, and this is the Antichrist at the end. I go with Jerome. Paul borrows this very language for the man of lawlessness, who exalts himself against every so-called god. And look what comes next.

Chapter twelve, verse one. At that time shall arise Michael, the great prince who has charge of your people. A time of trouble such as never has been, but your people shall be delivered, everyone whose name is written in the book. Verse two. And many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life, and some to shame and everlasting contempt.

This is the clearest statement of bodily resurrection in the Old Testament. John Gill notes that the phrase everlasting life appears here for the first time in the Old Testament. And when Jesus said in John five that all who are in the tombs will hear his voice and come out, some to life and some to judgment, he was quoting this verse.

Then verse three, and here is the word to watch. Those who are wise shall shine like the brightness of the sky above. The Hebrew is maskilim, the wise, or those who make others wise. In chapter eleven the maskilim teach many and then fall by sword and flame. Here they shine. And those who turn many to righteousness uses almost the same Hebrew as Isaiah fifty-three, where the Servant makes many to be accounted righteous. Daniel's wise teachers walk the Servant's road. They suffer, they make many righteous, and then they are lifted up.

Then the epilogue. Seal the book. A time, times, and half a time. Twelve hundred ninety days, then thirteen hundred thirty-five. And verse eight is Daniel's own confession. I heard, but I did not understand. He asks, O my lord, what shall be the outcome? And the angel does not explain. Go your way, Daniel. Calvin reads verse thirteen in two steps. You shall rest, that means you will die. And you shall stand, that means your death will not be the end of you.

So here is the application. You will not get every answer about the end. Daniel didn't. But you can be one of the wise in your own corner. Know your God, stand firm, help someone else understand, and go your way.

Job twenty-two.

This opens the third and final round of speeches. Eliphaz goes first, and for the first time he names specific sins. Verse six. You have exacted pledges of your brothers for nothing and stripped the naked of their clothing. Verse seven, you withheld water and bread. Verse nine, you sent widows away empty.

None of it is true. Job will answer these charges one by one in chapter thirty-one. Eliphaz has no evidence. His theology needs Job to be a great sinner, so he supplies the sins.

And then, in verses twenty-one through thirty, he says some of the most beautiful things in the book. Agree with God, and be at peace. Lay up his words in your heart. The Almighty will be your gold. Matthew Henry felt the strain. Methinks I can almost forgive Eliphaz his hard censures of Job, which we had in the beginning of the chapter, though they were very unjust and unkind, for this good counsel. Then he adds, But it is strange that out of the same mouth, and almost in the same breath, both sweet waters and bitter should proceed.

Now verse thirty. The King James has, he shall deliver the island of the innocent, which is hard to make sense of. Gill and Barnes both explain that the Hebrew word there is a negative, so the line means God delivers even the one who is not innocent, through the cleanness of your hands. And here is the irony. Albert Barnes saw it. At the end of the book, God is angry with Eliphaz, and it is Job who prays for him. The guilty man is delivered by Job's clean hands, exactly as Eliphaz said. He just had the roles backward.

The lesson is plain. You can say true things about God and still be wrong about your friend.

John thirteen, verses thirty-one through thirty-eight.

We left Judas going out into the night. The very next words are verse thirty-one. When he had gone out, Jesus said, Now is the Son of Man glorified. Piper noticed the timing. At the very moment the betrayal is set in motion, Jesus says now. In John's Gospel the hour of glory is the cross.

Verse thirty-three. Little children, the only time in this Gospel he calls them that. He is leaving, and he gives them one thing to hold. Verse thirty-four. A new commandment I give to you, that you love one another: just as I have loved you, you also are to love one another.

But love your neighbor is in Leviticus nineteen. What is new? The measure is new. Just as I have loved you. He has just washed their feet, Judas's included, and in a few hours he will die for them. R. C. Sproul, in his commentary on John, said Jesus demanded from His disciples and all who would follow Him a love that has no place for treason. And Piper adds that Jesus is both the pattern of that love and the power for it.

Then Peter. Lord, where are you going? You cannot follow me now, but you will follow afterward. Augustine noticed the mercy in that word now. Not never, only not yet. But Peter wants it now. I will lay down my life for you. And Jesus answers with a question. Will you lay down your life for me? The rooster will not crow till you have denied me three times.

Chrysostom saw a purpose in the fall. Christ left Peter to himself so that he would learn his own weakness. He writes, he was so beside himself, that in a short time thrice did he utter the words of denial, that he might learn that he did not so love as he was loved. Peter thought his love would carry him. He had to learn that Christ's love was carrying him.

Jude, verses seventeen through twenty-five.

This finishes the letter. After sixteen verses about the false teachers, Jude turns. But you, beloved. Remember the predictions of the apostles, that there will be scoffers, worldly people, devoid of the Spirit. Peter's second letter says nearly the same words.

Then verses twenty and twenty-one give one command with three supports. Keep yourselves in the love of God. How? By building yourselves up in your most holy faith, by praying in the Holy Spirit, and by waiting for the mercy of our Lord Jesus Christ.

Then mercy toward the wavering. Have mercy on those who doubt; save others by snatching them out of the fire. Calvin said that when a house is burning you do not beckon with a finger. You grab.

And then the doxology. Now to him who is able to keep you from stumbling. Watch the word keep. In verse one Jude's readers are kept for Jesus Christ. In verse twenty-one, keep yourselves. In verse twenty-four, he is able to keep you. So who does the keeping? The Puritan Thomas Manton answered it in one line. The apostle requireth duty of the faithful, but asketh grace of God. The Westminster Confession, chapter seventeen, says the same. The saints persevere because God preserves them.

The word in verse twenty-four is specific. Not just falling, but stumbling. Spurgeon has a sermon on it, and he said, A horse may stumble and not fall; yet it is a sort of falling. God is able to keep you from the stumble that leads to the fall, and to present you blameless before his glory, with great joy.

Here is the connection. Stumbling runs through three of today's readings. Peter was sure he would not stumble, and before morning he did. In Daniel eleven the wise stumble by sword and flame, so that they may be refined and made white. And Jude ends with the one who is able to keep you from stumbling. Peter's confidence was in his own love. Jude's confidence is in God's power. And Daniel is told he will stand in his allotted place, because God will raise him.

So carry this question through the day. Where are you saying, like Peter, I will never fall, when you ought to be saying, keep me?
