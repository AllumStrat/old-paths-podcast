A man in linen walks through Jerusalem with a scribe's writing case at his belt. Behind him come six men with weapons. He is looking for faces. Not the powerful, not the priests. He is looking for people who sigh and groan over what is being done in the city. When he finds one, he dips his pen and makes a mark on the forehead, a small cross-shaped sign, God's signature. Then the swords pass by.

If he walked through your town this morning, would he stop at you?

This is Old Paths. Today is day three of the eleventh month in the reading plan, day two hundred fifty-three of three hundred. The readings are Ezekiel chapters nine through twelve, Job chapter three, John chapter eight, verses one through eleven, and Second Peter chapter two, verses one through nine.

Last time we followed Ezekiel through the hole in the wall into the room of pictures, and we heard the elders say, the Lord does not see us. I told you the glory would start to leave. Today it does. Step by step it goes out to the Mount of Olives, and in between God marks the mourners and promises a heart of flesh for a heart of stone.

Job, who sat for seven days in silence, finally opens his mouth, and what comes out is a curse on the day he was born. In John, we reach the story of the woman caught in adultery, and the brackets around it in your Bible. And in Second Peter, after a chapter about true prophets carried along by the Spirit, Peter turns to the false ones.

Ezekiel chapters nine through twelve.

We are still inside the vision that began in chapter eight. It is late summer of 592 B.C. Ezekiel is sitting in his house in Tel-abib with the elders of Judah, and at the same time, in the vision, he is standing in the temple courts in Jerusalem. Zedekiah is king. The city has about six years left. And the people in it still believe that because the Lord lives in the temple, the city cannot fall.

These chapters answer that belief, and they are built around one slow movement. The glory of the Lord leaves. In chapter nine, verse three, it rises from the cherub to the threshold of the house. In chapter ten, verse eighteen, it goes out from the threshold and stands over the cherubim at the east gate. In chapter eleven, verse twenty-three, it rises from the city and stands on the mountain to the east, the Mount of Olives. Chapter twelve is a new message, acted out in a Babylonian street.

Matthew Henry noticed how slowly God walks out. "God's departures from a people are gradual, but gracious souls are soon aware of the first step he takes towards a remove." Nobody in Jerusalem noticed. Ezekiel did.

Chapter nine. Six executioners come from the north gate, the direction Babylon would come from, each with a weapon. With them is a seventh, a man clothed in linen, with a writing case at his waist. Then verse four. Pass through the city, through Jerusalem, and put a mark on the foreheads of the men who sigh and groan over all the abominations that are committed in it.

Notice who gets the mark. The mark goes on people who sigh and groan. In Hebrew the two verbs sound alike, ne'enachim and ne'enaqim, a groan said twice.

Henry presses that home. It is not enough, he says, to keep clear of other people's sins. We must mourn for them and lay them to heart. And he names two examples: David in Psalm one hundred nineteen, and Lot. Hold onto Lot. We will meet him again before this episode is over.

The word for mark is tav, which is also the name of the last letter of the Hebrew alphabet. In the old Hebrew script of Ezekiel's day, that letter was written as a simple X or a plus sign. In Job thirty-one, verse thirty-five, Job says, here is my signature, and the word is tav. So the man in linen writes God's signature on their foreheads.

You can guess what the early church did with that. Tertullian, writing against Marcion around the year 207, quotes this verse and says, "Now the Greek letter Tau and our own letter T is the very form of the cross, which He predicted would be the sign on our foreheads". Matthew Poole, the Puritan, thought it was a waste of time. "It is too curious, and as useless, to inquire what mark this was." The point, he says, is that the remnant is safe, like something under a seal no one dares break. Keil and Delitzsch take the middle road. There is no direct prophecy of the cross here, they say, but the coincidence is remarkable to anyone who watches the ways of God.

I am with Keil. The mark means ownership. But here the New Testament steps in first, one of only two times I will go there today. In Revelation seven an angel says, do not harm the earth until we have sealed the servants of our God on their foreheads. John is taking up Ezekiel nine. God marks his own before he judges.

Then the order to the six. Pass through the city after him. Calvin noticed the order of march. God first takes care of his own and hides them under his wings, and only then lets his wrath burn. The scribe goes first. The swords come behind.

Now the hard question. Did the mark really work? Calvin asks it out loud. Jeremiah, who certainly grieved over Jerusalem, was dragged to Egypt against his will. Meanwhile plenty of wicked people survived the fall of the city. If you count bodies, the mark did not spare all the godly and only the godly. Calvin's answer is honest. When God forbids the executioners to come near the marked, "he does not mean them to be free from all injury or disadvantage, but he promises that they should be so separated from the ungodly, that they should acknowledge by sure experience that God was never forgetful of his faith and promise."

That matters for you. The mark is not a promise that nothing hard will happen to you. The mark is a promise that you belong to God, and that what happens to you will never be the judgment that falls on the wicked. The same fire can be a sentence for one man and a refining for another.

Verse eight. While they were striking, and I was left alone, I fell upon my face, and cried, Ah, Lord God! Will you destroy all the remnant of Israel? And God's answer in verse nine quotes the elders from chapter eight back to them. For they say, the Lord has forsaken the land, and the Lord does not see. They said God does not see. God says he sees everything, and he will not look away.

Chapter ten repeats the throne vision of chapter one, but now it is leaving. The man in linen, who marked the mourners, is told to fill his hands with burning coals from between the cherubim and scatter them over the city. Remember Isaiah six? A seraph took a coal from the altar, touched the prophet's lips, and his guilt was taken away. Here the coals from God's presence do not cleanse. They burn.

How could God leave, when Psalm one hundred thirty-two says, this is my resting place forever? Calvin says they thought the sun would fall from heaven sooner than God would leave that temple. His answer is that God's promise was interrupted, not broken. The temple stood before God on his promise even while it lay in ruins before men. And Ezekiel will see the glory come back through the east gate in chapter forty-three.

Chapter eleven brings us to the east gate, where twenty-five leading men are in session. Their slogan is verse three. The time is not near to build houses. This city is the cauldron, and we are the meat. Calvin thinks they were mocking Jeremiah, who had told the exiles to build houses in Babylon. The meat in the pot is the choice cut. The fire is outside. We are safe inside.

God answers. The bodies of the people you murdered are the meat. You will be dragged out and judged at the border of Israel. And when the city fell, its leading men were executed at Riblah, near the northern border.

Then verse thirteen. While I was prophesying, Pelatiah the son of Benaiah died. Look at the names. Pelatiah means the Lord rescues. Benaiah means the Lord builds. The son of the Lord builds sits in the council that says, do not build. The man named the Lord rescues dies on the spot. And Ezekiel falls on his face again. Ah, Lord God! Will you make a full end of the remnant of Israel?

Now comes the turn of the whole vision. Ezekiel asks about the remnant, and God tells him he is looking in the wrong place. Verse fifteen. Son of man, your brothers, even your brothers, your kinsmen, the whole house of Israel. Kinsmen is literally the men of your redemption. And the people of Jerusalem say of them, Go far from the Lord; to us this land is given.

Calvin reads this as a gentle rebuke to the prophet. Ezekiel was measuring the future of God's people by the men at the east gate. God points him to the exiles sitting in his own house. The church, Calvin says, is often preserved in hiding places, among ordinary people the world thinks nothing of, not among the men with the impressive titles.

Then verse sixteen. Though I removed them far off among the nations, yet I have been a sanctuary to them for a while in the countries where they have gone. The Hebrew is miqdash me'at. Me'at can mean for a little while, or in a small measure, so the King James says, I will be to them as a little sanctuary. Both are true.

Spurgeon preached on this verse in January of 1888. He noticed that a cruel word from the Jerusalem crowd drew a tender word from God. And he hears God saying, "I will be a travelling temple to them. I will be as a little sanctuary to each one of them." Henry puts the contrast simply. Jerusalem had the temple without God. Babylon had God without the temple.

Then verses nineteen and twenty. I will give them one heart, and a new spirit I will put within them. I will remove the heart of stone from their flesh and give them a heart of flesh, that they may walk in my statutes. And they shall be my people, and I will be their God. Calvin says one heart does not mean everyone agreeing. It is the opposite of a divided heart, a heart no longer split between the Lord and the room of pictures. And he gives the image that makes the verse plain: "a stone by its own hardness repels even the strongest blows of the hammers, and nothing can be inscribed on it; but the fleshy heart by its softness admits whatever is inscribed or engraven on it." That is my second New Testament step, because Paul takes this exact image in Second Corinthians three, when he calls the Corinthians a letter written not on tablets of stone but on tablets of human hearts.

Notice what this does to Ezekiel's question. He asked, will you make a full end of the remnant? God answers, I will make a remnant, by changing their hearts. God does not lower his justice. He does heart surgery. A Ligonier devotional on this chapter recalls R.C. Sproul's three-word summary of salvation: regeneration precedes faith. Our Confession says the same in chapter ten. God does not wait for stone to soften itself.

Then the glory goes up to the Mount of Olives and stops there, looking back at the city. Henry has an old proverb for it: loath to depart bids oft farewell.

Chapter twelve takes us back to Babylon and another sign. Ezekiel packs a refugee's bag in daylight. At dusk, in front of everyone, he digs through the mud-brick wall of his house with his own hands, shoulders the bag, covers his face, and goes out. Yesterday he dug through a wall to see hidden sin. Today he digs to show where it ends up.

The sign is about the prince in Jerusalem, Zedekiah. He will dig through a wall at night, cover his face, and run. Verse thirteen: I will spread my net over him, and I will bring him to Babylon, yet he shall not see it, and he shall die there.

Here is something you will enjoy. The Jewish historian Josephus says Zedekiah heard both prophets and decided they contradicted each other. Jeremiah had told him that his eyes would see the eyes of the king of Babylon. Ezekiel said he would never see Babylon. So, Josephus says, Zedekiah "disbelieved what they both appeared to agree in, and condemned them as not speaking truth therein, although all the things foretold him did come to pass according to their prophecies". In 586 he fled through a gap in the wall, was caught, and at Riblah he saw the king of Babylon face to face before they put out his eyes and led him to Babylon in chains. He saw Nebuchadnezzar. He never saw Babylon. Both prophets were right. A man who hunts for contradictions in God's word so that he can ignore it will usually find one. It will not save him.

The chapter ends with two proverbs. The first: The days grow long, and every vision comes to nothing. The second: He prophesies of times far off. One says it will never happen. The other says it will not happen soon. Henry thinks the second is the more dangerous. When the devil cannot persuade you that judgment is uncertain, he persuades you that it is far away, and then it loses its force. God answers both proverbs the same way. None of my words will be delayed any longer.

So here is what I want you to carry from Ezekiel today, and it comes straight from chapter nine, verse four. The man with the writing case was looking for one thing: people who sigh and groan over what is done in the city. Not people who are clever about it, or angry about it online. People who grieve. This week, when something makes you angry, take it to the Lord in grief before you take it to anyone else as commentary. That is the forehead he marks.

Job chapter three.

After seven days of silence, verse one says, Job opened his mouth. What comes out of Job is a curse on the day he was born.

Here the book changes. Chapters one and two are prose. From chapter three to the end of the speeches, it is poetry, Hebrew poetry built on parallel lines, the second line echoing or sharpening the first. Let the day perish on which I was born, and the night that said, a man is conceived.

It falls into three movements: curse the day, why did I not die at birth, and why does life go on for the miserable.

Now, the first question every reader asks. Satan said Job would curse God to his face. His wife told him to curse God and die. And now, verse one, he curses. Has Satan won? No. Notice what he curses. John Trapp, the Puritan, puts it in three Latin words: "Diem, non Deum; his day, and not his God, as the devil would have had it." And the Hebrew agrees. In chapters one and two the word for curse was barak, literally bless, used as a polite cover word. Here it is a different verb, qalal, which means to treat as light or worthless. Job is not cursing God. He is saying his birthday should never have counted.

The first stanza is an undoing of creation. God said, let there be light. Job says of his birthday, let that day be darkness. Christopher Ash points out that it is Genesis one in reverse. He even calls on those who rouse up Leviathan, the sea monster of chaos.

The second stanza turns to death, and it is surprisingly peaceful. There the wicked cease from troubling, and there the weary are at rest. Kings and counselors lie there, and the slave is free from his master. This is not a doctrine of the afterlife. It is a man in pain saying the grave looks quieter than his ash heap.

The third stanza turns to God, though Job never speaks to him directly in this chapter. Why is light given to him who is in misery? And verse twenty-three: why is light given to a man whose way is hidden, whom God has hedged in? Do you hear the echo? In chapter one Satan complained that God had put a hedge around Job, to protect him. Now Job feels the same God has hedged him in, to trap him. Matthew Poole says the first hedge was for defense and this one feels like a pen for cattle. Job cannot see from inside which kind of hedge it is. We can, because we read chapter one.

So did Job sin in this chapter? Calvin, preaching on it in Geneva, says there is a mixture in him. His purpose is still to obey God, but like a soldier in a fight he takes blows and staggers, and words escape him that cannot be justified. Poole agrees: Job makes the devil a liar, yet he casts sideways reflections on God's providence. I think that is the right balance. Job did not blaspheme. He also was not a model. And God, in chapter forty-two, will still call him my servant.

You may also hear Jeremiah in this chapter. Jeremiah twenty: Cursed be the day on which I was born. Two godly men wished they had never been born, and Scripture does not hide it. Poole points out that Job never laid a hand on his own life. He waited. If you are ever in that kind of darkness, do what Job did. Say it out loud, and say it to someone. Call your pastor. Do not sit on the ash heap alone.

Derek Thomas, preaching this chapter, asked whether there is any light in it at all, and answered, "Only this: that Job is being utterly honest." Christopher Ash finds hope in Job's restlessness. A man who keeps asking why has not yet stopped believing that God has an answer. Chapter three is not the end of the book. It is where the honest struggle begins. If your prayers right now sound more like Job three than Psalm twenty-three, keep praying. God would rather hear your honest groan than your polished silence.

John chapter eight, verses one through eleven.

Yesterday I promised we would take the text question seriously, so let us start there. Your Bible puts this story in brackets with a note that the earliest manuscripts do not include it. That is true. The oldest papyri of John and the great fourth-century manuscripts do not have it. It first appears in a Greek manuscript in the fifth century, and later copies put it in different places, one family even in Luke, after chapter twenty-one, where it fits naturally. This is also the only place in John that mentions the Mount of Olives.

So what do we do with it? Our Confession, in chapter one, says the Scriptures were immediately inspired by God and, by his singular care and providence, kept pure in all ages. That does not mean every copy is perfect. It means the true text is preserved in the whole body of manuscripts. Calvin knew all of this in 1553. He admitted it was unknown to the ancient Greek churches, but since it "contains nothing unworthy of an Apostolic Spirit, there is no reason why we should refuse to apply it to our advantage." Most careful evangelical scholars today would say that it was not originally part of John, but that the event really happened. D.A. Carson says there is little reason to doubt it happened. I teach it that way. It sounds like Jesus, and nothing in it is out of step with the rest of Scripture.

Now the story. Early in the morning Jesus is teaching in the temple. The scribes and Pharisees bring a woman and stand her in the middle of the crowd. Teacher, this woman has been caught in the act of adultery. Now in the Law, Moses commanded us to stone such women. So what do you say? They said this to test him.

Where is the man? The law says both shall die. As a Ligonier devotional points out, charging only the woman shows these men did not care about the law. She is not a defendant. She is bait.

What was the trap? Say let her go, and he sets aside Moses. Say stone her, and he loses his name as the friend of sinners and invites trouble with Rome. John MacArthur calls it the ultimate theological dilemma: how does God fit his justice together with his mercy?

Jesus bends down and writes on the ground with his finger. What did he write? Nobody knows. Augustine thought of the finger of God that wrote the law on stone, and now writes on soil, looking for fruit. Ambrose and Jerome thought of Jeremiah seventeen: those who turn away from you shall be written in the earth. Calvin rejected all the guesses and thought it was a deliberate refusal to answer them. I lean toward Jeremiah, because that verse goes on to say they have forsaken the fountain of living water, and this is the morning after Booths. But hold it loosely.

They keep pressing. He stands up. Let him who is without sin among you be the first to throw a stone at her. That comes straight from Deuteronomy seventeen, verse seven: the hand of the witnesses shall be first against him. Jesus does not set aside the law. He applies it. R.C. Sproul puts it sharply: Jesus sides with Moses, then appoints the executioners and finds them lacking. Calvin is clear that this does not forbid courts to punish crime. It is a word to these accusers.

They go away one by one, beginning with the older ones. But notice where they go. Away. Calvin warns that this kind of conviction is not repentance. True repentance does not run from the Judge. It goes straight to him and asks for mercy. They felt guilt and left. She stayed.

Augustine has the most famous sentence on this passage. "The two were left alone, the wretched woman and Mercy."

Woman, where are they? Has no one condemned you? No one, Lord. Neither do I condemn you; go, and from now on sin no more.

Is that forgiveness, or only release? Calvin notes that Jesus was not acting as a civil judge here. But Matthew Henry hears more, and I think he is right. "For Christ to say, I do not condemn thee is, in effect, to say, I do forgive thee". Augustine says he condemned the sin, not the person. Grace first, then the call to a new life.

And how could the only sinless man in that courtyard say neither do I condemn you, when he was the one qualified to throw the stone? MacArthur answers that he knew he would die on a cross for her adultery. He did not throw the stone, because he was going to take her judgment on himself.

Second Peter chapter two, verses one through nine.

Chapter one ended with true prophets carried along by the Spirit. Chapter two turns the coin over. But false prophets also arose among the people, just as there will be false teachers among you. Israel had Jeremiah and Ezekiel, and it also had Hananiah. The church will have the same mix.

Verses one through three describe these teachers, and the words are vivid. They will secretly bring in destructive heresies. The verb pictures smuggling. Calvin pictures them tunneling in underground. Many will follow their sensuality, shameless indulgence that does not bother to hide. And in their greed they will exploit you with false words. False is plastos, from the verb for shaping clay. Think plastic. Exploit is emporeuomai, to trade, to make merchandise of someone. Think emporium. Hold that word next to verse one, where Peter says they deny the Master who bought them. The Master bought them, and now they put his people up for sale.

Then verse three ends with a sentence for every scoffer: their condemnation from long ago is not idle, and their destruction is not asleep. The judge is awake.

Now the hard question in this passage. Denying the Master who bought them. If Christ bought them, and they perish, did Christ die for people who are lost?

Reformed interpreters answer in more than one way. John Gill, the Baptist, argues that the Master here is God the Father, and that bought echoes Moses in Deuteronomy thirty-two: is not he your Father, who bought you? The difficulty is that Jude, writing about the same men, calls the Master our only Master and Lord, Jesus Christ.

So the answer most Reformed teachers give today is that Peter describes these men as they presented themselves and as the church received them. Matthew Poole put it this way three centuries ago. It is said "in the style of the visible church, which should judge them to be so till they declared the contrary by their wicked actions". Tom Schreiner, a Baptist New Testament scholar, says the redemption here describes how things appeared, not what God secretly did. And John Owen, in The Death of Death, notes that Scripture constantly calls everyone in the fellowship of the church saints, elect and redeemed, though only true members are those things in fact.

I hold that last view. Our Confession, in chapter eight, says that to all those for whom Christ purchased redemption, he does certainly and effectually apply and communicate the same. Christ's death does not fail. And a Puritan named Thomas Adams, who wrote a huge commentary on this letter in 1633, lays down this ground before he says anything else: no soul Christ has truly bought can perish. These men talked about their Redeemer, and Peter speaks of them the way they spoke of themselves.

Verses four through nine are one long sentence in Greek, an if with four parts and a then. If God did not spare angels when they sinned, but cast them into hell, and the word is Tartarus, the deepest prison. If he did not spare the ancient world, but preserved Noah, a herald of righteousness, with seven others. If he turned Sodom and Gomorrah to ashes. And if he rescued righteous Lot. Then, verse nine, the Lord knows how to rescue the godly from trials, and to keep the unrighteous under punishment until the day of judgment.

Jude uses some of the same examples, but only for judgment. Peter adds two rescued men, Noah and Lot, so his conclusion has two halves.

Noah is called a herald, keryx, the man who announces the king's decree. Genesis records no sermon, but Clement of Rome, writing to Corinth around the year 96, already says it: "Noah preached repentance, and as many as listened to him were saved."

And then Lot. Righteous Lot? He offered his daughters to a mob and lingered when the angels told him to run. How is he righteous? Gill answers with justification: Lot was righteous by the righteousness of Christ counted to him. But notice the evidence Peter gives. Verse eight: that righteous man, living among them day after day, was tormenting his righteous soul over their lawless deeds that he saw and heard. The verb is in the active voice and the ongoing tense. Lot kept grieving. He never got used to it. Henry says that being used to their wickedness never reconciled him to it. That is what made him different from Sodom.

And verse nine. The Lord knows how. You may not know how you will get through what is pressing on you. He does. Thomas Adams turns that into a prayer: for the things God has revealed, give me a sober knowledge, and for the things he has hidden, a contented ignorance.

Two connections today, and the first one is printed in the margin of your own Bible. Next to Second Peter two, verse eight, where Lot torments his righteous soul over what he sees and hears, the cross-references point back to Ezekiel nine, verse four. Henry made the link too.

So put the three scenes side by side. In Jerusalem, a few people sigh and groan over the abominations of their city, and God marks them. In Sodom, a flawed man named Lot grieves day after day over what he sees, and God rescues him. And in the temple courts, a group of men catch a woman in sin, and they do not grieve at all. They have found a trap to set. Same sin, three responses: grief, grief, and glee. And the only one in that courtyard who had the right to throw the stone was the one who bent down and refused to.

The people who sigh over sin are rarely the ones who enjoy exposing it. They usually know their own hearts best. Calvin says, on Lot, that if the godly grieve to see the world rushing into evil, they must groan all the more over their own sins.

The second connection is time. In Ezekiel twelve the proverb in Jerusalem was, the days grow long, and every vision comes to nothing. In Second Peter two, verse three, Peter answers that proverb before his scoffers can even say it: their condemnation from long ago is not idle, and their destruction is not asleep. God's patience is not God's absence. Zedekiah learned that at Riblah. His waiting is mercy, not a no.

Here is your question for the day. When you see sin today, in the news, in your church, in your own home, in your own mirror, whose face will you wear, the mourner's, or the accuser's?
