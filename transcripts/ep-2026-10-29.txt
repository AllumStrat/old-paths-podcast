The heavens were opened.

Not over the temple in Jerusalem. Not on Mount Sinai. Over an irrigation ditch in southern Babylonia, in July, among refugees.

A thirty-year-old priest who has never offered a single sacrifice, because the only altar where he could offer one is hundreds of miles away, in a city he may never see again, is standing by a canal with the other deportees. And a storm comes out of the north. Fire. Wheels. Wings. Eyes. A throne. And on the throne, something that looks like a man.

Here is my question for you this morning. Where do you expect to meet God? And what would it do to you if he showed up in the one place you were sure he had left?

This is Old Paths. Today is the first day of the eleventh month in the reading plan, day two hundred fifty-one of three hundred. The readings are Ezekiel chapters one through three, Job chapter one, John chapter seven, verses thirty-seven through forty-four, and Second Peter chapter one, verses one through eleven.

Yesterday three books ended. Today three begin: Ezekiel, Job, and Second Peter. Only John keeps walking, still at the Feast of Booths.

Last time I left you with the last line John Calvin spoke in his lectures on Lamentations. I will begin tomorrow the explanation of Ezekiel. And he did. Those Ezekiel lectures were the last lectures of Calvin's life. He lectured on Ezekiel through his final illness, got as far as chapter twenty, and died in May of 1564. So when you hear Calvin this morning, you are hearing a dying man on a prophet sent to people who would not listen.

I also promised you two connections, and they are both waiting. Job lived in the land of Uz, the same land we met in Lamentations four, verse twenty-one, where Edom dwelt. And the Satan in Job one comes from going to and fro on the earth, the verse Peter had in mind when he wrote about the lion who prowls around.

But we start by the river Chebar.

Ezekiel chapters one through three.

Ezekiel dates his book more carefully than any other prophet, so let me set the scene. Verse two: it was the fifth year of the exile of King Jehoiachin. Jehoiachin surrendered Jerusalem to Nebuchadnezzar in 597 B.C., and Second Kings twenty-four says ten thousand captives went with him to Babylon, the princes, the warriors, the craftsmen and smiths. Ezekiel, a young priest, son of Buzi, was among them. Five years later puts us in the fourth month, the summer of 593 B.C.

Zedekiah is on the throne in Jerusalem. And think back to Jeremiah. This is the season of Jeremiah twenty-seven and twenty-eight, the ox yoke and Hananiah promising the exiles home in two years. It is the season of Jeremiah's letter to the exiles in chapter twenty-nine. Build houses. Plant gardens. And it is the year, or very nearly, of Jeremiah fifty-one, verse fifty-nine, when Seraiah, Baruch's brother, read a scroll in Babylon, tied a stone to it, and sank it in the Euphrates. We watched that scroll go under in episode eight. Now, in the same land and at almost the same moment, God hands a different scroll to a different man and tells him to eat it.

Why date by Jehoiachin and not Zedekiah? Remember the Babylonian ration tablets from the end of Jeremiah. They are dated around 592 B.C., the year after this vision, and they still call Jehoiachin the king of Judah. The exiles counted time by their captive king.

And the Chebar canal. Older commentators thought it was the Khabur River far to the north. But the text says the land of the Chaldeans, and business records from Nippur, in the south, mention a navigable canal called the naru Kabaru. Ezekiel's people lived along one of the great waterways of the plain, and tablets from a nearby town the Babylonians called Al-Yahudu, Judah-town, show Judean exiles leasing land there for generations. They did what Jeremiah told them to do.

Now verse one. In the thirtieth year. Thirtieth year of what? Gill counted from the rise of Nebuchadnezzar's father. Calvin was sure it was reckoned from a Jubilee. Keil said honestly that it can no longer be known. I think the simplest answer is best. Ezekiel is thirty. In Numbers four, verse three, thirty is the age a priest began his service. This is the birthday he had waited for his whole life. And instead of a temple, he gets a canal. Instead of an altar, he gets the heavens opened.

Calvin noticed something else about the timing. Jeremiah had been preaching for thirty-five years, Calvin says, crying aloud to the deaf. So God gave him a partner in Babylon. "For it is not natural that one Prophet at Jerusalem, and another in Chaldea, should utter their prophecies, as it were, in the same key, just as two singers unite their voices in accordance with each other. For no melody can be devised more perfectly complete than that which appears between these two servants of God."

Here is the shape of the whole book, because it will carry us for weeks. In chapters one through three, the glory of the Lord appears in Babylon. In chapters eight through eleven, Ezekiel watches that glory leave the temple in Jerusalem and go east. In chapter thirty-three, a refugee arrives with news that the city has fallen. And in chapter forty-three, the glory comes home into a new temple. The last words of the book are the name of the new city. The Lord is there.

So, the vision. Verse four. A stormy wind came out of the north. North is the direction armies came from; Babylon came down on Judah from the north. But this storm is not Babylon. It is God.

Out of it come four living creatures, each with four faces, a man, a lion, an ox, and an eagle. In chapter ten Ezekiel will tell us plainly, I knew that they were cherubim. And the phrase that keeps coming back: each went straight forward, without turning as they went. They never have to turn, because whichever way they go, they are already facing it.

Then the wheels. A wheel within a wheel, so it can roll in any direction, and verse eighteen, their rims were tall and awesome, and full of eyes all around. The spirit of the living creatures was in the wheels.

What did that say to exiles who believed God lived in Jerusalem? Calvin says they treated the temple as if it were God's prison. This vision says the throne of God has wheels. He can come to a canal in Babylon. He can leave a temple full of idols. And the wheels full of eyes have always been read as providence. Matthew Henry: "Look every way upon the wheel of providence, it has a face toward you." And a moment later, "The disposals of Providence seem to us dark, perplexed, and unaccountable, yet are all wisely ordered for the best."

Spurgeon preached on these wheels as a young man, and he noticed the one part of a wheel that never turns, the axle. The rim goes up and down, and so does your life. But the axle, he said, is God's everlasting love for his people. The rim of your week may be at the bottom right now. The axle has not moved.

Now look at the top of the vision. Above the creatures, an expanse like awe-inspiring crystal. Above that, the likeness of a throne. And seated above the likeness of a throne was a likeness with a human appearance.

Listen to how carefully he speaks. He never says, I saw a throne. He says, the likeness of a throne. He never says, I saw God. And then verse twenty-eight: such was the appearance of the likeness of the glory of the Lord. Three layers of padding between the prophet and the thing itself. The Hebrew words are demut, likeness, and mar'eh, appearance, and he keeps stacking them. He even uses a word found only in this book, chashmal, gleaming metal. He is running out of vocabulary.

Calvin explains why. "We must hold, then, that as often as the Prophets and holy fathers saw God, they saw as it were the likeness or aspect of the glory of God, but not the glory itself, for they were not fit for it; for this would be to measure with the palm of our hands a hundred thousand heavens, and earths, and worlds."

That brings the first hard question. Who is on the throne, in the likeness of a man? Calvin, Henry, and Gill agree. It is the eternal Son, appearing in the form of a man though not yet made man. I think they are right, and this is our first trip into the New Testament, because John says Isaiah saw the glory of Christ when he saw the Lord on his throne. Ezekiel does not know the name Jesus. But the throne of the universe has someone on it who looks like a man, and that is good news for men.

Our second New Testament move is a direct borrowing. In Revelation four, John sees the throne, a rainbow around it, and four living creatures, like a lion, an ox, a man, and an eagle. The fathers went further. Irenaeus, around the year 180, argued there must be exactly four Gospels, and one of his reasons was Ezekiel's creatures. "For the living creatures are quadriform, and the Gospel is quadriform, as is also the course followed by the Lord." Quadriform means four-shaped. That is where the old symbols carved on pulpits come from, the eagle for John and the rest. It is lovely church history. But it is not what the exiles heard. They heard that God's throne can move.

One surprising disagreement. Verse twenty-eight compares the brightness to the bow in the cloud. Henry hears Noah's rainbow and covenant mercy around the throne. Calvin refuses, because, he says, the whole of this vision was formidable. I lean toward Calvin. Ezekiel does not smile at the rainbow. He falls on his face.

Then the voice. Chapter two, verse one. Son of man, stand on your feet. Son of man, ben adam, is what God calls Ezekiel more than ninety times. Derek Thomas, the Presbyterian pastor, says the title stresses his humanity and lowliness. He has just seen a man on the throne of glory, and he is reminded he is a man of dust. He cannot even get up by himself. The Spirit entered into me and set me on my feet.

The commission is brutal. A nation of rebels, impudent and stubborn. Whether they hear or refuse to hear, and that phrase repeats like a drumbeat, they will know that a prophet has been among them. Be not afraid, though briers and thorns are with you and you sit on scorpions. God does not promise him results. Success is faithfulness.

Then the scroll, written on the front and on the back. Scrolls were written on one side. This one is full, with words of lamentation and mourning and woe. Son of man, eat this scroll. And verse three: it was in my mouth as sweet as honey.

A scroll of woe tastes like honey? Henry says gracious souls can receive with delight truths that speak terror to the wicked. The word of God is sweet to those who love God, even when it is hard. And the eating is the point. Ezekiel cannot carry this message like a mail carrier. It has to go into him first. Spurgeon preached on this chapter in 1878 and said, "It is poor work to preach a Christ you never knew. It is terrible to talk of bread you have never tasted, of living water you never drank, and of joys you never felt." Keep that phrase, living water. We will need it in John.

Then verse fourteen. I went in bitterness in the heat of my spirit, the hand of the Lord being strong upon me. Sweet in the mouth, bitter in the spirit. Matthew Poole is hard on Ezekiel and calls him Jonah-like, angry. Calvin is gentler: Ezekiel was wholly consecrated, but he kept human feelings, and they were not sinful. I side with Calvin. A man untroubled by a message of woe for his own people has no business carrying it.

There is a wordplay here you miss in English. Ezekiel's name, Yechezqel, means God strengthens, or God makes hard. Verse seven: all the house of Israel have a hard forehead and a stubborn heart. Same root. Then God says, I have made your face as hard as their faces. Like emery harder than flint have I made your forehead. Their foreheads are hard against God. God will make his harder. His name is the promise.

Verse fifteen. I came to the exiles at Tel-abib, and I sat there overwhelmed among them seven days. The King James says, I sat where they sat. Seven days is the time of mourning; Job's friends will sit with him seven days. Before Ezekiel says a word to these people, he sits in their dust for a week.

Then the second hard question, the watchman. Son of man, I have made you a watchman for the house of Israel. Four cases follow. Fail to warn the wicked, and his blood I will require at your hand. Warn him and he refuses, and you have delivered your soul. And then verse twenty. If a righteous person turns from his righteousness, and I lay a stumbling block before him, he shall die.

Can a truly righteous person fall away and be lost? As a Presbyterian I hold, with the Westminster Confession, chapter seventeen, that those God has effectually called can neither totally nor finally fall away. So what about this verse? John Gill, the Baptist, answers head on. "This is to be understood not of one that is truly righteous, or is justified by the righteousness of Christ; for such can never turn from that righteousness, or be in an unjustified state". Calvin agrees: this righteousness has the outward appearance but not the root. But Calvin adds something we should not skip. This passage, he says, should stir us to ask God continually for a spirit of perseverance, because we run off in every direction like water unless God strengthens us. The warning is one of the means God uses to keep his people. And the stumbling block, Calvin says, is judgment.

Then the strangest part. Verse twenty-six. I will make your tongue cling to the roof of your mouth. Ezekiel will speak only when God opens his mouth to give an oracle, and that silence lasts until chapter thirty-three, about seven years later, when news comes that Jerusalem has fallen. Calvin says it is a sign of God's vengeance when the prophets go quiet. When God stops correcting you, that is not peace.

So here is what I want you to take from Ezekiel one through three. Before Ezekiel speaks one word to his people, he does two things. He eats the scroll, and he sits where they sit. If there is someone you need to warn this week, a friend, a son, a brother in your church, ask yourself first: have I eaten this word myself? Have I sat where he sits? Then speak, whether he hears or refuses to hear.

Job chapter one.

Job opens like a story. There was a man in the land of Uz whose name was Job. Where is Uz? Lamentations four, verse twenty-one, which we read yesterday, says, O daughter of Edom, you who dwell in the land of Uz. Job is very likely not an Israelite. There is no temple and no Levite. Job offers his own sacrifices for his family, like Abraham, and after his trial he lives another hundred and forty years. Most conservative scholars place him in the age of the patriarchs.

And was he a real man? Here is a gift from today's other reading. In Ezekiel fourteen, God names three righteous men, Noah, Daniel, and Job. Ezekiel treats Job as just as real as Noah. So does James, who says, you have heard of the steadfastness of Job.

Who wrote it? We don't know. Gregory the Great, the pope who wrote an enormous commentary on Job in the late 500s, said it is superfluous to ask, since the Holy Spirit is the author. To fuss about the human writer, he says, is like reading a letter and asking about the pen.

The book is a prose story at each end with almost forty chapters of poetry in between: Job and his friends arguing, then Elihu, then God out of the whirlwind.

Now the story. Verse six. The sons of God, the angels, present themselves before the Lord, and Satan came among them. In Hebrew it is ha-satan, with the article. The satan, the adversary, the accuser. The Lord asks, from where have you come? And he answers, from going to and fro on the earth. That is the line Peter had in mind. Your adversary prowls around.

And notice who raises Job's name. Not Satan. God. Have you considered my servant Job? Then the accusation, in verse nine. Does Job fear God for no reason? The Hebrew is hinnam, for nothing, gratis. The word comes from the root for grace. Satan's charge is that nobody loves God for free. Take away the hedge, and he will curse you to your face.

One small detail. In verses five and eleven the Hebrew actually says bless God and means curse; the writer will not put curse God on the page. The chapter ends with Job using the word for real.

Then four messengers, each arriving while the last is still talking. Sabeans, then fire from heaven, then Chaldeans, then a great wind that collapses the house on all ten of his children. Raiders, sky, raiders, sky. Each one ends, I alone have escaped to tell you.

And then verse twenty. Job arose and tore his robe and shaved his head and fell on the ground and worshiped. He grieves, and he worships, in the same moment, and the text does not set them against each other. Then verse twenty-one. Naked I came from my mother's womb, and naked shall I return. The Lord gave, and the Lord has taken away; blessed be the name of the Lord.

Here is the hard question. Satan did this. Why does Job say the Lord took it? And why does verse twenty-two say that in saying it, Job did not sin?

Gregory the Great saw it. "The holy man, under trial from the adversary, had lost every thing, yet knowing that Satan had no power against him to tempt him, saving by the Lord's permission, he does not say, "the Lord hath given, the devil hath taken away," but the Lord gave, the Lord hath taken away. For perchance it would have been a thing to grieve for, if what his Creator had given him, his enemy had taken from him: but when no other hath taken it away, saving He Who Himself gave it, He hath only recalled what was His own, and hath not taken away what was ours."

And the Puritan John Trapp puts it in one image. He says Job beheld God in all. Not a word against the Sabean plunderers, not a word against the devil. "Job easily discerned God's arrows in Satan's hand". The devil drew the bow, but God set the limit. Only against him do not stretch out your hand.

The reader sees the heavenly court. Job never does. Not in chapter one, and not in chapter forty-two. R.C. Sproul pointed out where that leaves us. "Rather, what God does in the mystery of the iniquity of such profound suffering, is that He answers Job with Himself."

So here is wisdom for today. Satan's question is a question about you. Do you fear God for nothing? If the hedge came down this week, would you still bless his name? Do not wait for the messengers to find out. Practice it now: thank him today for who he is, not only for what he has given.

John chapter seven, verses thirty-seven through forty-four.

We are still at the Feast of Booths, a week of living in shelters to remember the wilderness. Verse thirty-seven. On the last day of the feast, the great day. Seventh day or eighth is argued, and Ellicott calls the question antiquarian. What matters is the ceremony. Every morning of the feast, a priest filled a golden pitcher at the pool of Siloam, carried it up through the Water Gate with trumpets, and poured it out at the altar. The Mishnah says, in Gill's rendering, that whoever never saw the rejoicing of the drawing of water never saw any rejoicing in his life. Gill also quotes a rabbi who explained the name: from there, they draw the Holy Spirit.

So picture it. The water has been poured out, the celebration is ending, the pilgrims are about to go home. And Jesus stood up and cried out, If anyone thirsts, let him come to me and drink. Whoever believes in me, as the Scripture has said, Out of his heart will flow rivers of living water.

Which Scripture? There is no verse in the Old Testament that says exactly this. Calvin says Jesus is not quoting one passage but summing up the ordinary teaching of the prophets, who always pictured the Spirit as water. Isaiah fifty-five: come, everyone who thirsts. Isaiah forty-four: I will pour water on the thirsty land, and my Spirit on your offspring. But Matthew Henry and the Pulpit Commentary both point to a passage we just started this morning. In Ezekiel forty-seven, a river flows out from under the threshold of the new temple, the temple the glory returns to, and wherever it goes, everything lives. Jesus has already said in John two that his body is the temple. Now the temple is standing in the courts, saying, the river comes from me.

Whose heart? The Greek word is koilia, belly, the innermost part. Some read it as Christ's heart, the source. Others, like Augustine and the ESV, read it as the believer's heart, so that the man who drinks becomes a spring for others. I think both are true in order. The river starts in Christ, and it flows through those who drink. Ligonier put it well: we are not sources of living water ourselves, but Christ works through us to bless others.

And what does it take to drink? Spurgeon preached this text in 1885 and he made it as simple as it can be made. "Then you are told to drink. That is not a difficult action. Any fool can drink!" And a little later, "To drink is the act of a baby, a sick man, a wounded deer, or even a little chick." You bring nothing but your thirst.

Then John explains, verse thirty-nine. This he said about the Spirit, whom those who believed in him were to receive, for as yet the Spirit had not been given, because Jesus was not yet glorified. Literally, the Spirit was not yet. Augustine is careful here. The Spirit existed and worked in the prophets, in Simeon and Anna. What was not yet was the Spirit given in this new way, poured out on all who believe. And why wait? Chrysostom answers. "Calling the Cross, glory. For since we were enemies, and had sinned, and fallen short of the gift of God, and were haters of God, and since grace was a proof of our reconciliation, and since a gift is not given to those who are hated, but to friends and those who have been well-pleasing; it was therefore necessary that the Sacrifice should first be offered for us". The river could not flow until the rock was struck.

And then the crowd divides. This really is the Prophet, the one like Moses. This is the Christ. And then the objection. Is the Christ to come from Galilee? Has not the Scripture said that the Christ comes from the offspring of David, and comes from Bethlehem? Last time the crowd said nobody would know where the Messiah comes from. Now they say everybody knows he comes from Bethlehem, and they are right. That is the irony. John's readers know exactly where Jesus was born. The crowd uses a true Scripture as a reason not to ask a single question. Calvin says men often build obstacles out of the very Scriptures that lead them by the hand to Christ.

Verse forty-four. Some wanted to arrest him, but no one laid hands on him. Next time the officers come back empty-handed.

Second Peter chapter one, verses one through eleven.

A new letter, and a short introduction. Simeon Peter, a servant and apostle of Jesus Christ. Simeon is the old Hebrew form of his name, used of him only once elsewhere, by James in Acts fifteen. In verse fourteen of this chapter Peter says the putting off of his body will be soon, as the Lord showed him. So this is a dying man's letter, written near the end of Nero's reign, likely in the middle sixties A.D., probably from Rome. In chapter three he calls it the second letter to the same readers, the churches of Turkey we just finished with. And chapter two tells us why he wrote. False teachers are coming, who will turn grace into license and mock the Lord's return.

You should know that the early church was slower to receive this letter than First Peter. Eusebius, the church historian, lists it among the disputed books that were nevertheless recognized by many, and Jerome said its style was different. Calvin felt that too. But Calvin said that if the letter is genuine Scripture, Peter must be behind it, because writing in another man's name would be a fiction unworthy of a minister of Christ. Perhaps a disciple wrote it at Peter's command. But Calvin said he saw the majesty of the Spirit of Christ in every part of it and dreaded rejecting it. So do I.

Verse one. To those who have obtained a faith of equal standing with ours. The word is isotimos, equally precious. Peter walked with Jesus. His readers never saw him. And their faith is just as precious as his, because it grasps the same Christ. Spurgeon said that the weakest believer who ever crept into heaven on hands and knees had a like precious faith with the mighty apostle Peter. And notice the next phrase, by the righteousness of our God and Savior Jesus Christ. In Greek one article governs both titles. Jesus is our God and our Savior.

Verse four. Through his precious and very great promises, you may become partakers of the divine nature.

That is a bold phrase, and the fathers loved it. Athanasius, the great defender of Christ's deity, wrote in On the Incarnation, "For He was made man that we might be made God". What did he mean? Not that we become God. Calvin explains that nature here does not mean essence but quality. We are not swallowed into God. We are remade in his image, holy and righteous. The Westminster Confession, chapter twenty-six, says plainly that our communion with Christ does not make us partakers of the substance of his Godhead. Spurgeon said there is always a gulf between creature and Creator in essence. And yet through union with Christ we share his life. Notice the means, too. Through the promises. You partake of God by believing what he has promised.

Then verses five through seven. For this very reason, make every effort to supplement your faith with virtue. The word supplement is epichoregeo. A choregos was the rich citizen who paid for the chorus at a Greek festival, and the verb came to mean supplying generously. Faith, virtue, knowledge, self-control, steadfastness, godliness, brotherly affection, love. It starts with faith and ends with love. And watch verse eleven, where the same verb comes back. There will be richly provided for you an entrance into the eternal kingdom. You supply. God supplies far more richly.

Then verse ten, the famous one. Be all the more diligent to confirm your calling and election. Can you make your election more certain? Not to God. Spurgeon said, they are sure to him, make them sure to yourself. And he pointed out that Peter puts calling before election because calling is what you can know first. You cannot read the book of life. But you can see whether you have been called out of darkness.

The Puritan Thomas Watson said, "Sanctification is the seed, assurance is the flower which grows out of it: assurance is a consequent of sanctification." And John Piper, preaching on this text, put it in one line. "The confirmation of your election is your progress in sanctification." This is not salvation by works. Verse three comes first. His divine power has granted all things. Your effort grows out of his gift. But a man who says he is elect and does not care to grow has forgotten, verse nine says, that he was cleansed from his former sins.

So, two connections, and both are real.

The first is a river. This morning the heavens opened over a canal in Babylon. The glory came to a river in exile. In Ezekiel forty-seven, which we will reach in a few weeks, a river flows from the temple the glory returns to, and wherever it goes, everything lives. And in John seven, at the feast of water, Jesus stands in the temple courts and says, out of me, rivers. Ezekiel saw the glory by a river. In Jesus the glory is the river.

The second is what God gives you to take in. Ezekiel is told, eat this scroll. Jesus says, come to me and drink. Peter says you partake of the divine nature through the promises. In each case God puts his own word, his own Spirit, his own life inside a man before he sends him out. The prophet ate before he spoke. The thirsty drink before they flow. The believer believes the promises before he adds virtue to faith.

So here is your question to carry through the day. What has God given you to take in today, before you try to give anything out?
