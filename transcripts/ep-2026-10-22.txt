A man stands on the bank of the Euphrates. He is a royal official from Jerusalem, a long way from home, and Babylon rises behind him, walls so broad that chariots can pass on top of them. He has a scroll in his hands. He reads it quietly, to no audience at all. Then he ties a stone to it, throws it into the middle of the river, and watches it go under. And he says, Thus shall Babylon sink, to rise no more.

As far as we know, nobody saw him do it. Nobody in Babylon would have cared.

What do you do with the word of God when God alone is watching?

This is Old Paths. Today is day twenty of the tenth month in the reading plan, day two hundred forty-five of three hundred. The readings are Jeremiah fifty-one, the Song of Songs chapter four, verses eight through sixteen, John chapter six, verses twenty-five through forty, and First Peter chapter three, verses eight through twelve.

Last time Jeremiah wrote Babylon's funeral song while Babylon was still the terror of the world. We watched the cup of wrath, the one God passed around the table of the nations back in chapter twenty-five, finally reach the last seat. Today it is drained. Chapter fifty-one finishes the longest oracle in the book, and then it closes with the scene you just heard, the scroll and the stone. I promised you Seraiah, and here he is.

We have also been following a little family of Hebrew words for quiet and rest, from the sword that could not rest, through Moab settled on his dregs, to the rest God gives the earth when Babylon falls. Today that thread gets one last surprise, in a job title.

And one more thread. Back in chapter forty-eight, Moab had never been poured from vessel to vessel. Today Jerusalem says Nebuchadnezzar made her an empty vessel, and God answers by making Babylon's god give back what he swallowed.

In the Song of Songs, the groom calls her bride for the first time, and the garden is locked. In John, the day after the loaves, Jesus says, I am the bread of life. And in First Peter, the word finally gathers up everything Peter has said about submission and hands it to all of us.

Jeremiah fifty-one first. The date is given at the very end, verse fifty-nine. All of this went to Babylon in the fourth year of Zedekiah, 594 or 593 B.C. Jerusalem would stand seven more years. Babylon would stand fifty-five more.

Here is what makes that date striking. Chapters twenty-seven and twenty-eight happen in the same year. Envoys from Edom, Moab, Ammon, Tyre, and Sidon were sitting in Jerusalem, plotting revolt against Nebuchadnezzar. Jeremiah walked in wearing an ox yoke and told them all to submit. And a prophet named Hananiah stood up in the temple, broke the yoke off Jeremiah's neck, and announced that within two years God would break the yoke of Babylon.

So in one year, two prophets both said Babylon would fall. Hananiah said two years. Jeremiah said seventy, and told the exiles to build houses and pray for Babylon's welfare. Then Jeremiah quietly wrote this scroll, the most complete prophecy of Babylon's ruin in the Bible. False prophecy is not always a lie about what will happen. Sometimes it is a lie about when, and about what God requires of you in the meantime.

Verse one has a second code name. Last time we met Sheshach, the mirror-alphabet cipher for Babel. Here is another. Leb-kamai, which means the heart of those who rise against me. Run the same cipher and you get Kasdim, the Chaldeans. Even Babylon's name is turned into a verdict.

Verse five gives the reason God is doing this. For Israel and Judah have not been forsaken by their God. The Hebrew word is almost a picture. Not widowed. Calvin says the prophet is alluding to the marriage between God and his people. The exile was a real separation, a kind of divorce for a season, but not a widowhood. The husband is alive, and he is coming back for his wife.

Verse seven. Babylon was a golden cup in the Lord's hand, making all the earth drunken. Daniel saw Babylon as a head of gold. Jeremiah sees a golden cup. Beautiful, rich, splendid, and full of wrath. But notice whose hand holds it. The Lord's. Calvin says the passage teaches that even when the wicked rule with great display, God is governing all their violence, only not in a way anyone can see. You cannot see the hand from the outside. You can only hear it from the prophet.

Verses fifteen through nineteen are a hymn to the Creator. He made the earth by his power. Every goldsmith is put to shame by his idols. Not like these is he who is the portion of Jacob. Jeremiah wrote it once before, almost word for word, in chapter ten, to warn Judah not to fear the idols of the nations. Now he sings it again at the moment Babylon's idols seem to have won. Same hymn, new pressure. The truths you learned when things were calm are the ones you will need when they are not.

Verse twenty-five has a phrase worth slowing down for. Behold, I am against you, O destroying mountain. In Hebrew, har hammashchit. Ellicott points out that this is the very phrase Second Kings twenty-three uses for the hill outside Jerusalem where Solomon built shrines to Ashtoreth, Chemosh, and Milcom, the mount of corruption that Josiah tore down. Now Jeremiah pins it on Babylon. Babylon is the mount of corruption for the whole world. And the next verse says no one will take a stone from it for a corner. Which is fitting. Babylon was built of brick.

Now verse thirty-four. Listen to Jerusalem speak. Nebuchadnezzar the king of Babylon has devoured me; he has crushed me; he has made me an empty vessel; he has swallowed me like a monster; he has filled his stomach with my delicacies; he has rinsed me out. Remember Moab, the wine that had never been poured from vessel to vessel. Jerusalem has been poured, emptied, and rinsed. Calvin says the prophet puts this complaint on Jerusalem's lips so that the Jews might feel that their miseries were not overlooked by God.

And then verse forty-four answers it. I will punish Bel in Babylon, and take out of his mouth what he has swallowed. Babylon swallowed Jerusalem, and Bel, Babylon's god, swallowed the treasures. John Gill, the Baptist, saw exactly what that meant. When Nebuchadnezzar looted the temple, the gold and silver vessels went into the house of his god. And when Cyrus decreed the return, the first chapter of Ezra lists them going home, more than five thousand pieces. Gill says the return of those vessels "greatly fulfilled this prophecy; and was a refunding of what was lodged with him, or a vomiting what he had swallowed up." The god who devoured the world got sick to his stomach. That is Jeremiah's own picture.

One more scene before the hard question. Verse thirty-nine, and again in verse fifty-seven. While they are inflamed I will prepare them a feast and make them drunk, that they may become merry, then sleep a perpetual sleep and not wake. If you have read Daniel five, you know that is exactly how Babylon fell. Belshazzar threw a feast for a thousand lords, called for the gold and silver vessels from Jerusalem's temple, and drank from them. The same vessels. That night the hand wrote on the wall, and that night Belshazzar was killed. The Greek historian Herodotus independently says the city was taken during a festival, after Cyrus diverted the Euphrates and his men walked in along the riverbed. And verse thirty-six had already said it. I will dry up her sea and make her fountain dry.

Now the hard question. Go back to verse thirty-five. The violence done to me and to my kinsmen be upon Babylon, let the inhabitant of Zion say. My blood be upon the inhabitants of Chaldea, let Jerusalem say.

That is a prayer for vengeance. And God put it in Jerusalem's mouth. How does that fit with the letter Jeremiah sent to the exiles in chapter twenty-nine? Seek the welfare of the city where I have sent you into exile, and pray to the Lord on its behalf. Is Jerusalem supposed to pray for Babylon's peace or for Babylon's punishment?

Look at what God says next, in verse thirty-six. Behold, I will plead your cause and take vengeance for you. Notice who takes the vengeance. Not Zion. Zion is not handed a sword. Zion is handed a voice. Calvin says Jeremiah here exhorts the faithful to deposit their complaints in the bosom of God, and to appeal to him as their defender. And he draws the lesson plainly. "By this passage we are taught to present our complaints to God, if we wish him to undertake our cause."

So the two commands do not contradict each other. They divide the labor. In your daily life in Babylon, you seek its peace, you pay its taxes, you pray for its people. And the injustice Babylon has done to you, you do not settle yourself. You carry it up and lay it before the Judge. God does not ask his people to pretend they were not wronged. He asks them to file the case with him. He even gives them the words. Hold on to that, because Peter is going to say the same thing at the end of this episode.

Now verse forty-five. Go out of the midst of her, my people! Let every one save his life from the fierce anger of the Lord! And verse fifty, the line that I think is the pastoral heart of the chapter. You who have escaped from the sword, go, do not stand still! Remember the Lord from far away, and let Jerusalem come into your mind.

This was harder than it sounds. When Cyrus let the Jews go home, most of them stayed. Ezra counts the first returning company at a little over forty-two thousand. Babylon was comfortable. Calvin is blunt about it. When liberty to return was given, he says, only a small portion went. The rest were so used to their houses, so fixed in Babylon, that the temple and the promised land no longer counted for much.

Spurgeon preached on verse fifty in July of 1882, and he saw the same danger. Remembering Jerusalem, he said, "would keep them from settling down in the places to which they had been carried as captives." It was the Lord's purpose, he said, "that they might find no permanent satisfaction in Babylon, but ever sigh for the city of their solemnities."

Jerome, around A.D. 386, wrote a letter from Bethlehem in the names of his friends Paula and Eustochium, urging a Roman widow named Marcella to leave Rome and join them. After quoting Revelation's come out of her, my people, he wrote, "Turn back also to Jeremiah and pay heed to what he has written of like import: Flee out of the midst of Babylon, and deliver every man his soul." For Jerome, Rome was Babylon, and leaving meant buying a ticket to Palestine.

I think Jerome took the verse a little too literally, and Augustine, I think, read it better. Augustine never told his people to move. In his sermon on Psalm one hundred thirty-seven, the exiles' own song, by the waters of Babylon, he said the trouble is not where you live, but what you love. And then this. "If you weep in the remembrance of Sion, you ought to weep even when it is well with you in Babylon." In other words, the test is not whether you miss Jerusalem when Babylon hurts. The test is whether you miss it when Babylon is good to you.

That is exactly what Daniel did, by the way. Living in Babylon, serving its kings, he went home every day, opened his windows toward Jerusalem, and prayed. He let Jerusalem come into his mind three times a day.

Now the epilogue, verses fifty-nine to sixty-four, and our man by the river. The word that Jeremiah the prophet commanded Seraiah the son of Neriah, son of Mahseiah. That family line matters. In chapter thirty-two, Jeremiah's scribe Baruch is called the son of Neriah, son of Mahseiah. Same father, same grandfather. Seraiah is Baruch's brother. One brother wrote Jeremiah's words down in Jerusalem. The other carried them to Babylon. There is even a seal, inscribed for Seraiah son of Neriah, that the archaeologist Nahman Avigad identified as his. It came from the antiquities market rather than a dig, so hold it loosely. But it is a lovely possibility.

Then his job. Seraiah was the quartermaster. And here is our rest thread. The Hebrew is sar menuchah, literally the officer of the resting place. Menuchah is the word for rest in Numbers ten, when the ark went ahead of Israel to seek out a resting place for them. Keil and Delitzsch, and Ellicott, take it as an office, the man who arranged where the royal party would halt and sleep on the long road to Babylon. The older translators read it as a description of his character. The King James says, a quiet prince. Calvin, Matthew Henry, Matthew Poole, and Gill all took it that way. Poole calls him a man of moderate temper who persuaded others to peace. Henry adds a line every politician should hear. "Note, it is the real honour of great men to be quiet men, and it is the wisdom of princes to put such into places of trust."

I lean toward the ESV here. It is a job title. But do not miss the irony either way. The man in charge of the resting places carries the scroll that says Babylon will never rest again. And look how the scroll ends. Verse fifty-eight said the nations weary themselves only for fire. Verse sixty-four, the last words Seraiah speaks over the water, repeat it. And they shall become exhausted. The rest thread that began with a sword that could not be still ends with an empire that wore itself out.

Did Zedekiah himself make the trip? Calvin, Gill, and Poole doubt it, since Scripture records no such journey. Ellicott thinks Nebuchadnezzar summoned him to explain that conspiracy of envoys. Either way, Seraiah was on a diplomatic mission to calm Babylon down. And in his luggage was a scroll announcing Babylon's destruction.

Now notice how he was told to read it. When you come to Babylon, see that you read all these words, and say, O Lord, you have said concerning this place that you will cut it off. Calvin noticed that this is a prayer, addressed to God, not a sermon to the Babylonians. Seraiah read it alone. And Calvin made one of his most searching observations. Our courage, he says, is usually more visible in front of people than when we are alone. Shame keeps us brave in public. "There is hardly one in a hundred who is so bold as he ought to be when God alone is witness."

Put this scene next to one we watched a few episodes ago, in chapter thirty-six. King Jehoiakim sat by his fire, and as the scroll of Jeremiah was read to him, he cut it with a knife and burned it piece by piece. A king destroyed God's word in contempt. Here a servant drowns God's word in faith. Same prophet, two scrolls. One burned to deny the message. One sunk to seal it.

When we read Jeremiah fifty last time, I noted that John builds Revelation eighteen out of these chapters, including the angel who throws a great millstone into the sea. Seraiah's stone is that millstone's ancestor.

And then the last line of the chapter. Thus far are the words of Jeremiah. That is an editor speaking, probably Baruch. Chapter fifty-two, which we read next time, is a historical appendix, closely following Second Kings. The prophet's own last word in this book is not about Jerusalem. It is about Babylon sinking.

So bring it home. You live in Babylon. Not in the sense of a building or a city, but in the sense the exiles did. You live in a culture that is not your home, that gives you a lot, and that shapes what you love without asking permission. The danger named here is not mainly persecution. It is settling down.

So here is the concrete thing. Do what Seraiah did. Take the word of God and read it when God alone is witness. Not to impress anyone, not on a stage, but in a room where nobody knows. Say back to God what he has said. Lord, you have said this world is passing away. And then do what Daniel did, and let Jerusalem come into your mind on purpose, at fixed times, while things are going well. What has made Babylon most comfortable for you this year?

The Song of Songs, chapter four, verses eight through sixteen. Last time the groom praised his bride from head to toe and said, there is no flaw in you. Now verse eight brings a word we have not heard yet in the Song. Come with me from Lebanon, my bride. Kallah, bride. It appears six times between here and the first verse of chapter five, and nowhere else in the book. This is the wedding night.

Notice the geography. Lebanon, Amana, Senir, and Hermon are the high mountains of the far north, the dens of lions and the mountains of leopards. He is calling her away from the wild heights, away from danger, and the Hebrew is emphatic. With me. Come with me. Marriage begins with leaving.

Verse nine. You have captivated my heart, my sister, my bride; you have captivated my heart with one glance of your eyes. In Hebrew that is a single verb built from the word for heart. You have hearted me. You have taken my heart. One glance did it. And notice, my sister, my bride. The Cambridge commentators note that in ancient Egyptian love songs, lovers called each other my sister and my brother. It is not about family. It is about a love that feels like belonging, closer than kin.

Then verse twelve, the center of the poem. A garden locked is my sister, my bride, a spring locked, a fountain sealed. A garden in that world was walled, and a private spring had its mouth sealed. The point is exclusivity. This garden belongs to one man. Proverbs five uses the same picture: drink water from your own cistern, let your fountain be blessed, rejoice in the wife of your youth. The locked garden is chastity before marriage and faithfulness within it.

Verses thirteen and fourteen open the gate, and it is an orchard. The word is pardes, which gives us our word paradise. Pomegranates, nard, saffron, cinnamon, myrrh. No garden in Israel grew all of that. This is a lover saying, everything precious in the world is in you.

The church has always heard more here. Cyprian, the bishop of Carthage, writing around A.D. 256, applied verse twelve to the church as Christ's one bride. "But if His Church is a garden enclosed, and a fountain sealed, how can he who is not in the Church enter into the same garden, or drink from its fountain?" Cyprian used that argument to insist that anyone baptized by heretics had to be baptized again. The church later disagreed with his conclusion, and so do I. But his instinct about the image is sound. Christ has one bride, and she is his.

Then verse sixteen. Awake, O north wind, and come, O south wind! Blow upon my garden, let its spices flow. Who says that? Spurgeon read it as the bride's prayer. Richard Sibbes, the Puritan, began his famous sermons on the Song right here, and he said these are Christ's words, because he calls it my garden. The ESV agrees, giving the first half of the verse to him. I think Sibbes and the ESV are right. Listen to how it flows. He says, blow upon my garden. And she answers, Let my beloved come to his garden. He claims her, and she agrees with his claim. That is what consent sounds like in a marriage.

But both men saw the same thing in the winds. Sibbes put it this way. "The north is a nipping wind, and the south a cherishing wind; therefore the south wind is the welcomer and sweeter after the north wind hath blown." A cold wind and a warm wind, and the garden needs both before its fragrance carries. Spurgeon, preaching on this verse at a communion service in 1887, summed up the verse in a single line. "The prayer is, blow, and the result is, flow." And he added that in nature you cannot have the north wind and the south blowing at once, but in grace you can.

Here is the practical wisdom. In a marriage, and in your own soul, you do not get to choose only the warm winds. The hard seasons and the tender ones both draw out what is in you. So keep the garden locked for one, and when the north wind blows, do not assume love has failed. It may be releasing the spices.

John chapter six, verses twenty-five through forty. Last time the disciples rowed through the storm, and the crowd borrowed boats to chase Jesus across the lake. Now they find him in Capernaum, and verse fifty-nine tells us the conversation ends in the synagogue. They say, Rabbi, when did you come here? Jesus does not answer the question. He answers their motive.

Truly, truly, I say to you, you are seeking me, not because you saw signs, but because you ate your fill of the loaves. Augustine, preaching on this verse, looked around his own church and said, people come for help with lawsuits, for protection, for favors. Then this. "Jesus is scarcely sought after for Jesus' sake." Calvin says the same of the crowd. They were looking in Christ for something other than Christ himself.

Sit with that. They did seek him, hard, across a lake. But they wanted something else in him. You can do that for years. Seek him for peace of mind, for a better marriage, for good kids, for a sense of meaning. Those are his gifts. They are not him.

Verse twenty-seven. Do not work for the food that perishes, but for the food that endures to eternal life. So they ask, verse twenty-eight, What must we do, to be doing the works of God? Plural. Give us the list. And Jesus answers, verse twenty-nine, in the singular. This is the work of God, that you believe in him whom he has sent.

Here the voices disagree. Augustine noticed the phrase work of God, and said Jesus did not say this is your work, but this is the work of God, so that he who glories may glory in the Lord. Calvin thought that was reading too much into it. Jesus is telling them what God requires, Calvin says, not yet what God produces in them. And then Calvin gives faith a wonderful name. He calls it a passive work, a work no reward can ever be paid for. Faith is the empty hand. It does not earn the bread. It takes it.

Then the crowd asks for a sign. The day after five thousand men were fed from five loaves. Our fathers ate the manna in the wilderness. In other words, Moses fed a nation for forty years, and you fed us once. Jesus answers that it was not Moses who gave the bread, but my Father gives you the true bread from heaven. And when they say, Sir, give us this bread always, he says it. I am the bread of life; whoever comes to me shall not hunger, and whoever believes in me shall never thirst.

Last time on the water he said simply, I am. Now, for the first time in John, he adds a predicate. I am the bread. And notice what bread does. You do not admire bread. You eat it. Coming and believing are the same act seen from two sides.

Then verses thirty-seven to forty, some of the most comforting words in the Gospels. All that the Father gives me will come to me, and whoever comes to me I will never cast out. People have fought over those two halves for centuries. The first half is God's sovereign gift. The second is an open door. Spurgeon preached on this verse at Exeter Hall, and he refused to set them against each other. "I was once asked to reconcile these two statements, and I answered, 'No, I never reconcile friends.' These two passages never fell out, they are perfectly agreed." He also pointed out that the Greek has a double negative. I will not, not cast out. Never, never.

And verse thirty-nine closes a thread we opened two episodes ago. When the baskets were gathered, Jesus said, Gather up the leftover fragments, that nothing may be lost. Now he says what that was about. This is the will of him who sent me, that I should lose nothing of all that he has given me, but raise it up on the last day. Our Westminster Confession, in chapter seventeen, says those whom God has accepted in his Beloved can neither totally nor finally fall away from grace. My Baptist friends in the 1689 Confession say the same thing in nearly the same words. This is the text under both. Not one fragment lost.

First Peter chapter three, verses eight through twelve. Peter began a long section back at chapter two, verse thirteen. Be subject for the Lord's sake to every human institution. Then servants, then wives, then husbands. Now verse eight. Finally, all of you. He gathers everybody up.

Have unity of mind, sympathy, brotherly love, a tender heart, and a humble mind. Each of those is a single Greek word, five little portraits of a church that loves well. Then verse nine. Do not repay evil for evil or reviling for reviling, but on the contrary, bless, for to this you were called, that you may obtain a blessing. The verb is inherit. You were called to bless, so that you would inherit blessing.

Does refusing to hit back make you weak? Calvin answers directly. "Though it is commonly thought that it is an instance of a weak and abject mind, not to avenge injuries, yet it is counted before God as the highest magnanimity." Magnanimity means greatness of soul. The man who can bless his enemy is the bigger man, not the smaller one.

And John Gill, the Baptist, tells you why retaliation is so serious. Repaying evil for evil, he says, is "taking God's prerogative and work out of his hands." It is not just unkind. It is presumptuous. It is sitting in the Judge's chair.

Then Peter quotes Psalm thirty-four. Whoever desires to love life and see good days, let him keep his tongue from evil and his lips from speaking deceit; let him turn away from evil and do good; let him seek peace and pursue it. Peter has used this psalm before. In chapter two he said, you have tasted that the Lord is good, which is Psalm thirty-four, verse eight. And look at the psalm's title. David wrote it after he pretended to be insane in front of the Philistine king of Gath, a fugitive, an exile, scratching on the doors of the gate. Peter's exiles could not have chosen a better psalm.

Seek peace and pursue it. Calvin notices the second verb. It is not enough to accept peace when it is offered, he says. You have to follow it when it seems to run away from you. Some peace you have to chase.

And what are these good days? Augustine, preaching on the psalm, told his people their good days were not here. Calvin said it is a desirable thing to pass our days in peace in this world. I think Peter means both. He writes to exiles whose inheritance is kept in heaven, but he also believes that a man who guards his tongue usually has a better Tuesday.

Then verse twelve. For the eyes of the Lord are on the righteous, and his ears are open to their prayer. But the face of the Lord is against those who do evil. Calvin says that the reason we so quickly burn with the passion of revenge is that we do not consider that God cares for us. You are not unwatched. Your case is not unheard.

Two connections before we go, and both are really there.

First, go back to Jeremiah. My blood be upon the inhabitants of Chaldea, let Jerusalem say. And God answered, I will plead your cause. Now hear Peter. Do not repay evil for evil, but bless, for the eyes of the Lord are on the righteous, and his face is against those who do evil. That is the same faith in two different settings. The exiles in Babylon were allowed to tell God exactly how they had been wronged. They were not allowed to pick up the sword. Christians under Nero were told to bless their revilers, because God sees and God judges. In both cases, the wronged believer files the case with heaven and goes back to seeking the peace of the city. Gill's phrase fits both. Do not take God's prerogative out of his hands.

Second, look at the appetites. Babylon in Jeremiah is a mouth. Nebuchadnezzar swallowed Jerusalem like a monster and filled his stomach with her delicacies, and Bel gorged himself on the nations' treasure until God made him give it back. In John, the crowd wants bread for their stomachs. And Jesus says, I am the bread that comes down from heaven and gives life to the world. The world's great powers devour. The Son of God gives himself to be eaten. Babylon could not keep what it swallowed. Christ will not lose one thing the Father gave him.

So here is a question to carry through your day. Where are you living as though Babylon is home, and what would it look like today to let Jerusalem come into your mind?

Next time we read Jeremiah fifty-two, where the editor retells the fall of Jerusalem and ends with a captive king released from prison, the Song of Songs chapter five, with a knock at the door at night, John six, verses forty-one through fifty-nine, where Jesus says you must eat his flesh, and First Peter three, verses thirteen through twenty-two, with Noah and the ark.

This has been Old Paths. Grace and peace.
