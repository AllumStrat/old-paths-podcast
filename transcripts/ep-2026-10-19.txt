It is December in Jerusalem, and the king is cold. He sits in his winter house with a fire burning in a pot in front of him. A court official named Jehudi stands beside him with a scroll and reads it aloud. Three or four columns in, the king reaches out with a small knife, the kind a scribe uses to trim his pens, cuts off the part just read, and drops it into the fire. Jehudi keeps reading. The king keeps cutting. Column after column, until the whole scroll is ash.

The king tore the scroll. Nobody in the room tore his robe.

So here is today's question. When God tells you something you do not want to hear, which do you reach for, the knife or the robe?

This is Old Paths. Today is day fourteen of the tenth month in the reading plan, and the readings are Jeremiah thirty-five through thirty-eight, Ecclesiastes eleven, John chapter four, verses forty-three through fifty-four, and First Peter chapter one, verses ten through sixteen.

Last time we stood in Jeremiah thirty-five just long enough to meet the Rechabites, the family that would not drink the wine set in front of them. I said we would come back and stay longer. We will. Then we will follow Jeremiah through the burning of his scroll, an arrest at the city gate, a dungeon, and a muddy cistern. Carry one word with you through all four chapters.

The Hebrew verb is shama. It means to hear. In the Old Testament, hearing that never turns into doing is not really hearing, so the same verb also means to obey. Listen for it. The Rechabites obeyed. Jehoiakim would not listen. Zedekiah did not listen. And near the end of chapter thirty-eight, Jeremiah pleads with a frightened king, obey now the voice of the Lord. These four chapters are about who hears.

Ecclesiastes is in its last two chapters, and the Preacher is tying off his argument. John brings Jesus back to Cana for a second sign. And Peter tells us that the prophets themselves searched for the salvation we now hold, which turns out to bear on Jeremiah more than you might expect.

Start with where we are. Chapters thirty-two through thirty-four are set under King Zedekiah, with Babylon's army outside Jerusalem. Chapter thirty-four ended with that army pulling back for a while. Then chapters thirty-five and thirty-six jump back roughly ten years to Jehoiakim, and thirty-seven and thirty-eight jump forward again to Zedekiah.

Calvin's explanation is that the prophets' sermon summaries were later gathered into a book arranged by message more than by calendar. In thirty-five, a family keeps the word of an ancestor dead for centuries. In thirty-six, a king feeds the word of the living God to a fire. Then thirty-seven and thirty-eight give you a second king, Zedekiah, softer than his brother Jehoiakim and in the end no better at hearing.

So, back to the Rechabites. They descend from Jonadab son of Rechab, the man who rode in Jehu's chariot in Second Kings ten. He told his family to drink no wine, build no houses, sow no fields, and live in tents. When Nebuchadnezzar's raiders came, they moved inside the city walls but kept the rest of the rule. Jeremiah sets out wine for them in a temple room. They refuse.

Then God draws the lesson in verse fourteen. The command that Jonadab the son of Rechab gave to his sons, to drink no wine, has been kept. I have spoken to you persistently, but you have not listened to me. Persistently is a Hebrew idiom. Literally, rising early and speaking. The verb, shakam, is built on the word for shoulder. It pictures a man up before dawn, shouldering his load. That is how God spoke to Judah. Every morning, first thing.

Calvin draws out the contrast. "Jonadab only once gave his command to his children; that command, which might have been forgotten, remained perpetually in the hearts of his sons, so that they taught the same to his grandsons. But God commanded what was right not only once, but rose up early, that is, he sedulously anticipated them." Sedulously means diligently. One command from a dead man, kept for generations. Command after command from the living God, every morning, ignored. And Calvin notes the turn in verse seventeen. A people who will not have God's word spoken to them will get God's word spoken against them.

Then the promise of verse nineteen. Jonadab the son of Rechab shall never lack a man to stand before me. Standing before God often describes priests, and some rabbis said the Rechabites would join the priesthood. Calvin says no. God means that Jonadab's family will never die out. John Gill notes that the ancient Aramaic paraphrase renders it, ministering before me, and takes it as worship kept up in their own households.

The church has argued over this chapter. Jerome, writing to his friend Paulinus, listed heroes of the monastic life, and he put the Rechabites on the list. "The sons of Rechab too are of the number who drank neither wine nor strong drink and who abode in tents; men whom God's voice praises through Jeremiah." Jerome saw a model for monks. Calvin, more than a thousand years later, objected when the Rechabites were used to defend binding church rules. The text does not commend Jonadab's rule, he says. It commends the sons' obedience, and it uses that obedience to shame Judah. So do not build binding religious rules on this chapter. That is a real disagreement between a father and a Reformer, and I think Calvin reads the text more carefully.

Now chapter thirty-six. The fourth year of Jehoiakim is 605 B.C., the year Nebuchadnezzar beat Egypt at Carchemish, as Jeremiah forty-six records. God tells Jeremiah to write down everything he has preached since the days of Josiah, more than twenty years of sermons, in one scroll. Why? Verse three. It may be that the house of Judah will hear all the disaster that I intend to do to them, so that every one may turn from his evil way, and that I may forgive their iniquity and their sin. It may be. After two decades of rejection, God has it all written down one more time.

Jeremiah is banned from the temple, so his secretary Baruch reads it, in the ninth month of the fifth year, December of 604 B.C. The people have proclaimed a fast. Notice the timing. The Babylonian Chronicle, a cuneiform record of Nebuchadnezzar's campaigns, reports that in that same month he took the Philistine city of Ashkelon and left it in ruins. Judah fasts while a neighbor burns.

Baruch reads from the room of Gemariah son of Shaphan. In the City of David, archaeologists found a clay seal impression inscribed, belonging to Gemaryahu son of Shaphan, from a layer just before the Babylonian conquest. And you know Shaphan. He was Josiah's secretary, who read the newly found book of the law to King Josiah in Second Kings twenty-two. Josiah heard it and tore his clothes. Now Shaphan's son lends his room for the reading of another book, meant for Josiah's son.

The officials hear it, are afraid, and tell Baruch, go and hide, you and Jeremiah. Then they take the words to the king. And we are back in the winter house. Verse twenty-three. As Jehudi read three or four columns, the king would cut them off with a knife and throw them into the fire in the fire pot, until the entire scroll was consumed. The Hebrew calls it the knife of the scribe. The tool for making a scroll becomes the tool for destroying one.

Calvin caught a wordplay here. The Hebrew verb to read is kara. The verb to tear is also kara, with a different final letter. Jehudi reads, kara. The king tears, kara. Then verse twenty-four. Yet neither the king nor any of his servants who heard all these words was afraid, nor did they tear their garments. The same verb again. He tore the scroll. He would not tear his robe.

Matthew Henry ties it to the king's father. "So soon, so easily, do good impressions wear off. They showed some concern till they saw how light the king made of it, and then they shook off all that concern. They rent not their garments, as Josiah, this Jehoiakim's own father, did when he had the book of the law read to him." One generation earlier, a king heard the word and tore his clothes. His son hears the word and tears the word.

Three officials begged him not to burn it. He would not listen. There is our verb again. He orders the arrest of Baruch and Jeremiah. But the Lord hid them. Then the word comes again. Take another scroll and write on it all the former words. And verse thirty-two ends, many similar words were added to them. Henry says that out of the ashes of the first scroll rose a phoenix. You can burn a copy. You cannot burn what God has said.

Here is the first hard question. Verse thirty says of Jehoiakim, he shall have none to sit on the throne of David. But Second Kings twenty-four says his son Jehoiachin became king. Is that a contradiction? John Gill, the Baptist commentator, says the son's reign does not count, "for his son Jeconiah reigned but three months, which is reckoned as nothing, and could not be called sitting upon the throne." Jeconiah is Jehoiachin. As for Jehoiakim's body thrown out to the heat and the frost, Matthew Poole admits that Scripture never records how he died. But Jeremiah twenty-two had already promised him the burial of a donkey.

Now jump ahead about seventeen years. Zedekiah is on the throne, put there by Nebuchadnezzar in 597 B.C. Verse two of chapter thirty-seven sums up his reign. Neither he nor his servants nor the people of the land listened to the words of the Lord. Yet in verse three he sends to Jeremiah, please pray for us. Matthew Henry says Zedekiah wanted the prophet's prayers but had no use for the prophet's counsel.

During the siege, Pharaoh Hophra's army marches up from Egypt, and the Babylonians pull back to meet it. That is the lull we saw at the end of chapter thirty-four. Jeremiah's word in verse nine is blunt. Do not deceive yourselves. Even if only wounded men were left in their tents, they would get up and burn this city.

During the lull, Jeremiah sets out for the land of Benjamin to receive his portion there, very likely the family field at Anathoth from chapter thirty-two. A sentry at the Benjamin Gate grabs him. You are deserting to the Chaldeans. It is a lie, says Jeremiah. The sentry would not listen. The officials beat him and lock him in the dungeon cells under the house of Jonathan the secretary.

Then verse seventeen. The king sends for him and questions him secretly. Is there any word from the Lord? Jeremiah says, there is. You shall be delivered into the hand of the king of Babylon. Calvin notices what Zedekiah is really asking. He knows the old messages were true. He wants a new one. He hopes God has changed his mind. People like this, Calvin says, want God to bend to them, but God cannot deny himself.

Chapter thirty-eight. Four officials hear Jeremiah telling the people that whoever goes out to the Chaldeans will live. Two of those names, Jehucal son of Shelemiah and Gedaliah son of Pashhur, have turned up on clay seal impressions in Eilat Mazar's excavations in the City of David, a few yards apart. They tell the king, this man is weakening the hands of the soldiers.

Zedekiah answers in verse five. Behold, he is in your hands, for the king can do nothing against you. Spurgeon preached a whole sermon on that verse in March of 1890 and titled it, Zedekiah, or the Man Who Cannot Say No. "Zedekiah was a gentleman of a sort wonderfully common nowadays. A good-natured, easy man; his nobles could get anything they liked from him."

So they lower Jeremiah by ropes into the cistern of Malchiah. The Hebrew word is bor, a pit cut to hold rainwater, narrow at the mouth and wide below. There was no water in the cistern, but only mud, and Jeremiah sank in the mud. Remember that verb, sank. Lamentations three may preserve his memory of this day. I called on your name, O Lord, from the depths of the pit.

Help comes from the last place anyone in Judah would have looked. Ebed-melech, a Cushite from the upper Nile, an official in the king's house. His name means servant of the king. Calvin notes that among all the king's counselors not one stood up for the prophet, and the one man who did was a foreigner. Ebed-melech goes straight to the king at the Benjamin Gate, where the king sat to judge, and says to his face, these men have done evil. The king turns again and gives him thirty men.

Ebed-melech fetches old rags and worn-out clothes from a storeroom, lets them down on the ropes, and calls into the pit, put the rags and clothes between your armpits and the ropes. Alistair Begg preached on this scene and saw a pattern for Christian care in it. "It is an example which combines firmness with compassion. It's an example which is able to use the ropes, as they are necessary, but at the same time ensuring that we do not do so without the rags, which are equally necessary."

Then the last private meeting. Jeremiah lays out the plain choice. Surrender, and you and your house will live and this city will not burn. Refuse, and it burns. Zedekiah answers, I am afraid, afraid of the Judeans who have already gone over to Babylon. Jeremiah begs him in verse twenty, obey now the voice of the Lord. There is shama one more time. Then he describes a vision. The women of the palace are led out to Babylon's officers, chanting a taunt at their king. Now that your feet are sunk in the mud, your friends turn away from you. That is the same Hebrew verb as verse six. The prophet sank in the mud of the king's cistern. Now the king's own feet are sinking, and his friends are walking away.

That raises the second hard question. Zedekiah tells Jeremiah, if the officials ask what we discussed, tell them you were begging not to be sent back to Jonathan's house to die. The officials come, Jeremiah says exactly that, and they leave. Did Jeremiah lie?

The voices split. Calvin is uneasy. "It was not indeed a falsehood, but this evasion cannot be wholly excused." Fear, he thinks, bent the prophet's plainness. Matthew Henry, Matthew Poole, and John Gill all disagree. What Jeremiah said was true. He had made that very plea in chapter thirty-seven. Poole adds that these officials had no right to the conversation at all. The Westminster Larger Catechism, on the ninth commandment, forbids hiding the truth when justice needs it, and also counts telling the truth at the wrong moment as a sin. I side with Henry and Poole. Jeremiah said nothing false to men looking for a reason to kill him, and the text reports his answer without praising it. But Calvin keeps us honest about how fear can bend even a prophet.

Now one thing to take with you, and it comes from Zedekiah. Jehoiakim burned the word. Zedekiah kept asking for it. Twice he brings Jeremiah in secretly, twice he hears the same answer, and twice he goes back to doing what his officials want. Spurgeon traces that weakness to a missing fear of God. The man who fears God, he says, has no need to fear anyone else.

So here is the question. Is there a place in your life where you already know what God has said, and you keep coming back to him privately, hoping for a different word? Zedekiah had the word. He was waiting for one that would cost him less. Today, name that one thing, and do the first piece of what you already know.

Chapter thirty-eight ends with Jeremiah in the court of the guard until the day Jerusalem was taken. Matthew Poole notes that God hid Noah in an ark and Lot in a small town, and here he hides his prophet in a prison yard.

Ecclesiastes eleven opens the Preacher's final movement. Verses one through six are about how to act when you cannot see the outcome. Verse seven through the poem in chapter twelve is about youth, old age, and death. Watch one phrase in the first six verses. You do not know. Verse two, you know not what disaster may happen. Verse five, twice, you do not know the way the spirit comes, and you do not know the work of God. Verse six, you do not know which will prosper. Four times, and every time his conclusion is to act anyway.

Verse one. Cast your bread upon the waters, for you will find it after many days. Is that about charity or about business? Some modern readers hear sea trade. The early church heard generosity. Gregory Thaumaturgus, a bishop of the third century who wrote a paraphrase of Ecclesiastes, takes it as giving to the needy even when it feels like throwing it away. Matthew Henry manages both. Give it away, he says, the way a merchant sends a cargo on a voyage, and trust it to the water. Verse two agrees. Give a portion to seven, or even to eight, which means as many as you can, and then one more.

Verses three and four set two things side by side. Some things you cannot change. Full clouds empty out. A fallen tree lies where it falls. Other things you cannot predict. Then comes the proverb. He who observes the wind will not sow, and he who regards the clouds will not reap. Gregory's paraphrase is sharp. In his words, "there have been those who, scrutinizing the clouds and waiting for the wind, have had nought to do with reaping and winnowing, putting their trust in vanity." Nought means nothing.

Spurgeon preached on verse four in the summer of 1890, a few months after his Zedekiah sermon, and he called it sowing in the wind and reaping under clouds. He says that watching the weather this way is a form of unbelief. "Who manages the wind? You distrust him who is Lord of north, and south, and east, and west."

Verse five has a double meaning worth hearing. The Hebrew word ruach can mean wind or spirit. Right after the wind proverb comes, as you do not know the way the spirit comes to the bones in the womb of a woman with child. The footnote in your Bible gives the other reading, the way of the wind. Either way, the life forming inside a mother is hidden from you, and so is the work of God. So, verse six, in the morning sow your seed, and at evening withhold not your hand.

Then the hard question in verse nine. Rejoice, O young man, in your youth. Walk in the ways of your heart and the sight of your eyes. But know that for all these things God will bring you into judgment. Is the first half sarcasm? Matthew Henry and Matthew Poole both say yes. They read it like Elijah taunting the prophets of Baal. Go ahead, enjoy yourself, but the bill is coming. Gregory goes further. He puts those words in the mouth of a pleasure seeker and then answers him. John Gill reads the verse straight. Joy is allowed, he says, within proper limits, and the whole book commends it.

I think Gill is right. Ecclesiastes keeps telling you to enjoy your food, your work, your wife, and your days as gifts from God's hand, in chapter two, chapter three, chapter five, chapter eight, and chapter nine. Verse nine is the last of those. The judgment clause does not cancel the joy. It frames it. Enjoy your youth as a man who will give an account for it. Verse eight says the same thing to older people. Light is sweet. Rejoice in all your years. And remember that the days of darkness will be many. Twice in these verses the Preacher calls the years hevel, a breath, like your breath on a cold morning.

So, practically. If you are waiting for perfect conditions before you start the thing God has plainly given you to do, the conversation, the gift, the work, you will be standing at the edge of the field when the season ends. And while you sow, enjoy the light. It is his gift, and it is brief.

John four, verses forty-three to fifty-four. Jesus has spent two days in Samaria, where a whole town believed because of his word. Now he goes north into Galilee and comes back to Cana, where he turned water into wine. John calls this the second sign. Both signs happen at Cana, with Nicodemus and the woman at the well in between.

Verse forty-four is a puzzle. For Jesus himself had testified that a prophet has no honor in his own hometown. Then verse forty-five says the Galileans welcomed him. How does that fit? Augustine says the hometown is Galilee, and the point is the contrast with believing Samaria. Chrysostom thinks John means Capernaum. Calvin finds Augustine too clever and Chrysostom unconvincing, and follows Cyril of Alexandria, who thought Jesus passed by Nazareth. R. C. Sproul, preaching this text in 2002, suggested hearing the welcome with a touch of irony. The Galileans welcomed him because of what they had seen him do at the feast in Jerusalem. They welcomed a miracle worker. That is not the same as honoring a prophet. I think Sproul is onto something, because the very next scene tests exactly that kind of faith.

An official from Capernaum comes to him. John's word is basilikos, a royal man, probably in the service of Herod Antipas. Capernaum lies down by the lake, most of a day's walk below Cana in the hills. That is why everyone in this story keeps saying come down and going down. His son is at the point of death. Come down and heal him.

Jesus answers in verse forty-eight. Unless you see signs and wonders you will not believe. The footnote in the English Standard Version tells you the word you is plural, both times. Jesus is speaking past this man to the whole crowd. Calvin calls it a reproof of a whole people. The man does not argue. Sir, come down before my child dies.

Chrysostom saw what Jesus was doing. "For here He heals the father, sick in mind, no less than the son, in order to persuade us to give heed to Him, not by reason of His miracles, but of His teaching." Jesus will not come down. He gives the man a sentence instead. Go; your son will live. And verse fifty says the man believed the word that Jesus spoke to him and went on his way.

Sproul put it simply. "A Christian's life is not about believing in God; it is about believing God." The official had no sign in his hand. He had a sentence. He turned around and walked toward home on it.

The next day his servants meet him on the road. Your son is recovering. When did he get better? Yesterday at the seventh hour, about one in the afternoon. The very hour Jesus spoke. And verse fifty-three says, he himself believed, and all his household. Spurgeon preached this story in 1885 and traced faith growing in this man like a fire, a spark, then smoke, then flame, and at last the whole house catches. Calvin helps here too. The man's first faith was narrow, enough to trust Jesus for his son's life. By verse fifty-three it has become faith in Jesus himself, and his whole household follows him into it. Calvin wonders how many hearers get as much out of a year of sermons as this half-pagan official got out of one sentence.

Augustine notices that the Samaritans in the previous scene believed without any sign at all, on the word alone. Then he turns the passage on his own congregation, and on us. "I am speaking to God's people; so many of us have believed, what signs have we seen?" You have not watched a fever break at the word of Jesus. You have his words written down. The official walked home on one sentence. You have a whole book of them.

First Peter one, verses ten through sixteen. Last time we read Peter's opening praise of the living hope. Now he steps back and says, look how long this salvation has been coming.

Verse ten. Concerning this salvation, the prophets who prophesied about the grace that was to be yours searched and inquired carefully. Matthew Henry says the words picture miners digging down through earth and rock to reach the ore. The prophets received revelation, and then they studied it. Verse eleven tells you what they were digging for. They were inquiring what person or time the Spirit of Christ in them was indicating when he predicted the sufferings of Christ and the subsequent glories.

Stop on that phrase, the Spirit of Christ in them. Peter says the Spirit who spoke through Isaiah and Jeremiah was the Spirit of Christ. Henry draws the plain conclusion. Christ existed before he was born in Bethlehem, because his Spirit was already at work in the prophets. Clement of Alexandria, writing around the year 200, took the phrase another way and thought it meant angels who serve Christ. Calvin and Henry are right against him. Peter means the Holy Spirit, and he calls him the Spirit of Christ.

Here is the hard question. If the prophets were still searching for this salvation, did they have it? Some in Calvin's day said no. Calvin says that reading twists this passage. The prophets trusted the coming Christ and fed on him by faith. What they lacked was the sight of him come in the flesh. The Westminster Confession, in chapter seven, says the same. Under the law, the promises, prophecies, and sacrifices all pointed to the Messiah who was coming, and through them the elect had full forgiveness of sins and eternal salvation. There is one covenant of grace, administered in two ways.

Then verse twelve. It was revealed to them that they were serving not themselves but you. Calvin has a lovely line on this. "What else then was this, but that they spread the table, that others might afterwards feed on the provisions laid on it." The prophets set the table. You sit down to eat. And then the last clause, things into which angels long to look. The Greek verb is parakupto. It means to stoop down and peer in. John uses the same verb for the disciples bending to look into the empty tomb. Angels are bent over the gospel, looking.

Verse thirteen begins with therefore, and here is the first command in the whole letter. Preparing your minds for action, and being sober-minded, set your hope fully on the grace that will be brought to you at the revelation of Jesus Christ. The footnote gives you the literal Greek, girding up the loins of your mind. Tuck your robe into your belt and run. John Piper preached on this verse and noticed that the main verb is hope. The girding and the sobriety serve the hoping. And he says this about the order of Peter's letter. "Christianity is not first an ethic. It is not first a faith or a feeling or a theology. It is first the sovereign, initiative-taking action of God." Twelve verses on what God has done come before a single command.

Then verses fourteen through sixteen. As obedient children, do not be conformed to the passions of your former ignorance, but as he who called you is holy, you also be holy in all your conduct, since it is written, You shall be holy, for I am holy. Peter is quoting Leviticus, chapters eleven and nineteen. The word is hagios, set apart, belonging to God. Henry says we copy God's holiness even though we will never match it. And look at the phrase obedient children. Literally it is children of obedience, and the Greek word for obedience is built on the verb to hear. Peter, like Jeremiah, thinks of obedience as hearing that goes all the way down.

Two threads in today's readings belong together.

First, Peter says the prophets were serving not themselves but you. Think about what that means for Jeremiah thirty-six. A king burned the scroll. Jeremiah dictated it again, and many similar words were added. That second scroll did nothing for Jehoiakim, who never repented. It served the exiles who would read it in Babylon, and the church after them, and you this morning. The book you are reading exists in part because a scroll was burned and written again. Baruch's ink outlasted the king's fire.

Second, Ecclesiastes says you do not know which will prosper, this or that. Neither did Jeremiah, who watched twenty years of sermons go into a fire. Calvin, commenting on that very fire, says a servant of God is to do what God commands even when no fruit appears, and to leave the outcome with God. Sow in the morning. Write the scroll again.

And one contrast to sit with. A royal official in Galilee asked Jesus for help, got one sentence, believed it, and walked home. A king in Jerusalem asked Jeremiah, is there any word from the Lord, got the same answer twice, and would not walk out the gate. So here is your question for the day. What word has God already given you, in Scripture, plainly, that you are still standing at the gate about? Walk on it today, before you understand how it will turn out.
