A house in Tel-abib, by the Chebar canal. The elders of the exile have come again. They sit down in front of the prophet, the way men sit down when they want an answer from God. They have a question, and they have a plan.

And God says this. As I live, declares the Lord God, I will not be inquired of by you.

Then he tells them what their plan is. Verse thirty-two. What is in your mind shall never happen. The thought, Let us be like the nations, and worship wood and stone.

They wanted to disappear into Babylon. Blend in. Be like everyone else. And God says, I will not let you go.

Is that a threat, or is it mercy?

This is Old Paths. Today is day seven of the eleventh month in the reading plan, day two hundred fifty-seven of three hundred. The readings are Ezekiel chapters twenty and twenty-one, Job chapter seven, John chapter eight, verses forty-eight through fifty-nine, and Second Peter chapter three, verses ten through eighteen. That last one finishes Second Peter, so today we hear the apostle's final words.

Last time, Ezekiel eighteen ended with God pleading. Why will you die, O house of Israel? Turn, and live. Today Ezekiel twenty tells the history behind that plea. It turns out that Israel has been refusing to turn since the day God found them in Egypt. And the question underneath it all, why God keeps sparing them, gets its answer, four times over, in one phrase. For the sake of my name.

Two promises from last time come due today. In Ezekiel nineteen, the funeral song ended with the line, there remains in it no strong stem, no scepter for ruling. I said we would hear God's answer to that in chapter twenty-one, verse twenty-seven. We will. And back in episode eighteen, on John eight, verse twenty-four, I sided with Augustine against Calvin about what Jesus meant by I am he. Today Jesus says it in its fullest form, before Abraham was, I am, and Calvin himself gets a chance to answer.

And Peter, who left us asking why the Lord delays, closes that question in verse fifteen. Count the patience of our Lord as salvation.

Ezekiel twenty and twenty-one.

Start with the date, because Ezekiel gives it to the day. Chapter twenty, verse one. In the seventh year, in the fifth month, on the tenth day of the month. That is the seventh year of King Jehoiachin's exile, the month of Ab, high summer of 591 B.C. Ellicott's commentary works out that it is eleven months and five days since the temple vision of chapter eight. This opens a new dated section of the book, and the next date, in chapter twenty-four, will be the day the siege of Jerusalem begins.

John Gill notices something about this date. The tenth day of the fifth month is the day the temple would later burn, according to Jeremiah fifty-two. Five years early, on that very day, the elders sit in Ezekiel's house asking for a word.

Zedekiah is still king in Jerusalem, still talking to Egypt. Nebuchadnezzar is about three years away from marching west. And the elders are back, as in chapter fourteen. What do they want? Calvin reads verse thirty-two as the key. They were thinking that it would be better to stop being a peculiar people. The God of Israel had only brought them trouble. Why not become like the Babylonians, and be left in peace?

God refuses to answer the question. Instead he answers the questioner. Verse four. Will you judge them, son of man? Let them know the abominations of their fathers.

And then comes one of the great history lessons in the prophets. It moves in three rounds, and every round has the same four beats. God gives grace. Israel rebels. God says, I would pour out my wrath. And then, but I acted for the sake of my name.

Round one is Egypt, verses five through nine. On the day when I chose Israel, I swore to the offspring of the house of Jacob. The Hebrew says, I lifted my hand. The ESV footnote tells you that, and it comes back six times in this chapter. It is the gesture of a man taking an oath. God raised his hand in Egypt and swore, I am the Lord your God. And Israel, even in Egypt, kept the idols of Egypt. Ezekiel is telling you that Israel's idolatry did not start with the golden calf. It was in their luggage when they left Egypt.

Round two is the wilderness, verses ten through seventeen. I gave them my statutes, by which, if a person does them, he shall live. That is Leviticus eighteen, verse five. And I gave them my Sabbaths, as a sign between me and them. They rebelled. They profaned the Sabbaths. And again, verse fourteen, I acted for the sake of my name.

Round three is the children, verses eighteen through twenty-six. They did exactly what their fathers did.

Now what does for the sake of my name mean? It does not mean God is vain. Verse nine says it plainly. That it should not be profaned in the sight of the nations. God had attached his name to this people in front of the whole world. If he wiped them out in the desert, Egypt would say, Their God could get them out, but he could not keep them. Calvin puts the two things together. He says God spared them for his own glory more than out of pity for them. But then he adds this. "certainly God's glory and the salvation of the Church are things almost inseparably united."

That is a comfort. If God saved you because you were worth saving, you could lose it by becoming worthless. If he saved you for his name, your safety rests on his name.

Now we come to the hardest verse in the chapter, and one of the hardest in Ezekiel. Verse twenty-five. Moreover, I gave them statutes that were not good and rules by which they could not have life.

What? God gave bad laws?

The earliest Christian readers had an answer. Justin Martyr, writing to a Jewish teacher named Trypho in the second century, quotes these verses and says, "God enjoined you to keep the Sabbath, and impose on you other precepts for a sign, as I have already said, on account of your unrighteousness, and that of your fathers". Irenaeus went further. He said that after the golden calf, God laid the ceremonial law on Israel as a yoke, a kind of servitude that matched the slavery they had wanted when they looked back to Egypt. For both of these fathers, the statutes that were not good are the ceremonies of Moses.

Calvin will not have it. He says that reading distorts the passage, because Ezekiel is contrasting God's law with the superstitions of the nations. Here is his explanation. "God, therefore, gave them laws that were not good, when he suffered them to be miserably subjected to an immense heap of errors". Keil makes the same point from the other side. The ceremonial commandments, he says, were good, and carried the promise of life. Matthew Poole says the phrase is an understatement. Not good means very bad.

Look at verse twenty-six, which explains verse twenty-five. I defiled them through their very gifts in their offering up all their firstborn. That is child sacrifice. God commanded that the firstborn be redeemed and given to him. Israel passed them through the fire to Molech instead. Those were the statutes that were not good. And God gave them in the way a judge gives a man over to what he insists on.

That is where the New Testament genuinely helps, and Calvin makes this move himself. Romans one, verse twenty-four. Therefore God gave them up in the lusts of their hearts. The worst thing God can do to a person who keeps refusing him is to let him have his way.

So I side with Calvin and Keil. God's own law was holy; verse eleven says a man shall live by it. But the fathers saw something true. God can give a hard people what they demand, and what they demand can destroy them.

Then the elders' plan comes into the open in verse thirty-two. Let us be like the nations. And God answers with an oath. Verse thirty-three. As I live, declares the Lord God, surely with a mighty hand and an outstretched arm and with wrath poured out I will be king over you.

Those are exodus words. A second exodus, but this one starts with wrath. Matthew Henry says it about as well as it can be said. "There is no shaking off God's dominion; rule he will, either with the golden sceptre or with the iron rod; and those that will not yield to the power of his grace shall be made to sink under the power of his wrath."

Verse thirty-five. I will bring you into the wilderness of the peoples. Babylon itself will become a wilderness where God sorts his people.

And verse thirty-seven. I will make you pass under the rod, and I will bring you into the bond of the covenant.

Here two of our voices read the same words in opposite directions, and I think both of them are right. Calvin says the rod is a shepherd's crook, the staff a shepherd uses to number his flock. But Calvin hears this as a threat. The exiles thought they could slip away into the crowd of the nations. God says, no, I will count every one of you, and call you to account, as if your profession of faith were branded into you.

Spurgeon preached on these verses in May of 1885, and he saw the same shepherd at the same gate, touching each sheep with his staff as it comes through. And Spurgeon says this. "But there is far more room for comfort than for dismay, for that which God counts He values, and if He visits you with special chastisement it is because He has special designs of grace towards you which you shall understand by and by."

So which is it? The text answers. Verse thirty-eight. I will purge out the rebels from among you. The counting is how God separates. For the rebel, being counted is judgment. For the one who belongs to him, it is the most precious thing in the world. The same staff touches both.

The chapter's ending is pure grace. On my holy mountain, all the house of Israel shall serve me. As a pleasing aroma I will accept you. And then verse forty-three. There you shall remember your ways, and you shall loathe yourselves for all the evils that you have committed. Notice where the loathing happens. Not in exile, but in the land, after acceptance. Calvin says that is real repentance. God's kindness does not make you forget your sin. It makes you ashamed of it. And verse forty-four gives the last for my name's sake. When I deal with you for my name's sake, not according to your evil ways.

Then the tone turns dark again. Verse forty-seven. I will kindle a fire in you, and it shall devour every green tree in you and every dry tree. And Ezekiel says, Ah, Lord God! They are saying of me, Is he not a maker of parables? The prophet is being dismissed as a storyteller. So in chapter twenty-one God drops the parable and says it plainly.

Chapter twenty-one, verse three. Behold, I am against you and will draw my sword from its sheath and will cut off from you both righteous and wicked.

Both righteous and wicked. That should stop you. In chapter nine, the man in linen marked those who sighed and groaned. In chapter eighteen, the soul who sins shall die, and the righteous shall live. Has God changed his mind?

No. The sword of chapter twenty-one is the Babylonian army, and an army does not check the marks on foreheads. Daniel and Ezekiel themselves were carried off to Babylon. Good people would starve in the siege. The righteous really did suffer in the fall of Jerusalem. But Matthew Henry draws the line exactly where Ezekiel draws it. "No; God's graces and comforts make a great difference when his providence seems to make none." The same sword can fall on two men, and one of them is still marked for life.

So God tells Ezekiel to act it out. Verse six. Groan, with breaking heart and bitter grief, groan before their eyes. And when they ask why, say, Because of the news that it is coming.

Then comes the Song of the Sword, verses eight through seventeen. A sword, a sword is sharpened and also polished. It is a war chant, and Ezekiel is told to clap his hands and strike his thigh as he sings it.

Then verse nineteen gives us one of the most vivid scenes in the book. Ezekiel is told to draw a map. Two roads coming out of Babylon, with a signpost where they split. One road goes to Rabbah of the Ammonites, the modern city of Amman. The other goes to Jerusalem. Both nations had joined the rebellion, and Nebuchadnezzar can only take one at a time.

Verse twenty-one. For the king of Babylon stands at the parting of the way, at the head of the two ways, to use divination. He shakes the arrows; he consults the teraphim; he looks at the liver.

That last detail is not a figure of speech. Babylonian diviners read the livers of sacrificed sheep, and archaeologists have recovered clay models of sheep's livers used in that work. The British Museum holds one from the old Babylonian period, and the Science Museum explains how they worked. The diviner examined the real liver, placed small sticks into the matching holes in the clay model, and read the pattern. That is what Nebuchadnezzar is doing at the crossroads. And verse twenty-two says, into his right hand comes the divination for Jerusalem.

The most powerful man in the world is guessing with a sheep's liver, and God is steering his hand.

And then the prophet turns to Zedekiah. Verse twenty-five. And you, O profane wicked one, prince of Israel, whose day has come. Remove the turban and take off the crown. Things shall not remain as they are. Exalt that which is low, and bring low that which is exalted. A ruin, ruin, ruin I will make it.

And now listen to the promise I said we would hear. This also shall not be, until he comes, the one to whom judgment belongs, and I will give it to him.

Chapter nineteen said there was no scepter left. Chapter twenty-one says the throne will stay empty until its rightful owner arrives. And the Hebrew here is not new. Keil points out that it echoes Jacob's blessing on Judah in Genesis forty-nine, verse ten. The scepter shall not depart from Judah, until tribute comes to him. The word for judgment is mishpat, justice, the right to rule justly. A Ligonier devotional says Ezekiel understands Jacob's blessing "to be a messianic passage connected to the restoration of the Davidic monarchy, which was the prophetic hope".

This is a direct messianic prophecy, so the New Testament move is warranted. Luke one, verse thirty-two. The angel says to Mary, the Lord God will give to him the throne of his father David. I will give it to him, says Ezekiel. The Lord God will give to him, says Gabriel. No son of David wore that crown again. Then, nearly six hundred years later, he came, the one whose right it is.

Here is the application, and it comes straight from verse thirty-two. The elders' plan was not to become atheists. It was to become normal. To stop being different. Be like the nations. You know that pull. The quiet decision to stop being odd about the Lord's Day, your marriage, your money, your speech, because being different has only brought you trouble.

And God's answer is, What is in your mind shall never happen. If you belong to him, he will not let you dissolve into the crowd. He will count you at the gate. That can feel like the iron rod. It is actually the shepherd refusing to lose a sheep. Do not fight the hand that will not let you go.

Job chapter seven.

In chapter six, Job answered Eliphaz and called his friends a dry streambed. In chapter seven, something changes. He stops talking to the friends and starts talking to God. Watch the pronouns. Verse seven. Remember that my life is a breath. That is addressed to God. From here to the end of the chapter, it is you, you, you.

The chapter moves in four parts: life as hard labor, life as a breath, Job's complaint against God's watching, and finally, what is man?

Verse one. Has not man a hard service on earth, and are not his days like the days of a hired hand? The word for hard service is the word for military service, a term of conscription. Job feels drafted into suffering. He is a day laborer waiting for a wage that never comes, and his nights bring no rest.

Verse six. My days are swifter than a weaver's shuttle and come to their end without hope. The word for hope is tiqvah, and in Joshua two the same word is the scarlet cord Rahab hung from her window. A shuttle runs to the end of its thread, and Job's days are running out of cord. Gregory the Great, meditating on this verse fourteen centuries ago, noticed that a loom at least stops when the weaver rests. Life does not. He writes, "even while resting we are brought to the end of our way, and along the course of our passage, we go on even in sleeping."

Then verse twelve, the most startling line in the chapter. Am I the sea, or a sea monster, that you set a guard over me? The word is tannin, the great sea creature of Genesis one. In the ancient world the sea was the picture of chaos that God holds back with a boundary. Job says, Lord, you are treating me like the ocean. Like a monster that has to be chained.

Is Job sinning when he says this? Keil thinks he is being pushed toward sinful language. Spurgeon, preaching on this verse in 1891, did not excuse Job, but he understood him. "Where God's saints are most glorious, there you will find their spots. The weaknesses of the saints lie near their strength." Job, the most patient of men, is being impatient.

And then verse seventeen. What is man, that you make so much of him, and that you set your heart on him, visit him every morning and test him every moment?

You have heard those words before. Psalm eight. What is man that you are mindful of him? David asks it in wonder at the glory God gives to man. Job asks it in exhaustion. Keil calls it, in some degree, a parody of Psalm eight. Same question, opposite mood. David says, why do you care so much about us? Job says, why can't you leave me alone? Verse nineteen. How long will you not look away from me, nor leave me alone till I swallow my spit?

Verse twenty. If I sin, what do I do to you, you watcher of mankind? The phrase is notser ha'adam. The older translations said preserver of men. Keil says it means the strict and constant observer of men. It is the same word Isaiah uses for God keeping his vineyard. Job takes a title of loving care and spits it back as an accusation. You are the one who watches. Why are you watching me?

And the chapter ends with a question that is almost a prayer. Verse twenty-one. Why do you not pardon my transgression and take away my iniquity? Spurgeon preached on that question too. He said Job was mistaken to think his suffering came from some hidden sin, and then he said something you need to hear. Job, he said, was not under punishment. God was honoring him.

So here is the wisdom of Job seven, and it is very practical. Job is wrong about what God is doing. But notice where he takes his complaint. Not to the friends. To God. The worst thing a sufferer can do is stop talking to God. The man who says, Why are you watching me, is still speaking to the One who watches. Take your bitterness there, honestly. He can bear it better than your friends can.

John eight, verses forty-eight through fifty-nine.

We are still in the temple, and this is the end of the longest argument in John's Gospel. It began at verse twelve with I am the light of the world. It ends with stones.

Verse forty-eight. Are we not right in saying that you are a Samaritan and have a demon? That is the lowest insult they have. A heretic and a madman. Jesus answers one charge and not the other. I do not have a demon. Augustine noticed that he never said, I am not a Samaritan, and Augustine suggests a reason. Samaritan, he says, can mean keeper. And Christ is our keeper.

But Chrysostom notices something even more useful for you and me. When these same men had boasted about Abraham and God, Jesus answered them sharply. When they insulted him personally, he answered gently. Chrysostom says Jesus was "thus teaching us to avenge insults offered to God, but to overlook such as are offered to ourselves." Most of us do it the other way around.

Verse fifty-one. Truly, truly, I say to you, if anyone keeps my word, he will never see death. Abraham died, they say, and the prophets died. Are you greater than our father Abraham? Who do you make yourself out to be?

Spurgeon preached verse fifty-one after the sudden death of his senior deacon, William Olney, a member of the church for more than fifty years. He told a grieving congregation that only one question matters here. Do you keep Christ's saying?

Notice how Jesus answers in verse fifty-four. If I glorify myself, my glory is nothing. It is my Father who glorifies me. He will not grab glory. He receives it. And verse fifty-five turns their claim around. You say, He is our God, but you have not known him. I know him. Jesus says that to claim less would make him a liar like them. Humility for him is not pretending to be less than he is. It is telling the truth about who he is, and giving the Father the credit.

Then verse fifty-six. Your father Abraham rejoiced that he would see my day. He saw it and was glad. When did Abraham see it? Augustine says he rejoiced in hope, that he might see it, and then by faith he saw it. Chrysostom thinks the day is the cross, and that Abraham glimpsed it on Mount Moriah when God provided the ram in Isaac's place.

They answer, You are not yet fifty years old, and have you seen Abraham?

And Jesus says, verse fifty-eight. Truly, truly, I say to you, before Abraham was, I am.

Listen to the verbs. Before Abraham came into being, genesthai, I am, ego eimi. Augustine saw it. "Before Abraham was made. Understand, that was made refers to human formation; but am to the Divine essence." Abraham had a beginning. Jesus does not.

In episode eighteen, Calvin read I am he in verse twenty-four as Christ's office, not his essence. Here too he reads it broadly, of the Mediator known to the fathers in every age. But he does not stop there. He writes, "And yet the efficacy which belonged, in all ages, to the grace of the Mediator depended on his eternal Divinity; so that this saying of Christ contains a remarkable testimony of his Divine essence." So Calvin and Augustine end up in the same place. The office rests on the essence. He can save in every age because he is the I am of every age.

And they understood him perfectly. Verse fifty-nine. So they picked up stones to throw at him. The law said the blasphemer must be stoned. Either he is God, or he deserves those stones. There is no middle option.

But Jesus hid himself and went out of the temple. Hold that picture for a moment. Last week in Ezekiel we watched the glory of the Lord leave the temple, step by step. Now the Lord of the temple walks out of it, and they did not even notice who had gone.

Second Peter three, verses ten through eighteen.

These are Peter's last written words. Last time he answered the scoffers with the flood, with God's sense of time, and with God's patience. Now he tells you how that day will come.

Verse ten. But the day of the Lord will come like a thief. Sudden. Unannounced. Then the heavens will pass away with a roar, and the heavenly bodies will be burned up and dissolved, and the earth and the works that are done on it will be exposed. That last word in Greek is literally found. Everything done on earth will be found out. Nothing will be hidden in that fire.

Calvin says Peter is pressing on two diseases at once. "For we all labor under two very different evils". Too much haste, and slothfulness. "We are seized with impatience for the day of Christ already expected; at the same time we securely regard it as afar off." Isn't that you? Impatient that God has not fixed things yet, and at the same time living as if he never will.

Will the world be annihilated, or renewed? Augustine, in the City of God, says the fire will change the qualities of the elements, so that the world itself is renewed into some better thing, fit for men renewed in their bodies. Calvin agrees. The elements are consumed only so they may be renovated, their substance remaining the same. That is why verse thirteen says new heavens and a new earth, in which righteousness dwells. Not a different world. This world, made right.

Then Peter's question. Verse eleven. Since all these things are thus to be dissolved, what sort of people ought you to be in lives of holiness and godliness? In Greek, both of those words are plural. Holinesses and godlinesses. David Strain, preaching this text at First Presbyterian in Jackson, put it well. "A holy person, a godly person, is characterized by lots and lots and lots of little holinesses and godlinesses every single day."

Verse twelve. Waiting for and hastening the coming of the day of God. How can you hasten it? Strain says it is not that we can move up God's calendar. It is that we can live in such a way that the waiting time flies, the way the years fly for a parent who loves his children.

Then verse fifteen, the answer to last time's question. And count the patience of our Lord as salvation. Every day the Lord delays is not a broken promise. It is another day of rescue.

Then Peter does something remarkable. Just as our beloved brother Paul also wrote to you. There are some things in them that are hard to understand, which the ignorant and unstable twist to their own destruction, as they do the other Scriptures. Do you hear that phrase? The other Scriptures. Within Peter's own lifetime, he places Paul's letters alongside the Old Testament as Scripture. That is one of the clearest testimonies to the New Testament canon in the New Testament itself. And notice that Peter, the senior apostle, admits Paul is hard. Calvin's lesson is not to stop reading Paul, but to read him with a teachable mind.

And the letter ends with two commands. Verse seventeen, take care that you are not carried away. Verse eighteen, grow. But grow in the grace and knowledge of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ. Calvin says that is the only way to persevere, to keep moving forward. Spurgeon used the picture of a child's spinning top. It stands up as long as it keeps spinning. When it stops, it falls. The way to stand is to grow.

And the very last line is a doxology. To him be the glory both now and to the day of eternity. Amen. To him. To Jesus. Calvin says this line alone proves the divinity of Christ, because glory forever belongs to God alone. Peter, who once denied him, ends his life giving him the glory that belongs to God.

Two connections today, and both are really in the text.

The first is the name. In Ezekiel twenty, God says over and over, I acted for the sake of my name, and you shall know that I am the Lord. That is the name God gave Moses at the burning bush. I am who I am. In John eight, a man not yet fifty stands in the temple and says, before Abraham was, I am. And in Second Peter, the apostle ends by giving that same Jesus the glory that belongs to God forever. The name that kept Israel alive in the wilderness is the name that stood in the temple and was nearly stoned.

The second is the God who watches. Job says, What is man, that you visit him every morning, you watcher of mankind? It feels to him like surveillance. In Ezekiel twenty, God says, I will make you pass under the rod. I will count you, one by one, at the gate. To the rebel that is terror. To the sheep, it is love. That which God counts, he values.

Job could not yet see the shepherd behind the rod. Neither could the elders in Tel-abib. Here is your question for the day. Where are you quietly trying to become like everyone else, so that God will leave you alone? And what if his refusal to leave you alone is the kindest thing he is doing for you?
