Ezekiel twenty-nine, verse three. Behold, I am against you, Pharaoh king of Egypt, the great dragon that lies in the midst of his streams, that says, My Nile is my own; I made it for myself.

Picture a crocodile on a mudbank in the sun. Eyes half closed. The whole river around him. Nothing in that water is bigger than he is.

Then verse six. Egypt has been a staff of reed to the house of Israel. You lean your weight on it, it snaps, and the splintered end goes into your shoulder.

So here is the question for today. What are you leaning on? And has it ever actually held?

This is Old Paths. Today is day eleven of the eleventh month in the reading plan, day two hundred sixty-one of three hundred. The readings are Ezekiel chapters twenty-nine and thirty, Job chapter ten, John chapter ten, verses one through ten, and First John chapter two, verses twelve through seventeen.

Last time we watched Tyre go down in the heart of the seas, the proud merchant city sinking with all her cargo. And I promised you a strange verse. Ezekiel twenty-nine, verse eighteen, where God says that Nebuchadnezzar and his army labored against Tyre until every head was bald and every shoulder rubbed raw, and they got no wages for it. A prophet admitting that a conqueror came up short on pay. We will take that head on today, because it is one of the most honest verses in the prophets.

We also left a man in John nine outside the synagogue door, thrown out for telling the truth about the one who opened his eyes. John ten does not start a new scene. Jesus keeps talking to the same Pharisees, and now he talks about doors, thieves, and a shepherd who leads his sheep out.

In Job, the man who wished for an arbiter now stops talking about God and starts talking to him. And in First John, the apostle who told us yesterday that we have an advocate now tells us what not to love.

Ezekiel twenty-nine.

First, where are we in the book? Chapters twenty-five through thirty-two are the oracles against the nations. They sit between the beginning of the siege of Jerusalem in chapter twenty-four and the arrival of the refugee with the news that the city has fallen in chapter thirty-three. Seven nations are addressed. Egypt gets the most space by far, four full chapters. Keil counts seven separate words of God against Egypt, and they are not in date order. They are gathered here so that you hear the whole case against Egypt in one place.

Look at the date in verse one. The tenth year, the tenth month, the twelfth day. That is January of 587 B.C. Jerusalem has been under siege for a full year. And here is what makes the date so pointed. Jeremiah thirty-seven tells us that during this siege, Pharaoh's army marched up out of Egypt, and the Babylonians lifted the siege to go and meet it. For a few weeks, Jerusalem breathed. Egypt is coming. We are saved.

That Pharaoh was Hophra, as Jeremiah names him, known to the Greeks as Apries. He took the throne in 589 B.C., in the delta city of Sais. And the people of Judah had been looking south for help for a long time. Archaeologists dug up a set of letters written on broken pottery at Lachish, a fortress city southwest of Jerusalem, from these very years. In one of them, an officer reports that the commander of the army, Coniah son of Elnatan, has gone down to Egypt. That is the mood. When Babylon comes, you send someone to Egypt.

Into that hope Ezekiel speaks verse three. The great dragon. The Hebrew word is tannim, a long creature of the water, a serpent or sea monster. Here it is the crocodile, the animal of the Nile. Keil puts it well. As the crocodile lies quietly in the waters of the Nile, as though he were lord of the river; so did Pharaoh regard himself as the omnipotent lord of Egypt.

And listen to what the crocodile says. My Nile is my own; I made it for myself. Egypt really was the gift of the river. No rain to speak of, just the yearly flood and the canals. Pharaoh looks at the water that feeds his whole kingdom and says, I made that.

Matthew Henry sees exactly how deep the offense goes. He writes that Pharaoh usurps two of the divine prerogatives, to be the author and the end of his own being and felicity. He only that is the great Creator can say of this world, and of every thing in it, I have made it for myself. Then he adds one line I would like you to write down. Self is the great idol that all the world worships, in contempt of God and his sovereignty.

The Greek historian Herodotus, writing about a century and a half later, reports that Apries believed not even a god could remove him from his throne. That is Ezekiel twenty-nine, verse three, written by a pagan historian who had never read Ezekiel.

So God goes fishing. Verse four. I will put hooks in your jaws, and make the fish of your streams stick to your scales. The fish are Egypt's people and soldiers, clinging to their king. God hauls the whole thing up out of the water and throws it onto the open field, where the birds and the beasts eat it. For a pharaoh, that is the worst ending imaginable. These were kings who spent their reigns preparing tombs. Ezekiel says this one will not even be gathered for burial.

Now, not everyone has read the dragon as a crocodile. Origen, the third century Alexandrian, quotes these verses in his commentary on John and asks, For what real bodily dragon has ever been reported as having been seen in the material river of Egypt? But consider if the river of Egypt be not the dwelling of the dragon who is our enemy, who was not even able to kill the child Moses.

I think Origen is reaching past the text. There were plenty of real crocodiles in the Nile, and Poole, Gill, and Keil all say so plainly. Ezekiel is talking about a real king named Hophra. But Origen hears something true. The prophets did use the sea monster for Egypt and for the chaos that sets itself against God. Isaiah fifty-one asks, Was it not you who pierced the dragon? So when Pharaoh lies in his river claiming to be its maker, he is playing a much older part. The text keeps us on the Nile. But the Nile is not a neutral place in Scripture.

Then verse six turns the image. Because you have been a staff of reed to the house of Israel. Reeds grow everywhere along the Nile. Pick one up and lean on it like a walking stick and it splits lengthwise, and the jagged edge goes into your hand and shoulder. Your loins shake, as the ESV puts it, and you go down.

This was not a new insult. More than a century earlier, the Assyrian field commander had stood outside Jerusalem and shouted at Hezekiah's men, Behold, you are trusting in Egypt, that broken reed of a staff, which will pierce the hand of any man who leans on it. That is Isaiah thirty-six. Now God says the same thing. Keil notices that Egypt's guilt in Ezekiel is two things only: Pharaoh making himself a god, and the treacherous help Egypt kept offering Israel. D. A. Carson draws the plain moral from it: people and nations alike should never promise what they cannot deliver.

Verses nine through sixteen announce desolation from Migdol to Syene, that is, from the northeastern border all the way down to Aswan, the whole length of the land. Forty years of desolation. Then a surprise in verse thirteen. At the end of forty years I will gather the Egyptians. God restores Egypt. But only to Pathros, Upper Egypt, the land of their origin, and verse fifteen says it shall be the most lowly of the kingdoms.

Now a fair question. We have no record of Egypt lying completely empty for forty years. What do we do with that? Ellicott makes a good point. Ezekiel loves to speak in the language of Israel's own history. Forty years is the wilderness number. Egypt will have her own wilderness, her own time under judgment, and then a return. In his judgment the picture is drawn in strong, concrete colors that were never meant to be read like a census. Keil adds the part that did come true. After the Babylonian period, Egypt never got back its old power. Persia came, then Alexander, then the Ptolemies, who were Greeks ruling from Alexandria, then Rome. The empire of the pharaohs was over.

And verse sixteen tells you why God is doing this. It shall never again be the reliance of the house of Israel. God breaks Egypt partly for Israel's sake, so they will stop leaning on it. Henry says it this way. The creatures we confide in are often therefore ruined, because there is no other way effectually to cure us of our confidence in them.

Now verse seventeen, the verse I promised. The date jumps. The twenty-seventh year, the first month, the first day. That is April of 571 B.C. It is the latest date anywhere in Ezekiel. Ezekiel has been in exile twenty-seven years. Jerusalem has been in ruins for fifteen. This oracle was given long after everything else in these chapters, and it was placed here, out of order, because it belongs to the Egypt material.

Verse eighteen. Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon made his army labor hard against Tyre. Every head was made bald, and every shoulder was rubbed bare, yet neither he nor his army got anything from Tyre to pay for the labor.

Josephus tells us the siege of Tyre lasted thirteen years. The bald heads and raw shoulders likely come from carrying baskets of earth and stone to build a causeway toward the island city, under helmets, for years. And at the end, no plunder.

So here is the hard question. Back in chapter twenty-six, Ezekiel prophesied that Nebuchadnezzar would come against Tyre and that they would plunder her riches. Does verse eighteen quietly admit that the earlier prophecy failed? Many critics say so.

The older commentators answer with one voice, and I think they are right. Keil says the verse does not mean Nebuchadnezzar failed to take the city. It means he did not get the reward he expected for all that labor. Ellicott agrees: the point is the plunder, not the conquest. Henry and Poole explain why. Tyre had thirteen years of warning and a fleet of ships. They sent their wealth away by sea. Poole thinks the city finally surrendered on terms, which would leave little to loot. Gill reports that Jerome said the same thing about the Tyrians loading their gold onto ships. And remember what we saw last time. In chapter twenty-six, the prophecy itself shifts from he, Nebuchadnezzar, to they, the many nations who would come in waves. Nebuchadnezzar was never promised the whole job.

Here is what I want you to notice. A prophet inventing a fulfillment would never publish this verse. Ezekiel puts in writing that the most powerful king on earth worked thirteen years for nothing. And then God answers the problem himself.

Verses nineteen and twenty. I will give the land of Egypt to Nebuchadnezzar. It shall be the wages for his army. I have given him the land of Egypt as his payment for which he labored, because they worked for me, declares the Lord God.

They worked for me. Nebuchadnezzar had never heard of Ezekiel. He was not trying to obey the God of Israel. Gill puts it neatly: they served God not by their intention but by the outcome. Carson is careful here. He warns that no one should imagine these nations were consciously obeying the Lord, and he points to Isaiah ten, where Assyria is the rod in God's hand without knowing it. Then he says, But the Lord is no one's debtor, and these are the arrangements that Almighty God is making.

The Westminster Confession says that God's providence reaches even to the sins of men, and not merely by permitting them, but ordering and governing them to his own holy ends, while the sinfulness belongs only to the creature. That is chapter five, and Ezekiel twenty-nine is a living example. Babylon's greed is Babylon's own. And God pays the wages anyway.

Did it happen? A broken Babylonian tablet in the British Museum records that in Nebuchadnezzar's thirty-seventh year, 568 B.C., he marched against Egypt to do battle. Just three years after this oracle. The tablet is damaged and we do not know how far he went. But the march is there, in Babylon's own records.

Then verse twenty-one, and two threads come together. On that day I will cause a horn to spring up for the house of Israel, and I will open your lips among them. A horn is strength, the horn of a wild ox. Keil thinks the horn here is not one particular person, not Zerubbabel and not the Messiah himself, but the whole salvation the Messiah brings, and he points us to Psalm one thirty-two, I will make a horn to sprout for David. When Zechariah, the father of John the Baptist, sang that God has raised up a horn of salvation for us in the house of his servant David, he was singing in this same language. That is the one New Testament move I want to make today, because the words are the same and the promise is the same.

And notice, I will open your lips. Back in chapter twenty-four, God told Ezekiel that on the day the fugitive arrived, his mouth would be opened. That happens in chapter thirty-three. We have been tracking that silence for weeks. Here, years after that silence ended, God speaks of Ezekiel's open mouth again. The prophet who was made silent will be the prophet whose words are proved true.

Chapter thirty, verses one through nineteen, is a lament for the day of the Lord on Egypt. Wail, Alas for the day! A day of clouds. Then a sweep of the map. Memphis, the old capital with its temples. Zoan, which the Greeks called Tanis. Thebes, the great city of the south, where the ruins of Karnak and Luxor still stand. Pelusium, the fortress at the border, which Ellicott calls the key of Egypt. Ezekiel names the cities the way a man calls the roll of the dead.

Notice verse eighteen. Tehaphnehes, Tahpanhes. That was the border fortress where the remnant of Judah fled after Jerusalem fell, dragging Jeremiah with them. And there, in Jeremiah forty-three, Jeremiah buried stones at the entrance to Pharaoh's palace and said that Nebuchadnezzar would set his throne on them. Two prophets, hundreds of miles apart, one in Egypt and one in Babylon, naming the same town.

Finally, verses twenty through twenty-six. This oracle is dated the eleventh year, the first month, the seventh day. April of 587 B.C., three months after chapter twenty-nine opened. Verse twenty-one. I have broken the arm of Pharaoh king of Egypt, and behold, it has not been bound up.

Which broken arm? Ellicott and Henry think of Carchemish in 605 B.C., where Babylon crushed Egypt's army. But Keil notices the timing. Between January and April of 587, Hophra's relief army had come up toward Jerusalem and been turned back. Jeremiah thirty-seven says it plainly: Pharaoh's army that came to help you is about to return to Egypt. I think Keil has it. The arm Judah was counting on had just been broken in front of them, and God says no doctor will set it. Then verse twenty-two: I will break his arms, both the strong arm and the one that was broken. And verse twenty-four: I will strengthen the arms of the king of Babylon and put my sword in his hand.

My sword. Not Babylon's. Mine.

So what do we do with two chapters of judgment on an ancient kingdom? Go back to the staff of reed. Judah's sin was not that Egypt was strong. It was that Judah leaned on Egypt instead of on God, and kept leaning even after it had been pierced. And God's mercy toward Judah looked like breaking the staff.

You have something like that. A relationship, a salary, a reputation, a plan B that you reach for every time things get hard, before you ever pray. It may not be evil. Egypt was not always evil to Israel. But if you lean your full weight on it, you will learn what Judah learned. And if God takes it out of your hand, that may be the kindest thing he does for you this year.

Job chapter ten.

In chapter nine Job spoke about God. He wished there were an arbiter who could lay his hand on them both. Now, in chapter ten, he turns and speaks to God directly. Henry lays it out in four movements. Complaint, verses one through seven. Comfort in his Maker, verses eight through thirteen. Complaint again, verses fourteen through seventeen. And a wish for death, eighteen through twenty-two.

Verse two. I will say to God, Do not condemn me; let me know why you contend against me. The word contend is riv, a lawsuit word. Job is still in the courtroom.

Is that a good prayer or a bad one? Henry thinks it is a good one, and he gives it a beautiful shape. Though I lie under the cross, let me not lie under the curse; though I smart by the rod of a Father, let me not be cut off by the sword of a Judge. That is a prayer you can pray in your own suffering. Lord, discipline me if you must, but do not condemn me.

Then verses eight through twelve, some of the most tender lines in the book. Your hands fashioned and made me. Remember that you have made me like clay. Did you not pour me out like milk and curdle me like cheese? You clothed me with skin and flesh, and knit me together with bones and sinews. Job is describing his own conception and growth in the womb, the same mystery David sings about in Psalm one thirty-nine. Keil makes a wise comment. Scripture is not giving a biology lesson. It traces the workings of nature back to their final cause, which is God's own hands.

And verse twelve. You have granted me life and steadfast love. That word is hesed, God's covenant kindness. Job knows he has received it.

Then comes the hardest verse in the chapter. Verse thirteen. Yet these things you hid in your heart; I know that this was your purpose.

What does Job think God was hiding? Keil, the Cambridge Bible, and Ellicott all read it the same way. Job has started to suspect that all that early kindness was a veil over a darker purpose. As if God knit him together so carefully just so he would have something worth tormenting. Barnes reads it more gently, as Job simply confessing that all things are in God's counsel. I think Keil is right about what Job means. This is a man in agony thinking the worst.

But here is the irony that makes the book of Job what it is. Job is right that God has a hidden purpose. He is completely wrong about what it is. You and I have read chapters one and two. We have seen the heavenly court. Job has not. James, looking back on this whole book, tells us we have seen the purpose of the Lord, how the Lord is compassionate and merciful. The thing hidden in God's heart was not cruelty. It was a confidence in Job so deep that God put his own name on the line for him.

Gregory the Great, in his great work on Job, says God judges a man in this life in two ways. Either present suffering is the beginning of the torments to come, or present scourges do away with the torments to come. And then he gives us the line that tells you which is which. For chastisement delivers those alone from woe, whom it alters.

So the question is not whether you are suffering. The question is what the suffering is doing in you.

The chapter ends in the dark. Verse twenty-two. The land of gloom like thick darkness, like deep shadow without any order. That word for order, sedarim, appears only here in the Old Testament. Job imagines death as chaos with no shape at all. He does not yet see past it. That is where we leave him today, and Zophar is about to speak.

John chapter ten, verses one through ten.

Do not let the chapter break fool you. Augustine told his congregation that this lesson is interwoven with the last one. Jesus is still talking to the Pharisees who said, We see. And the man born blind has just been cast out.

Truly, truly, I say to you, he who does not enter the sheepfold by the door but climbs in by another way, that man is a thief and a robber.

Picture the scene. At night several village flocks were brought into one walled fold, with a gatekeeper at the entrance. In the morning each shepherd came to the gate, the gatekeeper let him in, and he called out his own sheep. They knew his voice and came. Then he went out in front, and they followed.

So who are the thieves? R. C. Sproul is blunt about it. Some say Jesus means false messiahs or the devil. Sproul says it is far more pointed than that. Jesus is calling those who cast out the man born blind, who was a sheep in the flock of God, thieves and robbers.

And now verse three lands. He calls his own sheep by name and leads them out. The Pharisees threw the man out of the synagogue. Jesus leads his sheep out. Same result, a man standing outside, but two completely different events. Chrysostom saw it sixteen centuries ago. He thought Jesus was alluding to the blind man himself, the one he had called and led out from among his opponents, and who knew his voice.

Here is an interesting tension in the voices. What is the door in verse one? Chrysostom says the Scriptures. The thief does not come in according to the Scriptures. Augustine and Calvin say the door is Christ from the beginning. Then in verse seven Jesus shifts the picture and says, I am the door. He is the shepherd and the door at once. Chrysostom handles it well. When He brings us to the Father, He calls Himself a Door, when He takes care of us, a Shepherd.

Another small disagreement. What does it mean that he calls them by name? Augustine connects it to names written in the book of life. Calvin is cautious. He doubts that it means the shepherd's personal knowledge of each sheep, and thinks it is about teacher and disciple joined by the same Spirit. Here I will gently disagree with Calvin. Isaiah forty-three says, I have called you by name, you are mine. The man born blind did not have a name in John nine. Jesus knows it anyway.

Verse eight. All who came before me are thieves and robbers. Does that include Moses and the prophets? No. Calvin insists the phrase has nothing to do with them, because their whole aim was to build Christ's kingdom. Augustine says they came with him, not apart from him.

Then verse nine. I am the door. If anyone enters by me, he will be saved and will go in and out and find pasture. Augustine has a wonderful line. For Christ the Lord is a low gateway: he who enters by this gateway must humble himself, that he may be able to enter with head unharmed. The Pharisees, standing tall and saying We see, could not get through. The blind beggar on his knees went straight in.

Spurgeon preached on this verse in 1879. He pointed out how many ways people deal with a door without going through it. Some admire it. Some knock and never enter. And some, he says, sit on the step. Doors were not made for us to sit on the doorstep. And a little later, A door is made for us to pass through it, not for us to sit down in front of it.

Have you been sitting on the step? You have heard it, you like it, you defend it. Have you gone in?

Verse ten. The thief comes only to steal and kill and destroy. I came that they may have life and have it abundantly. The word for life is zoe, life itself, the life of God. Keep that word. We will need it in a minute.

First John chapter two, verses twelve through seventeen.

Last time John told us we have an advocate with the Father. Now before he gives a hard command, he stops and gives assurance. Six short lines. I am writing to you, little children, because your sins are forgiven. Fathers, because you know him who is from the beginning. Young men, because you have overcome the evil one.

Is John addressing three age groups? Henry thinks so. New believers, men in their strength, and seasoned old saints. Calvin points out that the first word, little children, is the same word John uses for the whole church in verse one, so it covers everyone, and the fathers and young men are then special words within the family. Either way, notice the order. Forgiveness first. Knowing the Father first. Then the command.

Verse fifteen. Do not love the world or the things in the world. If anyone loves the world, the love of the Father is not in him.

Why put assurance right before this? Calvin has a picture I love. Until the heart is emptied of the love of the world, he says, you can repeat the teaching a hundred times with no effect. It would be like pouring water on a ball; you can gather, no, not a drop, because there is no empty place to retain water.

But does that mean the world God made is bad? Augustine answers with a wedding. As if a bridegroom should make a ring for his bride, and she having received the ring, should love it more than she loves the bridegroom who made the ring for her: would not her soul be found guilty of adultery in the very gift of the bridegroom? The ring is good. It was given so that the giver would be loved. The sin is loving the ring and not wanting to see his face.

Then verse sixteen names what John means by the world. The desires of the flesh, the desires of the eyes, and the pride of life. That last phrase in Greek is alazoneia tou biou. The word for life there is not zoe. It is bios, the stuff you live on, your living, your goods. The ESV footnote reads, pride in possessions. John Piper preached on this text and pointed out that the first two are cravings for what we do not have, and the third is pride in what we already have. Then he sums it up. The world is driven by these two things: passion for pleasure and pride in possessions.

Verse seventeen. The world is passing away along with its desires, but whoever does the will of God abides forever. The world is not just wrong. It is temporary. You cannot hold on to it, because it is already leaving.

Now let me give you the connections, because two of them are real.

The first is Pharaoh. My Nile is my own; I made it for myself. That is alazoneia tou biou, the pride of life, in its purest form. A man looking at what God gave him, the river his whole life depended on, and boasting as if he owned it and made it. John says that pride is not from the Father. Ezekiel shows what God does with it. Hooks in the jaws.

The second is in John's own words. In John ten, verse ten, Jesus says, I came that they may have life, zoe, abundantly. In First John two, verse sixteen, the world offers pride in bios, in your living, your stuff. The same apostle wrote both. One kind of life is given by the Shepherd, and you receive it by walking through a low door. The other kind is the life you can hold in your hands and boast about, and it is passing away.

So carry this question through your day. What are you leaning your weight on? If it is a staff of reed, you will find out sooner or later. Better to find out now, from a shepherd who calls you by name.
