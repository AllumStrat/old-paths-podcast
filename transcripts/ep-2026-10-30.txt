Son of man, dig in the wall.

A priest in Babylon is lifted by a lock of his hair and set down, in a vision, at the temple in Jerusalem. He finds a hole in a wall. God tells him to dig. He digs, and there is a door. Behind the door is a room, and on every wall, carved all the way around, are creeping things and loathsome beasts. Seventy elders of Israel stand in front of the pictures, each with a censer, and the smoke rises. And what are they saying to each other in that room? The Lord does not see us.

So here is my question for you this morning. If God dug through the wall of the room you think he cannot see, what would he find on the walls?

This is Old Paths. Today is day two of the eleventh month in the reading plan, day two hundred fifty-two of three hundred. The readings are Ezekiel chapters four through eight, Job chapter two, John chapter seven, verses forty-five through fifty-three, and Second Peter chapter one, verses twelve through twenty-one.

Last time we stood with Ezekiel by the Chebar canal, watched him eat the scroll, and heard God say, I will make your tongue cling to the roof of your mouth. How does a mute prophet preach? He preaches with a brick, with his own body, with a razor, and with his dinner. And then, in chapter eight, the glory we saw arrive in Babylon is shown standing in a temple so full of idols that, as God says, they are driving me far from my sanctuary. Tomorrow the glory starts to leave.

In Job, the Satan is back from going to and fro, and this time Job's wife speaks. In John, the officers sent to arrest Jesus come back. And in Second Peter, verse twelve, Peter says his readers are established in the truth. The word is from sterizo, the word Jesus used when he told Peter, when you have turned again, strengthen your brothers. We met it in First Peter five, verse ten. The man who needed strengthening now strengthens.

Ezekiel chapters four through eight.

First, where we are. Chapter one was dated to the summer of 593 B.C. Chapter eight, verse one, is dated the sixth year, the sixth month, the fifth day, so late summer of 592. Zedekiah is still on the throne in Jerusalem. The city is still standing, and the people left in it think they are the good figs. And the exiles in Babylon are listening to prophets who promise they will be home soon.

Calvin says the people in Jerusalem were more obstinate because God had spared them. Into that, God sends a man who has been told, at the end of chapter three, to shut himself in his house, that cords will be put on him, and that he will be mute except when God opens his mouth. Matthew Henry says his silence had a voice.

Chapter four opens with a brick. Take a brick, lay it before you, and engrave on it a city, even Jerusalem. Then build siege works around it, a ramp, camps, battering rams, and set an iron griddle between yourself and the city like a wall. Ligonier notes that city plans engraved on bricks have been found in the ancient Near East. Their beloved city, scratched into Babylonian clay. Henry points out the insult in it. God had once said he had engraved Zion on the palms of his hands. Now a cheap brick is good enough.

Calvin is honest about how it looked. "This had been a childish spectacle, unless God had commanded the Prophet to act so." Without God's word it is a boy's game. What turns play into prophecy is the word of God attached to it. Verse three: this is a sign for the house of Israel.

Then verses four through eight, the strangest command in the book. Lie on your left side, and place the punishment of the house of Israel upon it. Three hundred ninety days. Then on your right side for the house of Judah, forty days, a day for each year. And I will place cords upon you, so that you cannot turn from one side to the other.

The Hebrew phrase is nasa avon, to bear iniquity, or to bear punishment. The word avon can mean both the guilt and the penalty that guilt brings. What do the numbers mean? Calvin and Henry count three hundred ninety years from Jeroboam's golden calves to the fall of Jerusalem, and forty for Judah's last generation of hardened sin, from about the time Jeremiah was called. Keil and Delitzsch notice that three hundred ninety and forty make four hundred thirty, the number of years Israel spent in bondage in Egypt. That is a chilling thought. A new Egypt.

Matthew Poole steps back from the arithmetic and asks what the posture itself says. "Thou shalt bear their iniquity; signifying that as the prophet in the sign, so God in very deed, had patiently borne with them." Four centuries of idolatry, and God had carried it. A prophet lying day after day on his side pictures how long God has waited.

Now the hard question. Did Ezekiel really do this? Four hundred thirty days, tied up on his side?

Calvin said no. He says plainly that Ezekiel did not lie three hundred ninety days on his side, except in a vision. He thought the whole thing happened in Ezekiel's mind and was afterward told to the people. Part of the reason is arithmetic. Keil counts about four hundred thirteen days between chapter one and chapter eight, and the sign needs at least four hundred thirty-seven.

Henry takes the other side. Ezekiel really did it, but not around the clock. He lay there every day at a set time, when people came to the house.

I come down closer to Henry. Verse twelve says he is to bake his bread in their sight. Verse three says it is a sign for the house of Israel. A sign nobody sees is not much of a sign. I think the exiles walked past that house for more than a year and saw a man lying on his side in front of a brick. As for the arithmetic, Poole reports the view that the forty days overlap the end of the three hundred ninety, and then it fits.

Is this Ezekiel bearing sin the way Christ bore sin? Calvin flatly says no. No atonement is being described here, and Keil agrees. Ezekiel does not take anything away. He shows the people the weight they are carrying. And here is the one place I will step into the New Testament this morning, because the phrase demands it. Isaiah fifty-three, verse twelve, uses the same verb of the Servant: he bore the sin of many. Peter takes it up: he himself bore our sins in his body on the tree. Ezekiel could only lie under the burden and show it. Christ took it away.

Then the food. Six kinds of grain and beans in one pot, because in a siege you use whatever is left. Eight ounces a day, and a little over a pint of water. And then verse twelve: bake it on human dung, in their sight.

Here, for the first time, Ezekiel argues with God. Verse fourteen. Ah, Lord God! Behold, I have never defiled myself. From my youth up till now I have never eaten what died of itself or was torn by beasts. He is a priest, and God listens. I assign to you cow's dung instead. Calvin notes that God relaxed the means but not his decree for the people. Notice that God's servant can say, Ah, Lord God, and be heard. He does not refuse the command. He pleads within it.

Chapter five. Take a sharp sword and use it as a barber's razor on your head and your beard. A priest was forbidden to shave his head like the mourners of the nations. Now he must. Weigh the hair in a balance and divide it into thirds. A third burned in the city, a third struck with the sword around it, a third scattered to the wind. Then verse three. Take a small number and bind them in the skirts of your robe. Poole says that is the remnant God reserves. But read on. Verse four: and of these again you shall take some and cast them into the fire. Even the remnant is sifted.

Then verse five. This is Jerusalem. I have set her in the center of the nations. And verse six: she has rebelled more than the nations. Privilege raises the stakes. And all through these chapters you hear the refrain that runs through the whole book, more than sixty times. They shall know that I am the Lord.

Chapter six turns to the mountains of Israel, the high places where the people worshiped on every hill and under every green tree. Their bones will be scattered around their own altars. And then, verse eight, the first ray of hope in the book. Yet I will leave some of you alive. And verse nine, which stopped me cold. Those of you who escape will remember me among the nations, how I have been broken over their whoring heart.

The Hebrew is nishbarti, from shabar, to break. I have been broken. Henry hears in it the grief of a husband. God is "not only angry at it, but grieved, as a husband is at the lewdness of a wife whom he dearly loved, grieved to such a degree that he is broken with it; it breaks his heart to think that he should be so disingenuously dealt with". Calvin hears God worn down by their hardness, though he allows that it may mean God is ready to pardon. Either way, what brings the remnant to repentance in exile is not only that their country was broken. It is that they remember they broke God's heart. And verse nine ends, they will be loathsome in their own sight.

Chapter seven is a different kind of writing. It is short, hammering lines. An end! The end has come. The word is qets, and it echoes Amos, who saw a basket of summer fruit, qayits, and heard, the end has come upon my people. Behold, it comes. The day is near.

Then verse ten. The rod has blossomed; pride has budded. In Numbers seventeen, Aaron's rod budded to show whom God had chosen. Here a rod blossoms for beating. Calvin says the rod is Nebuchadnezzar, growing for years in Babylon while Judah thought it had made peace with God. But where did the rod come from? "The seed therefore of this staff was the pride of the Israelites." The punishment grew out of their own sin, like fruit from a seed.

Then chapter eight. Fourteen months after the call, the elders of Judah are sitting in Ezekiel's house. The hand of the Lord falls on him. A figure of fire, like the one on the throne in chapter one, takes him by a lock of his hair, and he is brought in visions of God to Jerusalem. The glory of the God of Israel is there, verse four. But it will not stay long.

He is shown four scenes, and each time God says, you will see still greater abominations.

First, at the north gate, the image of jealousy. Most likely the kind of Asherah idol Manasseh set up, which Josiah threw out and someone put back. God says these things are driving me far from my sanctuary.

Second, the hole in the wall, the room of pictures, and the seventy elders with their censers. And one of them is named. Jaazaniah the son of Shaphan. Shaphan was King Josiah's secretary, who read the rediscovered book of the law to the king. His sons were Jeremiah's protectors. Ahikam son of Shaphan saved Jeremiah from being put to death. Gemariah son of Shaphan lent his chamber in the temple for Baruch to read the scroll. Elasah son of Shaphan carried Jeremiah's letter to these very exiles. Gedaliah, his grandson, was the governor who took Jeremiah in. And here, in the dark, with a censer in his hand, is another son of Shaphan. Godly families do not guarantee godly sons.

Verse twelve. Have you seen what the elders are doing in the dark, each in his room of pictures? The word is maskit, an image, something carved, and Calvin notes it can also mean the imagination. For they say, The Lord does not see us, the Lord has forsaken the land.

Calvin says that line is not a theory. It is the complaint of men under pressure who felt no help from God. That is how it usually happens. Nobody wakes up wanting to worship a crawling thing on a wall. A man decides that God has stopped paying attention, and then he looks for something that will.

Third scene. At the north gate, women weeping for Tammuz. Tammuz was the Mesopotamian god of the dying and returning season. The fourth month of the year was named for him, and if you remember, Ezekiel's first vision came in the fourth month. The worship of Tammuz was stubborn. Jerome, writing from Bethlehem around the year 395, begins, "Even my own Bethlehem," and says that it "was overshadowed by a grove of Tammuz," and then he goes on, "that is of Adonis; and in the very cave where the infant Christ had uttered His earliest cry lamentation was made for the paramour of Venus." At the birthplace of the one who truly died and rose, they mourned a dying god of the harvest.

Fourth scene, the worst, in the inner court, between the porch and the altar, where only priests should stand. About twenty-five men, with their backs to the temple of the Lord, and their faces toward the east, worshiping the sun. To face the sunrise in that court, you had to turn your back on the Holy of Holies. It is apostasy you can see.

And here is something you will want for later. Centuries afterward, at the Feast of Booths, the priests had a ceremony at cockcrow. They walked to the east gate of the temple, and then they turned around to face west, toward the sanctuary, and recited this verse. The Mishnah, in the tractate Sukkah, records what they said after it: "and we, our eyes are to God." Every year at Booths, Israel reversed Ezekiel eight with their feet. Hold onto that.

The chapter ends with a phrase nobody fully understands, they put the branch to their nose, and I will leave it there. What is clear is verse eighteen. Though they cry in my ears with a loud voice, I will not hear them. They said, the Lord does not see us. God answers, I see everything, and now I will not hear.

So here is one thing to take from Ezekiel today. The elders' room was not out in the open. You had to dig to find it. Most of us have a room like that. A place in the mind where we keep pictures we would never show our wives or our elders, and where we tell ourselves, God does not see, or God has stopped caring. Henry asks a good question about this chapter: if they had chambers of imagery, should we not have chambers of devotion? The answer to a hidden room is not to pretend you do not have one. It is to let God dig. Bring the room into the light in confession, and then build another room, a regular, private place where you meet with the God who does see.

Job chapter two.

The second chapter opens almost word for word like the first. The sons of God present themselves, Satan comes among them, and the Lord asks the same question: Have you considered my servant Job? But now there is an addition. He still holds fast his integrity, although you incited me against him to destroy him without reason.

Integrity is tummah, wholeness. And without reason is hinnam, the very word Satan used in chapter one. Does Job fear God for nothing? Now God turns it around. You moved me against him for nothing. Gill says the word can mean in vain, and Poole renders it, to destroy him in vain, or without effect, or to no purpose. Satan's theory has failed.

Can God really be incited? Gregory the Great answers simply. Because we ourselves never strike unless something moves us, the Bible speaks of God's stroke as being moved. It is language fitted to us. God is not pushed around by the devil. The devil is on a leash, and here the leash is let out a little. Only spare his life.

Satan's new theory is skin for skin: a man will give up anything to save his own hide. Touch his bone and his flesh. So Job is struck with loathsome sores from the sole of his foot to the crown of his head. That exact phrase appears in Deuteronomy twenty-eight among the curses for breaking the covenant. Job looks cursed. He is not. That gap between how a man looks and what he is will drive the whole book.

He sits in the ashes, probably the town's ash heap, and scrapes himself with a piece of broken pottery. Gregory says he wiped away mud with mud.

Then his wife. Do you still hold fast your integrity? Curse God and die. Notice what she does. The first half is God's line to Satan, he still holds fast his integrity. The second half is Satan's prediction, he will curse you. She has God's words in her mouth and Satan's conclusion. Gregory the Great is hard on her, but he saw something true about the strategy. "Mark how in trying him, he took away every thing, and again in trying him, left him his wife, and shewed craftiness in stripping him of every thing, but infinitely greater cunning, in keeping the woman as his abettor". The sharpest temptations often come through the people closest to us.

Spurgeon was gentler. However she meant it, he said, she spoke like a foolish woman just when her husband needed wise comfort. And remember, she had lost ten children too.

Job's answer is careful. You speak as one of the foolish women would speak. Not, you are a fool, but, you are talking like one. The word is nabal, the name of Abigail's husband. In Hebrew, a fool is not someone with a slow mind. It is someone who lives as if God did not matter. Then the great line. Shall we receive good from God, and shall we not receive evil? Henry says, "though good and evil are contraries, yet they do not come from contrary causes, but both from the hand of God". The word receive matters. Job does not say endure. He says receive, the way you take what is handed to you by someone you trust.

In all this Job did not sin with his lips. Some have wondered why the narrator says lips and not heart. One old note puts it well. There might be risings of corruption in his heart, but grace had the upper hand. Wait for chapter three tomorrow, and you will see the storm that was building inside.

Then three friends come. Eliphaz the Temanite, from Teman in Edom, a place so famous for wise men that Jeremiah asks, is wisdom no more in Teman? Bildad the Shuhite, probably from Shuah, one of Abraham's sons by Keturah. They see him from a distance and do not recognize him. They weep, tear their robes, throw dust toward heaven, and sit with him on the ground seven days and seven nights, and no one spoke a word to him, for they saw that his suffering was very great.

Here is the wisdom for today, and it is very practical. That week of silence was the best thing these friends ever did for Job. Their trouble started when they opened their mouths. When someone you love is sitting in the ashes, go. Sit down. Do not explain it. You may not need to say anything for a long time.

John chapter seven, verses forty-five through fifty-three.

We are still on the last great day of the Feast of Booths. Back in verse thirty-two, the chief priests and Pharisees sent officers to arrest Jesus. These were the temple police. Now, after hearing him cry out, if anyone thirsts, they come back with empty hands. Why did you not bring him? And they give one of the great testimonies in the Gospels. No one ever spoke like this man!

Chrysostom loved the irony. The scholars had been with Jesus, seen his miracles, read the Scriptures, and gained nothing, "while the officers, who could not claim one of these privileges, were subdued by one single sermon, and they who had gone forth to bind Him, came back bound themselves by wonder." Spurgeon, preaching on this verse in 1870, said the same thing his own way. The constables were waiting for their chance, but "they could not take him, for he had fairly taken them".

But admiring him is not the same as following him. These men went back to their posts. Calvin, on this verse, says the word of Christ can overwhelm even those who resist it, and so we should take care to be softened by it rather than broken by it.

Now watch how the leaders respond. Have you also been deceived? Have any of the authorities or the Pharisees believed in him? But this crowd that does not know the law is accursed. Calvin calls it pride: men who thought there was no holiness outside their own rank. And Chrysostom turns it back on them. It is you who are accursed, he says, who keep not the law.

At which point the law is quoted to them by one of their own. Nicodemus, who had gone to him before, and who was one of them. Does our law judge a man without first giving him a hearing? It is a simple point of procedure from Deuteronomy. And they had just said, none of the rulers has believed in him, while a ruler was standing in the room.

What should we make of Nicodemus here? The voices disagree. Calvin is severe. He calls him a neutral man, who would not really defend the truth and yet could not stand to see it crushed. He only asked for a fair trial, Calvin says, which you would ask for a thief. Augustine is kinder. Nicodemus came by night, he says, not as an unbeliever but timid, because he wanted to be enlightened and was afraid to be known. I think both are right. It was a small, nervous word. But Calvin himself adds the hope. The seed was hidden, and later, he says, the faith of Nicodemus acquired new and sudden vigor from the death of Christ. In chapter nineteen, when the bold disciples have fled, Nicodemus carries seventy-five pounds of spices to the tomb. Do not despise the small word. Sometimes it is the first green shoot of a great tree.

Then the sneer. Are you from Galilee too? Search and see that no prophet arises from Galilee. They tell a scholar to search the Scriptures, and they are wrong on the facts. Jonah the son of Amittai came from Gath-hepher, a town of Zebulun, in Galilee, a few miles from Nazareth. And Isaiah nine had promised that Galilee of the nations would see a great light. Augustine has the last word: "Search the Scriptures, and see that out of Galilee arises no prophet. Yet the Lord of the prophets arose thence." Keep that great light in mind. Two verses later, in chapter eight, Jesus says, I am the light of the world.

Verse fifty-three says, they went each to his own house. Your Bible marks that verse and the story that follows. The earliest manuscripts do not include them. That is tomorrow's question, and we will take it seriously.

Second Peter chapter one, verses twelve through twenty-one.

Peter has told us to make our calling and election sure. Now he explains why he keeps repeating himself. Verse twelve. I intend always to remind you of these things, though you know them and are established in the truth. Then verse thirteen: as long as I am in this body, and the word is tent. Verse fifteen: after my departure, and the word is exodos.

On the mountain of the Transfiguration, Luke says, Moses and Elijah spoke with Jesus about his departure, his exodos. And Peter blurted out, let us make three tents. Now an old man, he calls his own body a tent and his own death an exodus. You fold up a tent. You go out of Egypt.

Calvin says a man's words just before he leaves this life carry the force of a will. Peter is writing his.

Then verse sixteen. We did not follow cleverly devised myths when we made known to you the power and coming of our Lord Jesus Christ, but we were eyewitnesses of his majesty. The pagan world was full of myths. Peter says, this is not that. John Piper, preaching this text in 1982, put it in one sentence. "The difference between mythical speculations and Christian faith is history." Peter was there. He heard the voice, This is my beloved Son. He calls it the holy mountain, because God had been there.

Then the hard question, verse nineteen. And we have the prophetic word more fully confirmed. The King James says, a more sure word of prophecy. More sure than what? Than the voice from heaven? How can anything be more certain than that?

Calvin says Peter is speaking to Jewish believers who had revered the prophets from childhood, so to them the prophets carried an authority no one disputed. The Reformation Study Bible, which R.C. Sproul edited, takes it more strongly: the prophetic word of Scripture is a more solid proof than even the experience of the Transfiguration. Piper reads it the way our English Standard Version does: the prophecies are more sure now, because the mountain confirmed them with a preview of their fulfillment.

I take that reading. The mountain confirmed the prophets. But the Reformation Study Bible's point is the one you and I need, because we were not on the mountain. Peter is about to die. Soon there will be no eyewitnesses left. What will the church have? The prophetic word, the written Scripture. And Peter says, that is enough.

You will do well to pay attention to it as to a lamp shining in a dark place. The word for dark is auchmeros, and Ellicott says its usual meaning is dry. A squalid place, with no water and no light. That is the world, and on some days it is your own heart. Until the day dawns and the morning star rises in your hearts. Calvin thinks the day is the last day, when we see face to face. Augustine said that in that day the Scriptures, our lamps in the night of this world, will no longer be needed. Until then, you walk by the lamp.

The Puritan William Bridge, who sat in the Westminster Assembly, preached on this verse, and he made this observation. "In other knowledges, the more ye know, the less ye admire; but in Scripture knowledge, the more light ye have, and the more ye know, the more you will lift up your hands and admire, at your own ignorance and God's grace."

Then verse twenty. No prophecy of Scripture comes from someone's own interpretation. Rome took that to mean ordinary Christians may not interpret the Bible. Calvin and the Reformation Study Bible both say Peter is talking about where prophecy came from, not who may read it. For no prophecy was ever produced by the will of man, but men spoke from God as they were carried along by the Holy Spirit.

Carried along is pheromenoi, and it is the same verb Peter used two verses earlier, when the voice was borne from the Majestic Glory. The voice was carried down to the mountain. The prophets were carried along by the Spirit. Luke uses the word in Acts twenty-seven for a ship driven by the wind. Albert Barnes says the prophets were no more self-moved than a ship at sea impelled by the wind. The sailors still worked, but the wind decided where the ship went.

That is why our Confession, in chapter one, says that the supreme judge of every controversy "can be no other but the Holy Spirit speaking in the Scripture." Not councils, not the opinions of ancient writers, not private spirits. The Spirit who carried the prophets still speaks through what they wrote.

Two connections today, and both are in the text.

The first is darkness. In Ezekiel eight, the elders went into a dark room, painted its walls, and said, the Lord does not see us. In Second Peter one, the world is a dark and squalid place, and God has put a lamp in it. You can sit in the dark room and tell yourself God does not see, or you can pick up the lamp. The lamp does not only show you the road. As Bridge put it, it lets you see your own dirty face. That is a mercy.

The second is the Feast of Booths. Ezekiel saw twenty-five men turn their backs on the temple and face the rising sun. Centuries later, at Booths, the priests walked to that same east gate every year, turned around, and said, our eyes are to God. At that very feast, in the temple courts, officers sent to arrest a man from Galilee came back saying, no one ever spoke like this man. The sanctuary they turned toward had someone greater than the sanctuary standing in it. Some turned toward him. The leaders turned their backs.

So here is your question to carry through the day. When you go into the room where you think no one sees, which way are you facing?
