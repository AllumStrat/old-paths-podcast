# The Scroll and the Cistern

## John 4:43-54

Two days after the Samaritan villagers have told the woman at the well that they know for themselves who Jesus is, he crosses into Galilee. John gives a puzzling reason for the move. Watch how he explains it, because the verse seems to say that Jesus left Samaria *because* a prophet is not honored at home, and then immediately says the Galileans welcomed him. Hold both facts together as you read.

{{passage: John 4:43-45}}

The welcome is real, but it is thin. These Galileans had been in Jerusalem for the feast and saw the signs there, so they are receiving a miracle worker, not yet a Lord. John has been pressing this point since chapter 2, where many believed in his name because of the signs, yet Jesus did not entrust himself to them. Samaritans believed on the strength of a word. Galileans are about to be tested on exactly that.

Notice also where he goes: Cana, the village where he turned the water into wine. John counts this as the second sign, and he wants you to see the pair as bookends around a section about people responding to Jesus.

The man who walks twenty miles uphill from Capernaum is a royal official. The word could mean a soldier or a court servant of Herod Antipas. Either way he is a man used to giving orders, and here he can only beg. Jesus' first answer sounds harsh, a rebuke about needing signs and wonders to believe. The *you* is plural. He is speaking to the whole crowd, and the official is standing in it.

The official does not argue. He repeats the plea, and the plea is one of the most human lines in the Gospel: come down before my child dies. He thinks Jesus has to be present, in the room, hands on the boy. Jesus gives him less than he asked and more than he knew to ask for. He gives him a word, and the man *believes the word* and starts home. That is the turn of the whole passage. The faith John wants is not faith in a sign you can see, but faith in what Jesus says before you see anything.

The servants meet him on the road the next day, and the hour matches. The household believes. John closes by numbering the sign, and the number matters: this is the second sign in Galilee, after he came from Judea.

{{passage: John 4:46-54}}

## 1 Peter 1:10-16

Peter has just told his scattered readers that their salvation is guarded for them and that their faith is being refined by trials. Now he tells them how old this salvation is. It is not a new religion invented by Galilean fishermen. The prophets were looking for it.

{{passage: 1 Peter 1:10-12}}

Three groups are straining toward the same thing. The prophets searched and inquired. They knew they were writing about grace meant for someone else, and they were trying to work out the person and the time. The angels long to look. And the apostles preached it by the Holy Spirit sent from heaven. Peter's point for his struggling churches is that you are standing where prophets wanted to stand. Your ordinary Sunday is the thing the Old Testament was reaching for.

Watch the phrase about the sufferings of Christ and the subsequent glories. That order, suffering and then glory, is exactly the pattern Peter is about to ask his readers to live in.

Peter's *therefore* turns the doctrine into marching orders. The picture behind the call to prepare your minds for action is a man tucking up his long robe so he can run or work. Gird the mind. Be sober. Set your hope fully, and notice the hope is aimed at a grace that will be *brought* to you at the revelation of Jesus Christ. The hope rests on a delivery already on its way.

Then comes holiness, and Peter quotes the Old Testament himself. The call to be holy because God is holy comes from Leviticus, where it was said to a camp of freed slaves. Peter says it again to a scattered church of freed slaves of another kind. The pattern did not change. God redeems a people and then tells them whose they are.

{{passage: 1 Peter 1:13-16}}

## Ecclesiastes 11

The Preacher has spent ten chapters showing that life under the sun cannot be controlled. Chapter 11 is where he tells you what to do with that fact. It is not a counsel of despair. It is a counsel to get up and work while admitting you cannot see how it will turn out.

{{passage: Ecclesiastes 11:1-6}}

The first verse is about commerce. Grain was shipped across water, and the return might take months, if the ship came back at all. So the advice is to send it out anyway, and to spread the risk, a share to seven, or even to eight. The second half of the paragraph adds a bleaker reminder: you cannot read the clouds or the wind, and you cannot understand how a child forms in the womb. Verse 4 draws the conclusion that a man who waits for perfect conditions never sows or reaps.

Look at where verse 5 lands. You do not know the work of God who makes everything. That is not an excuse for doing nothing. It is the reason to sow in the morning and keep your hand busy in the evening, because you cannot know which seed will take.

The mood shifts. Light is sweet, and the Preacher says to enjoy every year, but with the days of darkness in view. Then he speaks straight to the young man and tells him to rejoice. This is not permission to follow every impulse, because the same verse says that God will bring him into judgment for all of it. The word *vanity* returns at the end, and it carries its usual meaning of breath, something that vanishes. Youth is a vapor. So rejoice now, and remove vexation from your heart.

{{passage: Ecclesiastes 11:7-10}}

## Jeremiah 35-38

These four chapters are not in chronological order, and that is the first thing to see. Chapters 35 and 36 belong to the reign of Jehoiakim, around 605 and 604 BC. Chapters 37 and 38 jump forward to the last days of Zedekiah, about 588 to 586. Jeremiah is arranging them as a contrast: ordinary people who obey a human command, and kings who will not obey the word of God.

{{passage: Jeremiah 35:1-11}}

Jehoiakim's Judah is under pressure from Babylon, and refugees have poured into Jerusalem. Among them are the Rechabites, a clan descended from Jonadab son of Rechab, the man who stood with Jehu in 2 Kings 10. Their family rule, set about two hundred years earlier, forbade wine, houses, vineyards and fields. They were to live in tents. Jeremiah brings them into a chamber of the temple, sets wine before them and tells them to drink. It is a test, and the test is rigged to fail. They answer that their father commanded them not to drink, and they have kept it. They only came into the city for fear of the army.

{{passage: Jeremiah 35:12-19}}

Then comes the point of the whole scene. If these men have honored the command of a dead ancestor for two centuries, why will Judah not obey the living God who has risen early and sent his prophets again and again? The contrast is the sermon. The Rechabites are not praised for their rules about wine, which are never made binding on anyone else. They are praised for faithfulness, and the promise to them is that Jonadab will never lack a man to stand before the LORD.

{{passage: Jeremiah 36:1-10}}

Now the scene moves to the fourth year of Jehoiakim, the year Nebuchadnezzar won at Carchemish. Jeremiah is barred from the temple, so he dictates every word the LORD had spoken to him to his secretary Baruch, and Baruch writes it on a scroll. Baruch reads it aloud on a fast day, so the crowd will be at the temple. The hope in verse 3 is simple: perhaps they will hear and turn.

{{passage: Jeremiah 36:11-26}}

The reading passes up the chain of command, from Micaiah to the officials to the king. The officials are afraid, and they tell Baruch to go and hide with Jeremiah. The king is in his winter house in the ninth month with a fire in the brazier. As Jehudi reads three or four columns, Jehoiakim cuts them off with a knife and throws them in the fire, until the whole scroll is gone. Nobody in the room tears his clothes. Contrast this with his father Josiah in 2 Kings 22, who tore his robes when he heard the law.

{{passage: Jeremiah 36:27-32}}

You cannot burn the word of God. Jeremiah is told to take another scroll, and Baruch writes it again, and the text adds that many similar words were added to it. The king has destroyed paper and gained a longer book.

{{passage: Jeremiah 37:1-21}}

Zedekiah is on the throne, put there by Babylon, and he is a man who wants a prophet's blessing without a prophet's message. When the Babylonians lift the siege briefly because Pharaoh's army marches north, the princes think the danger has passed. Jeremiah says it has not. He is arrested on a charge of deserting to the Chaldeans as he tries to leave the city, beaten, and put in a dungeon. Zedekiah asks him in secret if there is any word from the LORD. There is: you shall be delivered into the hand of the king of Babylon. Jeremiah asks to be spared a return to the house of Jonathan, and the king moves him to the court of the guard with a ration of bread.

{{passage: Jeremiah 38:1-13}}

The officials hear what Jeremiah is telling the people, and they ask the king for his death on the grounds that he weakens the hands of the soldiers. Zedekiah answers that he is in their hands, and the king can do nothing against them. They let Jeremiah down by ropes into a cistern with no water but mud. Ebed-melech, an Ethiopian official in the palace, goes to the king to plead for him. Notice who it is. A foreigner and a eunuch has more courage than all of Judah's princes. He takes thirty men and old rags and cloths and pulls Jeremiah out.

The last scene is a private audience in the temple's third entrance. Zedekiah wants to know what will happen, and Jeremiah gives him a way out: surrender, and live. The king is afraid of the Jews who have already gone over to Babylon and of what they might do to him. So he asks Jeremiah to keep their conversation secret, and then he sends him back to the court of the guard. The chapter ends with Jeremiah still there on the day Jerusalem fell. It is a bleak ending, because Zedekiah had two thrones to choose between, the one in Jerusalem and the one he could not see.

{{passage: Jeremiah 38:14-28}}
